## Embedded true random number generators enabled  by hexagonal boron nitride memristors   

S. Pazos 1 , T. Zanotti 2 , W. Zheng 1 , Y. Shen 1 , K. Zhu 1 , Y. Yuan 1 , F.M. Puglisi 2 , J.B. Roldan 3 , M. Lanza 1,\*   1 Physical Science and Engineering Division, King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, Thuwal, Saudi Arabia,   2 Dipartimento di Ingegneria "Enzo Ferrari", Università di Modena e Reggio Emilia, Modena, Italy,       3 Departamento de Electrónica y Tecnología de Computadores, Facultad de Ciencias, Universidad de Granada, Spain,  Email :  mario.lanza@kaust.edu.sa

Abstract —   Internet-of-things (IoT) devices require cheap,  light, small and reliable true random number generators  (TRNG) to encrypt data before transmission. Finding reliable  randomness sources to seed the TRNG can be challenging in the  presence of trade-offs such as power consumption, stability, and  cost of the circuit implementation. We analyse the suitability of  the random telegraph noise (RTN) signals of memristors made  of two-dimensional (2D) layered hexagonal boron nitride (hBN). After thorough evaluation of the noise characteristics, the  h-BN memristor is connected to an analogue front-end and a  microcontroller to fabricate a functional TRNG. Instead of  using simulations as proof of potential functionality or bulky  laboratory instrumentation, we provide an embedded TRNG  which   is   scalable   to   the   complementary-metal-oxidesemiconductor (CMOS) integration level and experimentally  tested for randomness at the application level.

Keywords— memristor, 2D materials, hexagonal boron  nitride, true random number generator, data encryption.

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

Edge devices for the IoT generate and share information  from their environment at a rapidly increasing rate. These  transactions are susceptible of interception and, therefore,  virtually all embedded systems include a cryptographic core  to secure this process, generating encryption keys on-demand  and at a high rate. State-of-the-art TRNG circuits employ an  entropy source (thermal noise of resistors, jitter of ring  oscillators, metastability of flip-flops [1,2]) to generate strings  of unpredictable numbers. While these solutions provide high  randomness and high throughput   $^{(>1}$   Mbit/s), their power  consumption is large   $(>1\:\mathrm{\mW})$  ), and they are difficult to  integrate into low-cost, very low power embedded systems  [3]. The intrinsic cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device  variability in the electrical characteristics of memristors has  been proposed as such entropy source. However, this usually  involves cycling the devices (consuming energy), dealing with  endurance issues on the long term, and/or introducing  complex postprocessing to increase the throughput of the  TRNG system. RTN has been explored as alternative [4], but  the evasive nature of its presence is typically not discussed.  Moreover, some rely on simulations to proof circuit  functionality [4], require bulky laboratory equipment [5],  expensive/complex hardware [6-8], or fail to provide a clear,  stand-alone, experimental, full benchmark of TRNG [9,10].  We use 2D multilayer h-BN to fabricate memristors that  exhibit excellent RTN current signals, observed reliably by  electrically tailoring the device characteristics. We perform a  thorough analysis of the noise parameters in Au/h-BN/Au  memristors under multiple conductance states and extract the  optimum bias conditions and device limitations to generate  RTN signals that are suitable for the implementation of  TRNGs. The memristor is connected to an inexpensive,  components-off-the-shelf front-end, enabling a TRNG  embedded in a commercial low-cost microcontroller.

For the TRNG implementation (Fig. 2a), an Arduino Nano  (Fig. 2b, blue enclosed in Fig. 2a) generates the adequate bias  for the device using pulse width modulation and a low pass  filter   $(\mathrm{R}1=15~\mathrm{k}\Omega$  ,   $\mathrm{\bar{C}}\mathrm{\bar{1}}=10~\upmu\mathrm{F}$  ). C1 is integrated on a custom  transimpedance amplifier (TIA, Fig 2d, red enclosed in Fig.  2a), setting a low-noise DC bias. The output of the amplifier  is fed to a voltage limiter   $({\mathrm{R}}4={\mathrm{R}}5=10~{\mathrm{k}}\Omega$  ,  $\mathrm{D}1=1\mathrm{N}\bar{4}148)$  ),  to adapt the voltage of the TIA (-7.4 V to  $+7.4~\mathrm{V}$  ) to a single,  buffered 5V output, compatible with the microcontroller. The RTN signal (two  examples for two distinct conduction states of the memristor  are shown in Fig. 2c) is sampled at   $15\,\mathrm{Hz}$   to generate a random  seed for a 19-bit Non-Linear Feedback Register (NLFSR),  which runs in the microcontroller, providing up to 7.8 Mbps  throughput (or higher for a longer NLFSR). applications. For testing, a Python script runs in a PC and  manages the TRNG experiment (Fig. 3d). To perform low  frequency noise measurements and acquire RTN signals for  the TRNG, a self-biased low noise TIA was implemented  using components-off-the-shelf. The circuit is battery  powered and enclosed in a metal case connected to a probe  station. The TIA includes a configurable gain (2 to 101), ACcoupled voltage post-amplifier stage to decouple slow, deviceinherent DC drifts. For low frequency noise analysis,  amplified signals are acquired using a NI-PCI4462 acquisition  board at a sampling rate of 2000 points per second. Data series  were processed via MATLAB, computing weighted time-lag  plots   (w-TLP),   noise   spectral   density   ( S I )   and  capture/emission times   $(\tau_{\mathrm{c}},\tau_{\mathrm{e}})$  .

RTN signals of Figs. 2c and 4a are acquired by the same  analogue front-end (i.e., TIA in Fig. 2) used to feed the TRNG  implemented with the microcontroller. Although the signals  are very stable, a change on conduction state can take the  device out of its best operating condition for TRNG. To avoid  device failure, a current limitation is imposed (through the  TIA) to the device at a value   $\mathrm{(VDD-V_{\mathrm{bias}})/R_{F}},$  , with V DD  the  supply voltage of the amplifier, R F  the feedback resistor and   $\mathrm{V_{bias}}$   the applied voltage to the memristor. At a gain 100  MV/A, current limitation is \~65 nA. If a conductance state  change is produced, the Au/h-BN/Au memristor is held to its  volatile regime. The microcontroller detects this condition,  removes the bias voltage, and applies it again, bringing the  device back to the proper conductance state for TRNG  operation. During this reset process the NLFSR ensures  uninterrupted operation. ## IV.   CONCLUSIONS 

We analyse the suitability of Au/h-BN/Au memristors as  an entropy source for TRNG by thoroughly characterizing  noise signals under different conductance states. We identify  the optimum biasing conditions to obtain reliable RTN signals  that can be harvested through simple, low-cost electronics. We  connect the memristors to a commercial microcontroller and  build a functional, low-power standalone hardware solution  for TRNG. This solution is experimentally demonstrated with  components-off-the-shelf, without lab instrumentation in the  loop. We also fabricate the h-BN based TRNG using TSMC  180 nm CMOS technology. This is an important step forward  for low power, embedded TRNG circuits that are ideal for the  IoT, and for the integration of 2D materials with wellestablished electronics, exploiting the benefits of both to  achieve technology improvement.
