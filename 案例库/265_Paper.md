## Generation and Tracking of Optical Signals inside the  IC to Improve Device Security and Failure Analysis   

Elham Amini 1,2,\* , Norbert Herfurth 3 , Anne Beyreuther 3 , Jean-Pierre Seifert 1 , and Christian Boit 2,\*\*  1 Security in Telecommunications, Technische Universität Berlin, Berlin, 10587, Germany  2 Semiconductor Devices, Technische Universität Berlin, Berlin, 10587, Germany    3 Sensor and Actuator Systems, Technische Universität Berlin, Berlin, 10587, Germany    \* elham.amini@campus.tu-berlin.de, Tel: +49-30-835354237,  \*\* christian.boit@tu-berlin.de

Abstract-   Optical signal tracking techniques accessing the IC  through the chip backside have become the most successful  methods in hardware attacks. On the other hand, tracking the  optical signal can be employed to protect the IC against such  attacks. Furthermore, this technique is an advantageous method  for failure analysis. Tracking the optical signal reflected from, or  transmitted through a surface, reveals some information about  this surface that can be used to monitor the surface. Thus,  detection of the light reflected from the silicon chip back surface  discloses any harms and violations to the backside. For this  purpose, we need to generate light inside the IC, which is possible  by applying forward bias to a p-n junction. However, in most ICs,  silicon is the base material, and the light generated by silicon LED  is weak. Furthermore, this application may lead to degradation of  the silicon LED. Therefore, an externally produced a stronger  light source mounted onto the chip is desirable. This paper  considers possible ways to generate and track optical signals  inside the silicon to achieve a proper protection structure that  prevents attacks from being carried out through the silicon back  surface. We describe how an efficient LED can be integrated into  an IC to illuminate light in the demanded directions, as well as  which kind of LED is favorable for this target.

Keywords  –  Optical signal generation, Optical signal tracking,  IC security, Silicon LED, Non-silicon LED, Hardware security.  Optical and physical attacks .

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

Despite the many countermeasures protecting semiconductor  devices such as smart cards and microcontrollers against  hardware attacks, these devices are not yet safe enough because  they are vulnerable to attacks that target the ICs through the  chip backside (silicon side). The available or proposed  countermeasures such as sensors, shields, and attack detectors  only prevent assaults through the chip frontside [1,2].  Therefore, to have a secure system, both frontside and backside  must be protected. So far, no countermeasure is established on  the chip backside because the backside is not controllable via  IC electronics on the frontside as there is no electrical  connection between the frontside and the backside of an IC. The  only exception is through-silicon via (TSV) which is expensive,  and this is not desirable for the manufacturer. Assaults through  the IC back surface can be performed using failure analysis  tools and techniques like focused ion beam, photon emission  spectroscopy, laser stimulation, etc.

We divide attacks targeting the ICs through the back surface  into two categories, based on the level of tampering with the IC

backside required to perform the attacks. The first category  consists of attacks which need backside preparation, such as  silicon thinning, silicon polishing, or structure editing by  focused ion beam [3,4].  The second group includes attacks  which are based on optical signal tracking. These attacks are not  destructive to the back surface and are accomplished optically  without any changes to the IC backside. For instance, through  laser flout injection or tracking photon emitted by the IC  structure [5,6]. Therefore, an appropriate countermeasure for  the IC backside must prevent the photon transition through the  IC backside and avoid any tampering with the IC back surface.  An opaque silicon back surface which can be achieved with  coating an opaque thin film on the surface prevents attacks  through the IC backside. As long as the layer remains intact,  neither optical attacks nor attacks which are physically  tampering with IC backside, such as attacks using focused ion  beam (FIB), are possible. In this method, the integrity of the  layer is checked through measuring the photocurrent of  detectors.

Through optics, the information or signal can be transferred  between sender and receiver without an electrical connection.  Light emitting devices and photodetectors are optically coupled  and are used to generate and detect optical signals, respectively.  Therefore, we can utilize an optical technique to get  information about the backside when there is no electrical  connection between frontside and the backside. For this  purpose, we need to generate light inside the IC which is  emitted in the substrate toward the backside, reflected on the  chip backside, and absorbed by the IC components on the  frontside. In the following section, we describe how to generate  and track optical signals inside an IC that can be utilized to  protect the IC against attacks through chip backside and  conduct failure analysis, as well as which kind of light emitting  device is applicable for this purpose.

## II.   E XPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

To generate optical signals inside the IC, the convenient way  is to operate a p-n junction in forward bias. The energy of the  photons generated by the silicon p-n junction corresponds to the  silicon band gap (1.12 eV) [13]. When the light spreads inside  the silicon, depending on the wavelength (energy) of the light  and doping level of the silicon, it may be absorbed by the silicon  bulk or pass through it. The part of the light that is not absorbed  reaches the silicon back surface and depending on the interface  and the angle of incidence of the light, it may be reflected or

transmitted. Figure 1 illustrates the cross-section of a chip  where a p-n junction acts as a light emitter (LE). The LE emits  in all directions within the silicon substrate towards the IC back  surface. For the silicon-air-interface, there is a total reflection  for the light with an angle of incidence (AOI) greater than   $16^{\circ}$    (blue line in fig. 2). For tracking the optical signal, we can run  the p-n junctions in reverse bias, as photodetectors (PDs). These  p-n junctions will absorb the reflected light on the IC backside  and generate a photocurrent, as shown in fig. 1. The  photocurrent of the detectors is proportional to the intensity of  the reflected light. Any structure on the IC backside may  change the intensity of the reflected light. Therefore, the  photocurrent of the detectors is defined by the added structure  on the IC backside. In this method, through tracking the optical  signal illuminated inside the IC, we gain information about the  IC backside.

III. N ON - SILICON  LED

In this section, we discuss alternatives for the light source,  which can be used for this method. Silicon is the base material  for most of the ICs, and with today’s technology, we cannot  have a non-silicon based diode within the IC. Therefore, an  efficient light emitting device (LED) which is made of  semiconductor material with a direct bandgap can be used as an  external LED to create an optical signal. An LED applicable for  this technique, firstly, must emit light in the wavelength that is  absorbable by the drain and the source of the transistors that are  used as detectors. Second, it should emit in the direction where  the photodetectors absorb only the light reflected on the IC  backside (proper placement of the LED on the chip), and third,  the optical power of the LED should be enough to affect the  photodetectors.

To have an efficient LED, we need to define the wavelength  where the detectors have the maximum absorption. It is also  important to consider that light is partially absorbed by the  silicon substrate as it passes through to reach the detector. To  find this wavelength, we measured the photocurrent of a  detector over varying light wavelength. The detector for this  measurement was a p-n junction in   $250\mathrm{nm}$   technology with a  doping concentration of about   $10^{20}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}$  . For this assessment,  light reaches the detector vertically from the backside of the IC,  where the thickness of the IC (silicon bulk) is   $310\upmu\mathrm{m}$  . The  emission power was almost the same for all wavelengths. Thus,  we can consider the photocurrent curve over the wavelength as  a spectral response of the detector. The response curve is  normalized from zero to one. The spectrum in fig. 4, black line  illustrates that at the wavelength of 1010nm, the detector has its  maximum absorption. This wavelength may differ slightly for  different technologies and depends on the thickness of the IC.  For each device that we want to protect with this method, we  know the depth of the chip and the location of the detectors.  Therefore, we can easily calculate the light path length to get to  the farthest detector. Figure3 shows the schematic cross-section  of the LE and a photodetector, where d is the ship’s thickness  (silicon bulk depth), and X is the horizontal distance between  LE and PD. We know d and X; thus, we can calculate the length  of the path that the light should travel to get to the PD, 2L (from  LE to the back surface and from the back surface to the PD).

Comparing the curves explains that the wavelength of the  maximum response has been shifted to the larger wavelength.  The photocurrent of the detector is reduced if the detector is a  greater distance from the LE because (1) photons with shorter  wavelengths are absorbed by the silicon bulk more than photons  with longer wavelengths. (2) The detector is also a silicon p-n  junction, and there is a low absorption coefficient for silicon in  larger wavelength. Thus, the light source must emit at a  wavelength which all detectors respond to, and this wavelength  is the peak of the response of the farthest detector. For instance,  where 2L for the last detector is   $1100\upmu\mathrm{m}$  , the illumination peak  of the LE must be at 1070nm. A narrow band emitting device is  recommended. Because in wide-band emission, part of the  radiation with higher energy (lower wavelength) will be  absorbed by the silicon bulk, and the detector is not responsive

to the light with lower energy. The emission spectra of the LED  must be like the red curve in fig. 5.

In order to maximize the angle of the incidence of the LED  light within the IC (silicon-substrate), the LED must be placed  in contact with the IC and the LED material should have a  refractive index close to the silicon’s refractive index. For  instance, GaAs (Gallium Arsenide) can be an appropriate  choice [11].  Therefore, our non-silicon LED can be a single p-n  junction formed by a layer of p-type doping in an n-type GaAs  wafer. Besides material, the location of the external LED on the  chip is an essential consideration that in the next section is  discussed.

## IV. P LACEMENT OF THE EXTERNAL  LED  INTO THE CHIP   

In each IC, the side where the IC components (transistors,  capacitors, etc.) are located is called the frontside. Backside  refers to the back silicon substrate side. On the frontside, on top  of the active region, there are several layers of metal  connections, interconnect wires, dielectric, and meshes (shown  in fig. 6). To generate an optical signal inside the IC using an  external LED, we need to place the LED in contact with the IC.  In general, there are three ways to set the LED in touch with the  IC, namely from the frontside, backside, or along the sides.  Should the LED be placed on the IC backside, light is unable to  penetrate the opaque protection layer. Should the LED be  placed after the coating inside the silicon, it is not desirable as  well because light will reach the detectors directly without  interference.

Since the direct light  path from the emitter to the detector is the shortest, the intensity  of the reflected light from the coated layer is significantly  lower, hence, not desirable. One way to mitigate is to place an  opaque layer on a portion of the LED surface to block the direct  light path between LED and detectors and direct the emanating  light to a certain angle. However, this is not a desirable location for the  LED, especially for large area devices such as FPGAs where  the circuits of high sensitivity are located further away from the  sides.

Similarly, the placement of the LED on the frontside or in  between the interconnects of the IC is unable to achieve the  desired outcome because of very limited light penetration  through the dense metallization to enter the silicon and reach  the reflective surface. The most efficient way to place the LED  on the IC frontside is by removing the metal layers and  dielectric from a small portion of the chip, within the area of  interest. In this case, there will be little obstructions, and light  will emit into the IC towards the IC backside and the light  reflected on the chip backside be detectable by photodetectors  (fig. 8). Accordingly, the surface of the LED which is in contact  with the silicon must be completely flat; the p-n junction must  be on top of the GaAs wafer so that light can pass through the  GaAs and enter the silicon. As the absorption coefficient of the  GaAs in the wavelength over   $1000\mathrm{nm}$  is insignificant and much  lower than silicon, we can ignore the light intensity reduction  caused by GaAs [11].

## V. P ROS AND CONS OF THE EXTERNAL  LED  

In this chapter, we discuss the advantages and drawbacks of  using a non-silicon LED for our IC protection mechanism and  compare it to the silicon LED. First, we analyze the  electroluminescence of the silicon LED and non-silicon LED.   Figure 9 shows the spectral Si-LED emission and the spectral  response of the detector. Comparing these two curves illustrates  that most of the Si LED radiation is in the range where the  detector is not responsive or has very low absorption. The  maximum emission is at   $1110\mathrm{nm}$   while the maximum  responsivity is at 1010nm when the light hits the PD’s surface,  which shifts to 1070nm when lights travel   $1100\upmu\mathrm{m}$   in silicon,  and then hits the PD (discussed earlier - fig. 4). The second and greatest advantage of non-Si LED over Si  LED is that the non-Si LED is made of a direct semiconductor.  Radiative recombination is the process that dominates in the  direct bandgap semiconductors, which is not the case for Si  LED but GaAs-LED. into the chip. Since the light must pass through Si-GaAs  interface to enter the silicon, the silicon and GaAs surfaces must  be flat enough and should be in good contact with each other.

## VI. C ONCLUSION 

This paper considers a non-Si LED to generate an optical  signal in a silicon IC to be utilized in an IC protection  mechanism. The non-silicon LED is a GaAs LED with a  narrow-band emission. The LED produces light with an energy  that can be absorbed by the IC structure after traveling inside  the silicon substrate. So, to build an efficient LED, the emission  spectra of the LED must be determined by considering the size  of the IC and the responsivity of the structures on the device  that we need to protect. Gallium arsenide is a direct bandgap  semiconductor with a refractive index close to the silicon  refractive index. Therefore, the generated light by external LED  spreads into the silicon substrate in all directions without  changing the direction. The optical signal generated by the  external LED will be tracked by the IC structure to reveal any  harms on the IC backside. In this protection mechanism, a  non-Si LED will be installed on the IC frontside to generate the  optical signal toward the silicon substrate. The IC back surface  is coated by an opaque layer that provides angle-dependent  reflectivity for the silicon interface. Several p-n junctions on the  IC are used as the photodetectors to detect the reflected light on  the IC backside to reveal any tampering with the layer. In this  work, silicon and non-silicon are compared, and the location of  the external LED is discussed.

Although mounting the non-silicon light source into the chip  adds another step to the manufacturing but provides an efficient  light to affect the detectors without facing degradation issues.  When the chip is protected with the mentioned technique and  the silicon surface is opaque to infrared light, applying optical  techniques for failure analysis from the outside is no longer  possible. Having a stronger light source within the IC has the  advantages of generating photocurrent for failure analysis or  fault injection.
