## Imaging Electronic Degradation in a Stressed GaN  HEMT  

William A. Hubbard   NanoElectronic Imaging, Inc., Riverside, CA, USA

Phone : (+1) 617-347-6436, Email:  bhubbard@NanoElectronicImaging.com

Abstract —The standard tool for high-resolution imaging of  microelectronics is the transmission electron microscope  (TEM), which can resolve atomic-scale features in thin crosssection   samples   extracted   from   (pristine   or   stressed)  components. However, cross-sectioning typically leaves such a  sample “dead” electronically. Even for an electronically  “viable” sample, TEM is insensitive to a device’s electronic  structure and may be blind to the dynamics responsible for a its  function and failure. Here we discuss the preparation, and  in  situ  stressing, of a cross-section extracted from a GaN high  electron-mobility transistor (HEMT) using a plasma-focused  ion beam (PFIB). Scanning TEM electron beam-induced  current (STEM EBIC) imaging is used to confirm the electronic  viability of the sample and to reveal bias-induced electronic  changes under normal operating values applied  in situ . Under  in  situ  stressing, subtle electronic defects appear in STEM EBIC  prior to any physical signs of stress. After stressing to the point  of failure, indicated by a sudden decrease gate-source resistance,  defects form which generate intense STEM EBIC contrast  under small bias, but no evidence of the defects are seen with  standard TEM-based imaging.

Keywords— GaN HEMT, STEM EBIC, TEM, PFIB, reliability

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

The TEM is a central tool in the development,  manufacture, and qualification of electronic components.  TEM excels at resolving the physical structure and, with  spectroscopic attachments, the composition of a sample.  Modern systems can routinely do so with atomic resolution.  But TEM imaging contrast is insensitive to local changes in  electronic properties, such as conductivity and electric field,  and can typically only detect defects when they are coincident  with substantial physical damage. Additionally, TEM samples  are typically thin cross-sections extracted from components  using a focused ion beam (FIB). This process can deposit  damage and contamination, rendering the extracted region  “dead” electronically. (Here we make the distinction between  a “failed” device, which ceases normal function due to defects  introduced during manufacturing or stressing, and a “dead”  device, which may or may not have failed before FIB sample  preparation.) Making connections to such a sample, to enable  electrical biasing in the microscope, compounds sample  preparation challenges.

Here we use STEM EBIC to map electronic changes,  induced  in situ , in a GaN HEMT cross-section under normal  bias conditions, under stressing, and after a failure event. GaN  HEMTs are a less mature semiconductor technology than, for  instance, Si-based components. Their applications in highpower and high-frequency signal processing, often in extreme  environments, make understanding reliability in these  components   critical.   However   degradation,   such   as  irregularities in the two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG),  may not produce obvious contrast in TEM. Here, an  electronically “alive” GaN HEMT cross-section sample is

prepared using a PFIB, and STEM EBIC enables live  observation of electronic changes that are invisible to normal  STEM imaging.

## II.   E XPERIMENTAL  D ETAILS 

STEM EBIC maps are generated by measuring sample  current at each image pixel.  EBIC current can be generated  by different processes, each of which corresponds to different  electronic signals. The current generated from the separation  of electron-hole pairs (EHPs) in an electric field is the most  common form of EBIC imaging and has been in use in  scanning electron microscopes (SEMs) since the 1960’s [1].  In an EBIC mode more recently demonstrated in STEM,  called SEEBIC, a positive (hole) current is generated by the  emission of secondary electrons (SEs) from the sample [2].  SEEBIC is typically a much smaller current [2], can be  acquired at atomic resolution [3], and can generate resistance  contrast in samples with more than one electrical connection  [4, 5].

The sample in Fig. 1 was extracted from a commercial RF  GaN HEMT using a PFIB with inert  $\mathrm{Xe^{+}}$   ions, which reduces  surface damage and contamination compared to   $\mathrm{Ga^{+}}$   milling.  As indicated in Fig. 1, the gate is measuring EBIC, the source  is grounded, and the drain is floating. STEM EBIC, and in  particular conductivity contrast provided by SEEBIC, allows  for direct feedback on sample quality. For example, the bright  contrast in the gate-measured EBIC in the upper metal layers  of the device (where we expect no electric field) indicate good  continuity from the gate electrode all the way to the EBIC  amplifier. The sharp interface of this bright region falls along  the insulating SiN layer below and to the right, indicating it is  behaving well as an insulator even after cross-sectioning. (The  much more intense signal below the gate will be discussed in  depth in the following section.)   In general, STEM EBIC  screening enables much more straightforward preparation of  electrically connected TEM samples, eliminating the blind  trial-and-error involved in only using simple electrical testing  to determine sample quality.

Under bias, the Fig. 1 device exhibits I-V (current-voltage)  characteristics that are strongly diode-like, as shown in Fig. 2.  This is another indication that the sample surface is free from  conductive   contamination.   If   there   were   significant  conducting surface contamination in the I-V, this parallel  ohmic path would result in a much less pronounced knee in  the I-V plot, and the current would rise must faster under a  larger negative bias. For each data point in the Fig. 2 plot, the  current measurement was extracted from a STEM EBIC  image acquired with the indicated gate bias applied. A STEM  EBIC image is effectively a precise current measurement  associated with each pixel. As shown in the Fig. 2 inset, the  electron beam is blanked for the first few lines of each EBIC  image. The current in the beam-blanked image region  provides a measure of device current. Because this is a beamindependent signal (and, by definition, not an EBIC), it also

serves as background to be subtracted before currentcalibrating STEM EBIC images.

III.   R ESULTS AND  D ISCUSSION

The images in Fig. 3 show STEM EBIC under bias values  within the safe operating range (-4 V to 1.2 V). Under zero  bias, the bright EBIC signal measured is due to the electric  field at the Schottky barrier formed at the gate/AlGaN  interface. Under negative bias (the polarity that depletes the  2DEG and turns the channel OFF), the field at the barrier  increases with bias magnitude, and a particularly strong field  signal is observed at the edges of the gate. Under positive bias,  the barrier is reduced, which enables measurement of the dark  EBIC current from the 2DEG at the AlGaN/GaN interface  below the gate. Compared to the barrier signal, the 2DEG  appears less smooth, which may indicate non-uniformities in  the 2DEG charge density distribution.

The defects, which generate intense signal in the Fig. 5 STEM EBIC,  produce no measureable contrast with these two standard imaging modes.

The electric field-sensitive mode of EBIC, EHPseparation, can persist 100’s of nm from the field and is  typically limited in resolution by the minority carrier diffusion  length [1]. However, this mode of STEM EBIC can be  performed with  much  higher spatial resolution in samples with  carrier mobility significantly limited in one or more directions.   In this sample, for example, EBIC signals are confined  vertically to regions  ${\sim}20~\mathrm{nm}$   thick and attenuate within just a  few nm outside of them. The unusually sharp features of the  EBIC here are the result of carrier confinement (by-design) in  the heterostructure. Laterally, the barrier signal exists only  along the gate/AlGaN interface, as the barrier field is caused  by, and only present at, this interface. The 2DEG should be  continuous laterally across the entire field of view in this  normally-ON channel. However, the EBIC signal from the  2DEG observed under bias also seems to end at the gate edges.  This may be due to the insulating SiN that crosses the field  lines between the gate and 2DEG in this region, preventing  EHP separation.

The absolute maximum gate-source bias rating for the  source GaN HEMT component is   $\mathrm{-}10\ \mathrm{V}$   to 2 V. The crosssection device was stressed by applying  $\mathsf{a}-20~\mathrm{V}$   gate-source  bias for approximately 20 minutes. During stressing, device  current increases by a few percent (as measured in Fig. 1), and  afterward the bright spot at the right edge of the gate, seen  under -3V bias, becomes much brighter (shown later in Fig. 6  “stressing”). While the efforts to completely understand these  subtle changes in the EBIC signal are ongoing, the location,  bias polarity, and increase in signal with time (not shown here)  are all consistent with electron injection into the barrier layer.

While applying  $+8~\mathrm{V}$   bias, a sudden 100-fold increase in  gate-source current is measured,  coincident with the  appearance of some nanoclusters spread uniformly around the  gate region (see Fig. 4A). Neither the STEM images in 4A nor  elemental maps in Fig. 4B, generated by energy-dispersive xray spectroscopy (EDS), show obvious signs of a leakage path  formed during during the failure event. Without applied bias,  STEM EBIC also shows no significant changes after failure.

## IV.   C ONCLUSION   

We prepared an electrically contacted, TEM-compatible  GaN HEMT cross-section. Electrical testing and STEM EBIC  imaging confirm good isolation between device electrodes,  and that the component’s electronic structure is largely intact  after cross-sectioning. We mapped electronic changes to the  barrier and 2DEG with the application of gate bias in the  microscope. We bias-stressed the device  in situ  and observed  electronic degradation before any indication of physical  damage. Finally, we observed the formation of distinct defect  regions in the EBIC following a sudden increase in gatesource current under stressing. No such features are observed  in the standard STEM imaging channels or EDS mapping.

The biasing/stressing results are summarized in the flow  chart and images in Fig. 6. In this study, we began with a  device extracted from a component that had not been biased  prior to cross-sectioning and performed stressing  in situ . But  cross-section samples may be extracted at any point in the Fig.  6 flow chart. For example, components may be subjected to  normal stressing or lifetime tests, and defective regions can be  located using a lower-resolution technique such as SEM  EBIC. Defective regions can then be extracted for STEM  EBIC imaging and biasing, like the data in Fig. 5, to  unambiguously locate and characterize defects produced  under more realistic operating conditions.

The observation of nanoscale electronic changes,  measured alongside standard STEM images and electrical  testing data, during  gradual  degradation provides a uniquely  complete view of the processes involved in defect formation.

While an off-the-shelf GaN HEMT was chosen for this  particular experiment, these methods can be adapted to study  the function and failure of countless other materials and  devices. This methodology provides unique, high-resolution  means for the characterization of manufacturing defects,  studying the fine details of normal and stressed device  operation, and definitively identifying and understanding  faults.
