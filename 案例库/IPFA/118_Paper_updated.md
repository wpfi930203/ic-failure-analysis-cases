## Automotive Electronics EOS Failure Analysis and  Diagnosis  

Suming Wang, Yongxiang Liu, Hongri Bai,   Lixiang Innovation Laboratory Technology (Jiangsu) Co., Ltd., Li Auto, Changzhou, China  Phone : (+86)13862112914, Email :  wangsuming@lixiang.com

Abstract —Automobile industry has dramatically changed  over past two decades. All OEMs start to integrate more IC  components and implement high-end technology functions.  Among automotive electronics failure, EOS related issue always  takes the highest failure rate. This paper identifies failure  analysis of component ESD failure, board level and system level  EOS failure. Related diagnosis process and root cause  identification are also shared.

Keywords—Automotive IC, EOS, ESD, Failure Analysis

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

For all automotive electronics, electrical overstress (EOS)  induced failure is always a controversial topic. Original  Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs), Tier one vendor, Tier two  vendor, semiconductor related industry all suffer different  kinds of EOS failure. Some may define electrical induced  physical damage (EIPD) as the same category as EOS.  Another common related issue is electrostatic discharge  (ESD) related failure, which is normally considered as a subcategory of EOS failure [1]. According to statistical results [2],  EOS related failure contributes over   $10\%$   of total failures and  over  $30\%$   of electronics failure. However, many EOS failure  cases ended with unknown root cause. Oftentimes, when  analyst observe obvious failure phenomenon such as  carbonization, wire melting, die surface damage, there is no  much further analysis can be pursued. Also, misunderstanding  between EOS and ESD would cause completely different  investigation direction. For OEMs, it can be extremely hard to  execute effective actions to address EOS induced quality  issues with unknown reasons. Not only does this affect cost,  but it also impacts market reputation and contributes to low

market confidence levels for customers’ purchasing decision.  Thus, for OEMs, it is crucial to identify different kinds of EOS  failures, define true root causes, and take preventive actions.  In this paper, three cases are shared to demonstrate EOS  failures from component-level failures to system-level  failures. Related failure analysis, repeated experiments, root  cause identification, and prevention actions are detailed.  Finally, a standard EOS investigation process flow is also  provided.

## II.   S TUDY  I   C OMPONENT LEVEL  F AILURE   

During the early months of 2023, the application field  reported several cases of brake system downgrading. Upon  inspection, they were confirmed to be caused by electrical  motor sensor failures. Further testing at the printed circuit  board (PCB) level, specifically through an on-off switch test,  revealed that the oscilloscope captured an initiation reset cycle  of 1.5 seconds, which is abnormal, as the correct reset cycle  should be 2 seconds. Subsequent testing confirmed that all  failed sensors exhibited the same abnormal reset cycle time  failure.  Upon  examination of the PCB design, it was found  that the reset cycle time is monitored by a watchdog chip. This  chip is used to monitor the microcontroller working status to  prevent software malfunction in automotive microcontroller  or microprocessor power-supply applications [3].   A  multimeter was used to check for any pin-to-pin failures.  However, no obvious open or short failures were detected.  Yet, after replacing the suspected failed IC with a fresh  watchdog IC, the reset cycle time returned to a normal state,  indicating a highly suspected malfunction in the watchdog IC.

Consequently, further  destructive analysis was undertaken, involving the use of laser  and chemical methods for decapsulation. Thermal hotspot  testing was then employed to isolate the die's internal failure  location. Fig. 1(a) indicated a suspicious hotspot near the  failure pin pad. For failure analysis, it's critical to delineate the boundary  conditions of package-level and die-level failures. However,  die-level physical analysis always entails high risk. Once  delayering is initiated, it becomes challenging to recheck the  electrical status unless micro-probe technology is employed. Returning to this case analysis, reactive ion etching (RIE) and  manual polishing were utilized to eliminate die’s internal  metal layer. Optical microscope observation was meticulously  performed at each layer. Fig. 1(b) to 1(d) showed de-layer  observation and no obvious anomality was observed. As the  observation approached the transistor layer, scanning electron  microscope is used for micro-scale observation. The final  observation is done at gate oxide (GOX) layer.  Fig. 1(e) and  1(f) illustrated that the failure occurred on a typical ESD  protection multi-finger structure [4]. Pinholes observed in Fig.  1(f) further indicated local overstress-induced gate structure  breakdown. This case exemplifies a typical component-level  ESD failure, where no obvious physical damage signature was  present on the die surface.

To further confirm root cause, it is necessary to verify if FA  result aligns with the failure background.  The damaged ESD  protection structure, located under the failure pin bonding pad,  responsible for timer adjustment, correlates with the abnormal  on-board reset time cycle failure. These modes share many  similarities, such as short pulse width, quick rise time, and  short duration time.   The primary difference lies in the ESD  energy carrier: human body or conductive surface. Also, CDM  is normally faster than HBM, and obtains higher current peak  with low energy. ## III.    CASE STUDY II BOARD LEVEL FAILURE 

In a single vehicle, there are over 200 pieces of PCBs, and  not all PCB function failures would immediately cause car  breakdown. For instance, a failure in an interior light control  module might only result in a lamp failure, not affecting the  normal driving experience. However, for critical modules  such as body control and power control, any electrical  component failure could lead to immediate breakdown. Such  failures are always regarded as severe quality issues,  irrespective of the total failure quantity.   During the winter of  2022, control module failures were reported at the end of the  line (EOL) testing at OEM production lines. After boardlevel analysis, abnormality in the current sense amplifier chip  was identified. The I-V curve indicated a shortage failure.   Upon chemical decapsulation, physical damage on the die  surface was observed, as shown in Fig. 4(a). This clearly  indicates a typical Electrostatic Overstress (EOS) failure  signature.  Most analysts might stop at this point, as the failure  symptom is identified. However, for the OEMs, this marks  just the beginning. If EOS source is not found, similar failure  could persist during car assembly and affect manufacturing  processes.

Noteworthy is the physical damage surrounding the  bonding aluminum pad, typically encircled by the ESD  protection circuit. Another crucial aspect is that the two  damaged pins are linked to supply and load side shunt resistor,  indicating that external power supply should hit those pins  first. At this point, high energy ESD event failure is suspected.  Following a similar investigation approach to previous case  study, Tier one vendor’s SMT process was checked,  especially ICT station. However, no obvious evidence was  found. Direct human touch was also ruled out both at OEM  and Tier one production lines. To better pinpoint the root  cause, repetition experiment was designed. There are total

three sets of condition; ESD event on IC external pin, ESD  event on board, consecutive negative voltage pulse. For IC  and on board ESD test, the condition both range from 5kV to  15kV with 1kV step. And for negative voltage pulse, it was  set at -20V for 10s through PCB power supply side. This  simulates unexpected opposite side power supply through  external connector. The experiment is set to simulate  component level ESD event, board level ESD event and  continuous EOS condition.  Fig. 4(b) to 4(d) depict the results.  The current sense amplifier chip survived the 5kV ESD event  but failed at both the 15kV ESD event and negative voltage  pulse. Fig. 4(d) illustrates severe carbonization, a typical EOS  failure. However, this doesn't align with the original failure  in Fig. 4(a).  The 15kV ESD event on board yielded the same  failure observation as Fig. 4(a), physical damage on die  surface near bonding aluminum pad. Hence, the failure is  confirmed as a board level ESD event and next is to find event  source.

Returning to the OEM production process, in the final  assembly stage, most electronic units are interconnected by  wires. Any potential ESD event can easily pass through these  wires and reach the PCB connectors and internal components.  In the case of this particular power control module, its high  operating conditions necessitate the use of large-scale wires.  To secure these coarse wires, in addition to normal screws,  plastic covers are employed to tighten the connections.  Using  an electrostatic voltage tester,  it was discovered that these  plastic covers had high voltage discharge residue. Fig. 5  depicts random checks at different production lines, revealing  that the highest voltage residue exceeded 15kV on the plastic  covers, posing an immediate risk of ESD failure during Endof-Line (EOL) hot plug-in tests. The high energy voltage  residue creates an electric field, this induces charges in the IC  device. When external power supply is plugged in, the high  voltage potential difference would initiate ESD event. This  issue was identified as a material problem: the plastic cover  lacked dissipative properties. During transportation, material  contact and peeling could initiate ESD events, and such  plastic material had poor ability to transfer ESD residue. By

This case shows a common EOS failure signature, die  surface physical damage. From FA perspective, EOS failure  can be defined as long as die surface failure is visible. The  experiment indicated that carbonization requires a relatively  longer EOS time compared to an ESD event.

## IV.    CASE STUDY III SYSTEM LEVEL FAILURE 

In previous two cases, failures were only discernible after  component analysis, whether assembly level or die internal  level.  Root causes were found to originate from either  electronics manufacturing or external material issues.  However, for OEMs, physical damage is often already visible  without the need for detailed component IC level analysis. Fig.  6(a) and 6(b) illustrate an example of severe EOS damage on  a certain type of control module PCB, with obvious burnt  marks visible on both the PCB and components. If  decapsulation were to be performed on the components, there  would undoubtedly be evident carbonization, wire melting, or  other common EOS signatures. In such situations, there isn't  much component analysis work required for failure analysts.  However, there are still certain tasks that Failure Analysis  (FA) can accomplish.

By observing the burnt marks on the PCB,  it becomes  apparent that the initiation of damage starts near the connector  and extends through the chip power pin. This suggests that an  unexpected spike hit the connector, providing excessive  power supply. The energy involved is significant enough to  damage the PCB's internal copper traces. The connector is  covered by a plastic shell, eliminating the possibility of  accidental human touch at the OEM factory. To make sure  there was no discharge residue remains at front side, SMT  assembly in the Tier one vendor was also scrutinized, yet,  similar to the previous two cases, no obvious clues pointed to  human touch. Production-related HBM possibilities were  excluded. So far, there is no clear indication pointing to a  potential EOS source. This scenario reflects a common  challenge faced by OEMs: encountering known EOS failure  symptoms with unknown root causes.

When dealing with this kind of tough case,  it is essential  to consider system level failure. ## V.    D IAGONIZE  EOS  FAILURE 

To effectively address EOS failure, it's crucial to  recognize that any EOS event must surpass the absolute  maximum rating (AMR) work condition of the affected  components. These failures can manifest as latent issues or  immediate failures, and they can range from high-voltage,  short-duration events to milder, longer-duration events. EOS  failures often induce secondary failures, complicating root  cause identification.  The correct analysis flow for EOS  failure should begin with comprehensive background  information collection. OEMs, Tier one vendors and IC  companies should gather information outlined in Table 1  before initiating actual analysis. When the issue is narrowed  down to a certain IC chip, failure analysis should follow  electrical test, non-destructive package level inspection and  destructive physical analysis. Case study I illustrates a proper  FA flow from I-V test to final GOX layer abnormal SEM  observation.  Once the failure symptom is observed, repetition  experiments should be conducted to confirm the EOS failure  mode. Case Study II demonstrates that different EOS  conditions can lead to different failure symptoms,  emphasizing the importance of repetition experiments before  jumping to root cause identification. After defining the EOS  category, the process of identifying the root cause begins.  While this step is challenging and time-consuming, the true  root cause is often not straightforward. Neither OEMs nor  Tier one vendors should pass the pressure down to the next  level vendor. Efficient investigation requires cooperation  among multiple parties. In Case Study III, where there were  no obvious component or PCB defects, the final prevention  actions involved OEM structure design, Tier one PCB design,  and IC component selection. Without cooperation from  OEMs to IC companies, the problem cannot be solved in a  short period.  The endless discussion between OEMs, Tier one  vendors and IC companies about whose responsibility it is are  counterproductive. Instead, every party needs to work  together to effectively address EOS failures and prevent their  recurrence.

For failure analysis,  the primary objective is to provide  accurate analysis results. Physical damage can occur at  various levels, ranging from transistor level to package level,  and further to board level and system level. In Case Study I,  the physical damage occurred at the die internal ESD  protection structure gate oxide layer. The analyst may  conclude the investigation as an ESD failure in the FA report.

To enhance understanding, it is recommended to include a  comment that ESD failure is typically considered as a special  category of EOS.  During package level analysis,  common  EOS symptoms include molding compound carbonization,  wire melting, die surface damage, and die surface  discoloration. At the board and system level, EOS symptoms  are usually visible, and destructive physical analysis may not  always be necessary.  For root cause identification, FA analyst  can contribute to the design of repetition experiments and  related qualifications. Additionally, FA teams can construct  database from past cases and give potential solution for  similar issues. This collaborative approach helps improve the  efficiency and effectiveness of failure analysis processes.

## VI.    C ONLUSION 

EOS failure is indeed one of the most common electronics  failures encountered by OEMs. In this context, three failure  cases have been presented to illustrate component-level,  board-level and system-level EOS failures. Component-level  ESD failure manifests at the die internal gate oxide layer,  while the symptom of component EOS failure is typically  visible at die surface. However, the root cause of component  EOS failure may originate from board-level ESD events.  Both board level and system level failure may exhibit similar  failure symptoms, but the root cause can vary, ranging from  material issue to system design issue. Key factors for  diagnosing   EOS   include   comprehensive   information  collection, following the correct analysis flow, and fostering  multi-party cooperation. By adhering to these principals,  OEMs can effectively identify and address EOS failures,  thereby improving the reliability and performance of their  electronic systems.
