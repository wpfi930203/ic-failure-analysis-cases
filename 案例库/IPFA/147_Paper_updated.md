## The characterization of CoSi x  spike in silicon  substrate by transmission electron microscope  

Guoyang Ye\*, Tong Wu , Xianfeng Chen   Semiconductor Manufacturing International (Shanghai) Corporation Failure Analysis Laboratory, Shanghai 201203, China   Zhangjiang Road #18, Pudong New Area, Shanghai, P. R. China, 201203  Phone : (+86)18850570147, Email : guoyang_Ye2@smics.com

Abstract —In this work, we reported an analysis case study  on CoSi x  spike. In order to understand the generation  mechanism and root cause of CoSi x  spike, the detailed  characterization on microstructure and composition of the  CoSi x  are performed by transmission electron microscope,  including in-situ transmission electron microscope (TEM),  electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS), and energy dispersive  spectroscopy (EDS). In-situ TEM is a powerful tool to analyze  the evolution of CoSi 2  silicide. It was found out that during  silicidation process, the volume expansion is caused by phase  change, diffusion of Co atoms as well as stress in multi-phases  locations are the root cause of CoSi x  spike generation. Finally,  possible solutions were provided to avoid the   $\mathbf{C_{0}S i_{x}}$   spike and  improve the production yield.

## Keywords—in-situ TEM, EELS, CoSi x  spike  

## I.    I NTRODUCTION :  

As the devices scale down to sub-  $0.15\upmu\mathrm{m}$  , self-aligned  Cobalt silicide contacts (with low sheet resistance of   ${<}5$  ohm/square) are widely used for its outstanding performances,  especially for its immunity to narrow line width effect [1-3].  However, channel leakage and resistance variations issues of  Co silicide cause several low yield problem in ultra-shallow  junction devices with technology node below   $0.13~\ensuremath{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$  [3].  Hsu et al. have reported that the resistance variation was  induced by Co-Silicide layer with highly varied thickness and  forms, resulting from which are given by the stress from  surrounding structures [3]. The junction leakage problem has  been clarified in terms of a   $\mathrm{CoSi_{x}}$   spike leakage model [4, 5].  Normally, low resistance CoSi 2  phase was formed by using  two-step annealing process [5]. At the first anneal step (at   ${\sim}400–425^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$  ), the transformations of    $\mathrm{Co}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{Co}_{2}\mathrm{Si}$   and   $\mathrm{Co_{2}S i\!\Rightarrow\!C o S i}$   are both take place simultaneously. At the  second step (at   ${\sim}820^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$  ), the   $\mathrm{Co}_{2}\mathrm{Si}/\mathrm{CoSi}$   is then transformed  into CoSi 2  low resistance phase [5]. Sukegawa et al. have  proved that the junction leakage current increased  dramatically during the first salicidation process (from  $450\mathrm{{}^{o}C}$  )  [5]. Thus, it is reasonable to speculate that the   $\mathrm{CoSi_{x}}$   spike is  formed at first anneal step and remains at the end of second  anneal step. Lowering the reaction temperature of first step  can reduce the probability of   $\mathrm{CoSi_{x}}$   spike defect. In order to  completely solve this problem, more detailed investigations  are needed to answer when these defects generated and what  the composition of these defects are and how to prevent   $\mathrm{CoSi_{x}}$  spike formation during process.

## II.   E XPERIMENTAL 

The Si substrates used in this work were (110) oriented.  The TiN cap layer was utilized to prevent oxidation of Co  during annealing. Two-step rapid thermal anneal (RTA) was  adopted to fabricate the CoSi 2  low resistance phase.  Transmission electron microscope (TEM), energy dispersive  X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), electron energy loss spectroscopy

(EELS) and In-situ heating TEM analysis were used to  characterize the morphology, chemical composition, crystal  structure, chemical valence and material evolution process.  All the cross-sectional sample analyzed by TEM were  fabricated by focused ion beam technology (FIB, FEI Helios,  CX) operated at   $30\,\mathrm{kV}$  . TEM investigation was done by using  FEI Tecnai F20 TEM operated at   $200\ \mathrm{keV}$  with EDS for  elemental analysis. Elemental chemical valence state analysis  was performed by using EELS (Gatan, GIF 965). In-situ  heating TEM experiments was done by using DENSsolution  Lighting TEM holder. An in-situ heating TEM analysis was  preformed to directly observe the characteristics of   $\mathrm{Co}/\mathrm{Si}$    reaction and composition at each RTA temperature. The  heating rate of in-situ heating TEM experiment is   $10^{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{C/min}$    from room temperature to   $300^{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{C}$  ,   $3^{\circ}\mathrm{C/min}$  from   $300^{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{C}$  to   $500^{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{C}$  , and   $8^{\circ}\mathrm{C/min}$   from   $500^{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{C}$   to  $800^{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{C}$  .

## B.   Valence states analysis by EELS analysis  

EELS is a powerful technique with nano-meter spatial and  high energy resolution (1 eV) for analyzing the chemical  environment of  transition metal oxide compounds [7]. When   L2, L3 electrons are transferring into these empty 3d bands to  form bonds with others,  energetically narrow d band causes  strong sharp features for the L edges, known as white line.

The L3 and L2 lines are the electron transitions from 2p3/2  to 3d3/2, 3d5/2 and from 2p1/2 to 3d3/2, respectively. Their  individual intensities are related to the unoccupied states in the  3d bands and the L3/L2 ratio in the EELS spectrum can be  used to determine the valence state of   $\mathrm{CoSi_{x}}$   composition.   EELS mapping was performed as shown in Fig. 3. Co is  transition metal and is related to the presence of elements with  mixed valence states. Fig. 3 showed the EELS results of Co  and Si mapping which is in consistent with EDS results (Fig.  1) in terms of elements . The sphere defects is composed of  Co and Si. The decreasing L3/L2 intensity ratio gives  increasing density of state for occupied 3d bands. As shown  in Fig. 3 d), the L3/L2 ratio of defect area is 1.01, it is similar  to the value of L3/L2 ratio in Source/Drain side, which is 1.07.  Also, both silicide curve and defect curve in spectrum shows  no chemical shift with respect to each other. Combine the  results of NBD index (Fig. 2), the   $\mathrm{CoSi_{x}}$   compound can be  identified as  $\mathrm{CoSi}_{2}$   with high confidence.

After RTA 2, CoSi  continue reacting with Si to form CoSi 2 . Because CoSi spikes  usually generate locally and grow rapidly, it is hard to find the  original place until it starts generating. ## IV.   D ISCUSSION 

From the results shown above, the generation progress of  CoSi 2  spike can be illustrated in Fig. 6. At first step, Co layer  was deposited on Si substrate, the following TiN capping layer  is to prevent Co oxidation. After that, a   $400{\sim}500^{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{C}$   thermal  anneal was used for cobalt silicification. Theoretically, the Co  reacts with Si substrate to create intermediate product Co 2 Si.  Immediately after that, more Si reacts with  $\mathrm{Co}_{2}\mathrm{Si}$   to form CoSi.  However, this  $\mathrm{Co}\!\to\!\mathrm{Co}_{2}\mathrm{Si}\!\to\!\mathrm{CoSi}$   reaction can’t take place at  identical rate for every horizontal location. When the Co layer  is thin enough, in this case, below 80 nm, the CoSi conjugates  upward to form grain. Also, Co 2 Si forms grains due to the  ascending CoSi. The   $\mathrm{Co}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{Co}_{2}\mathrm{Si}$   and   $\mathrm{Co_{2}S i\!\Rightarrow\!C o S i}$   reactions  take places on the same surface and in the form of grain. Both   $\mathrm{Co}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{Co}_{2}\mathrm{Si}$   and Co 2 Si  CoSi reaction consumes Si then leave  Si vacancies. Meanwhile, the stress caused by thermal  expansion coefficient difference between CoSi and Co 2 Si,  volume and phase change during the reaction, force part of  CoSi in Co 2 Si-CoSi phase boundary to migrate towards those  Si vacancies. The migrating CoSi then shapes like a spike. The  CoSi spikes keeps generating at   $\mathrm{T{=}}450^{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{C}$   before   $\mathrm{Co}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{Co}_{2}\mathrm{Si}$    transformation completed, then it reacts with adjacent Si then  contracts to a CoSi 2  beads at higher temperature   $(\mathrm{T}{>}600^{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{C})$  .  The band of voids, due to the Kirkendall effect and phase  formation induced volume changes, was formed between the   $\mathrm{Co}_{2}\mathrm{Si}$   and CoSi phase boundaries [8-10].

Tripe-point (CoSi,   $\mathrm{Co}_{2}\mathrm{Si}$   and   $\mathrm{Si/SiO_{x}}$  ) was generated  (illustrated in Fig. 7).   Etch usually carves a sharp corner,  which causes stress hotspot, a CoSi spike may generate,  penetrating along the specific direction due to the high surface  energy among different phases. In order to obtain the low  resistance phase of CoSi 2 , a   $2^{\mathrm{nd}}$   rapid thermal anneal is  adopted. There is possibly a two-step reactions to form CoSi 2   in RTA 2, namely [10]:

2CoSi→CoSi2+Co  (1)  $12.2\%$  volume reduction CoSi + Si→CoSi2    (2)  $75\%$  volume reduction

The   $75\%$   volume reduction of reaction (2) is less likely  to occur, since the strain energy introduced by this  shrinkage is sufficient to impede the reaction progress. Due  to the volume reduction, the needle-like CoSi spike is  shrinking into spherical CoSi 2 .

From the discussion above, it comes to the conclusion that  the CoSi 2  sphere originates from CoSi spike in RTA1. Thus,  the key to decrease the CoSi 2  sphere is to suppress the needlelike CoSi spike formation in first rapid thermal anneal process.  Sukegawa et al. suggested that lower RTA1 temperature can  decrease the density of CoSi spike   $(<\!430^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$   [5]. Also, a preion amorphous of Si substrate before Co/TiN deposition by  using   $\mathbf{N}_{2}/\mathbf{G}\mathbf{e}$   to keep uniformity of boundaries can also be a  good way to decrease CoSi spike [11].

## V.   C ONCLUSION   

In this work, we presented the detailed TEM investigation  for the root cause and mechanism understanding of Co  diffused into Si substrate. The   $\mathrm{CoSi_{x}} $  compound were  identified as CoSi 2  by using the combination of EDS, EELS  and NBD techniques. The generation mechanism of CoSi 2   sphere were analyzed by In-Situ TEM and possible solutions  were also provided. In order to avoid such spiking/sphere  morphology, a reliable silicide process needs to be carefully  developed.
