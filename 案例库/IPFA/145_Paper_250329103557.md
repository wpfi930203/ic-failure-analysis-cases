## Analysis of Etcher Configuration on Part Marking Characteristics for Counterfeit Identification 

Jacob Harrison ∗ , Nathan Jessurun † , Raphael R. Dos Santos ‡ , Shajib Ghosh ∗ , Navid Asadi ∗ , and Mark Tehranipoor ∗ ∗ University of Florida, Gainesville, Florida. Email: jacob.harrison@ufl.edu † Terraverum LLC, Austin Texas ‡ Email: raphael.rds.work@gmail.com

Abstract —Laser mark analysis is common when analyzing suspect counterfeit electronics, but prior mark analysis has been limited to subjective quality evaluation or visual comparison against golden samples. In an effort to identify objective metrics for part mark analysis, we explore how laser etcher characteristics and configurations impact a mark’s appearance. We first analyze the theoretical impact of etcher configuration changes on mark characteristics, then conduct a controlled study of fifty integrated circuits etched with different settings to assess which settings can be discerned in low-magnification images. Our study suggests that observable part marking characteristics can indicate part provenance and inform objective, data-driven decisions about integrated circuit authenticity.

Index Terms —Counterfeit, marking, etching, assurance, hardware security

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

Counterfeit chips are a booming business [1] and a threat to reliability in critical systems [2]. To mitigate this threat, new devices can incorporate anti-counterfeiting technologies like ‘odometers’ and manufacturer-attested cryptographic identities [3]. Unfortunately, these technologies cannot be used to verify obsolete chips or commodity parts (e.g., capacitors). For parts like these, visual inspection is still one of the most common anti-counterfeiting tools [3]–[5].

Visual inspection for counterfeit detection could be strengthened by improving analysis of laser-etched part numbers and logos. Laser etchers have many configurations, each of which impact mark appearance. Understanding the relationships between etcher configuration and mark appearance could yield objective, granular metrics for describing marks and distinguishing authentic electronics from counterfeits. We explore this idea by 1) assessing the theoretical impact of several etcher configurations on part mark appearance, then 2) marking fifty integrated circuits using different etcher configurations to study whether configuration changes can be inferred from the resulting mark.

We begin with brief background on counterfeit electronics, prior part mark analysis, and other approaches for identifying counterfeit components. Then, we explain how laser etchers work and explain why marks made with different etcher configurations are expected to be visually distinguishable. We give the methodology and results from an experiment that assessed visual distinguishability of parts marked with

different etcher configurations. Finally, we discuss our results and establish directions for future work.

## II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Laser marks on integrated circuit (IC) packages often indicate a chip’s model, manufacturer, and manufacturing date or lot code. They consist of letters, numbers and, on some chips, the manufacturer’s logo.

A counterfeiter might re-mark an IC for various reasons: recycled ICs may be blacktopped and re-marked so that they appear new [6], commercial-grade parts may be re-marked as military or space grade to fetch higher profits [1], or one part could be re-marked and misrepresented as a completely different part [2].

Laser mark inspection aims to identify counterfeit parts by assessing whether characteristics of a part’s mark are consistent with expectations for that chip. Assuming that genuine chips were all marked in the same way, inconsistencies in mark characteristics between a known-genuine and suspect part may be evidence of counterfeiting.

## A. Prior study and practice of part mark analysis 

Several published case studies discuss part mark inspection [6]–[8]. These studies describe:

1) Comparison of fonts and symbols [6], [8]   2) Searching for irregular character alignment [6], [8]   3) Comparison of marking color [8]   4) Burn marks as an indicator of low mark quality [8]   5) Mark texture analysis (“smooth” or “coarse”) [7]

First, we note that the large number of variables involved in producing a laser mark (see Section III) suggests that  more information about part authenticity could be extracted from part marks than prior work has explored. 1

Second, analysis based on subjective metrics (e.g., quality or smoothness) or on visual comparisons (e.g., alignment inconsistencies or the presence of burn marks) is  only meaningful in the context of a human expert’s intuition and holistic assessment.  This diminishes the value of mark analysis for counterfeit investigations in several ways:

1) Mark inspection is  difficult to automate because mark characteristics cannot be objectively measured.  Automated visual inspection (AVI) is necessary to enable high-coverage and high-confidence counterfeit screening.   2) Inspection  accuracy depends on the inspector’s judgment and attention to detail.  It is challenging to assess the rigor of an analysis based on subjective metrics and visual comparisons.   3) It is  challenging to interpret mark quality differences without granular measurements.  For example, a low quality mark could be caused by a failing laser at an authentic manufacturer, or it could be a sign of counterfeiting. These cases may be differentiable by more specific criteria.

In contrast to subjective measurements and visual comparisons,  we propose metrics based on specific, observable mark characteristics that tie back to etcher configuration. Such metrics are objective, straightforward to interpret, and amenable to automation.

## B. Related work in automated visual inspection 

We are aware of no prior work that automates laser marking analysis, but other AVI algorithms for counterfeit detection have been explored.

Some proposed approaches automatically search for signs of abuse (e.g., from harsh recycling processes [1], [2]), such as scratches [4] and bent leads [9], but these techniques fail if counterfeits are handled carefully. Moreover, when considered in isolation, most defects are only a weak indication that a chip is fraudulent (authentic chips can display scratches, too!). Cosmetic defect detection could certainly play a role in AVI-based counterfeit detection, but more study is needed, specifically in distinguishing authentic wear-and-tear from counterfeit characteristics.

Another approach, which relies less on human intuition, uses statistical analysis of package texture and indentations to separate fraudulent from authentic ICs [10]. The initial evaluation of this technique was promising, but it is unclear from limited study how well it generalizes across manufacturing facilities, package types, component types, etc. This approach, if developed further, could complement laser mark analysis in a suite of AVI algorithms for counterfeit detection.

One recent technique combines AVI with the idea each component could have a unique, difficult-to-forge signature [11]. This works by noting that some manufacturing processes (injection molding, in this case) naturally impart a unique “fingerprint” that could be extracted using inexpensive cameras and fast image processing [12]. The idea is promising, but it can only be applied to parts that are currently in production and therefore cannot solve a major counterfeiting problem involving scarce, obsolete parts [2]. A key advantage of the laser mark metrics we propose is that they can be applied to old components.

## III. A  BRIEF TUTORIAL ON LASER ETCHING 

A simplified laser etcher schematic is shown in Fig. 1. To create a mark, control circuitry excites a laser source, causing it to emit photons. An amplifier focuses these photons to concentrate their energy on a smaller area. Technical details of this process depend on the laser medium, which is selected for the wavelength it emits.

After the laser is focused and amplified, motor-controlled mirrors redirect the beam to move it around the sample. The movements of these mirrors are programmed by the laser etcher’s control software. Besides the positions of the mirrors, their scan speed may be adjusted and rest periods can be programmed to allow the positions of the scan mirrors and motors to stabilize after a series of rapid movements.

Finally, lenses focus the beam such that the laser’s power will be most concentrated a certain distance from the condenser lens. Moving nearer to or further from the lens relative to this focal point will bring the laser spot out of focus.

When the laser beam meets a sample surface, the sample reacts to the added energy and its appearance is permanently changed. The specifics of this reaction depend on the laser source, the amount of laser energy absorbed, and the sample material [13] Different sample materials have different tendencies to accept energy of a particular wavelength, so it is important to select a laser type appropriate for a sample tobe-etched.

The laser’s beam is not continuous; it is a pulse train. Characters and shapes on the sample are the sum of many discrete marks. The frequency, peak power, and duty cycle of this pulse train are all configurable.

Most lasers used for IC package etching are    $Q$  -switched lasers. The physics and mechanics of a Q-switch are beyond the scope of this discussion, except we note that operators of these lasers must contend with  giant pulses . When a Qswitched laser medium is not emitting energy, it accumulates it. Thus, if a laser marker does not emit any pulses for a period of time (e.g., the time required for scan mirrors to reposition from the end of one character to the beginning of the next), the laser’s next pulse will be especially powerful due to extra accumulated energy. If these giant pulses are not managed, they burn the sample at the beginning of laser strokes.

Peak power  controls a mark’s hue, texture, and depth. Power differences may be responsible for the differences in color and texture visible in Fig. 2.

Focus  controls how concentrated a laser’s energy is on the sample. Intentional de-focusing yields a lighter hue and softer texture [15].

Scan speed  controls how quickly an etcher’s mirrors move the laser pulse train over a sample. Fig. 3 illustrates the difference between a character etched with a slow (a) and fast (b) scan speed, assuming constant pulse frequency [16].

Pulse frequency  controls how rapidly laser pulses are emitted. It is related to scan speed as shown in Fig. 3.

Initial acceleration , also called start TC, controls the “ramp time” where scan mirrors accelerate before the laser starts pulsing. A proper start TC setting ensures that energy is distributed evenly throughout a stroke and prevents burn marks. Fig. 3c illustrates laser pulses with poor initial acceleration.

First-pulse suppression  prevents excess energy stored in a Q-switch from being unevenly dissipated in the laser’s first few pulses. Poor first-pulse suppression leaves burn marks [17] as seen in Fig. 2a.

Repeat etching  strokes a patterns multiple times to improve contrast more gradually than blasting it with more energy in a single stroke. A repeated etch can also occur if no clearance is allowed over previously-marked spots, as in the authentic chip’s  4  in Fig. 2b.

Scan mirror positioning and alignment  can degrade over time as motors wear. It can also be impacted by excessively high scan speeds as motors attempt to snap from one location to the next and begin etching before their position settles. Hatch settings  control how logos or raster/true-type fonts are filled. Fig. 4 illustrates several possible patterns. An operator can also determine whether a region gets an outline stroke, and can set  line spacing  between adjacent strokes.

## IV. E XPERIMENT DESIGN 

Our work tests two general hypotheses: first, that  computed properties of laser marks are consistent across samples etched with the same configuration  and, second, that  computed properties of laser marks change across samples etched with different configurations.  Table I elaborates on our second hypothesis to make per-configuration predictions. Note that Table I gives only changes that we deemed most

likely to be observable with rudimentary analysis and is  not an exhaustive list of property changes that may be observable in a well-tuned environment.

In addition to testing these predictions, our work has an exploratory component. This work is the first to explore how much an etch visually changes for a given quantitative configuration change. Future work can use our findings to write testable hypotheses of the form: “a counterfeit mark whose peak power differs from the authentic part by    $X$   will be distinguishable in  Y  characteristic”.

## A. Procedure 

To evaluate the visual impact of various etcher configurations, we etched 62 IC samples with different laser configurations. The 62 ICs were the same, 2   and the same laser was used to mark all samples. Table II describes the laser used. The same characters 3   and logo were etched on all samples.

We started by etching a “base mark” to serve as a standard for comparison. Every other sample in our dataset differs from the base mark in exactly one configuration. The amount of difference varies across samples. Two samples were etched for every configuration, including the base mark. The base mark’s configuration is summarized in Table III with the configurations for other samples.

We photographed each sample using a Zeiss stereoscope. All images were taken at the same time to minimize differences in ambient lighting. We used VGG Annotator [18] to draw rectangular annotations around each character and logo in each image, yielding 11 annotations per image. Finally, we wrote a program to analyze annotated regions.

## B. Analysis 

Fig. 5 illustrates our analysis pipeline. The script that executes this pipeline is on TrustHub with our data.

The script takes in annotated images and crops out each annotated box. It iterates over these cropped-out-annotatedregions.

For each region, we compute the image transforms listed in the next section. Each transform yields a 2-dimensional matrix of results (not a single scalar value).

Next, we compute a histogram for each property with ten uniformly-spaced bins. The count of each bin is the number of pixels from the image transform matrix whose value was in that bin. The bin edges are determined by finding the highest and lowest values of each property across all eleven regions of an image and evenly spacing within these bounds. After this step, we have one histogram per image transform per annotated region per image.

Finally, for each image and image transform, we take the median bin count across all eleven annotated regions. The result is ten values per property per image. These are the values displayed in the plots in Section V.

The following image transforms were chosen to measure differences between the laser markings.

The  Hough line transform  computes the tendency of image pixels to make straight lines. When lines are more clearly resolvable in an etch, the Hough line transform will increase.

The  Hough circle transform  computes the tendency of image pixels to make circles. When circles are more clearly resolvable in an etch, the Hough circle transform will increase.

A  standard deviation filter  measures how similar neighborhoods of pixels are. Where discrete laser etch points can be discerned, individual strokes are visible, or underlying package texture is more pronounced, the standard deviation for pixel neighborhoods will increase.

Mean pixel intensity  measures the average color in an image or region.

Edge gradient phase  measures the angle at which edges occur in an image. When changes from light to dark pixels consistently occur in the same direction, edge gradient phase will be consistent. Conversely, jagged edges result in lots of  $90^{\circ}$   phases instead of   $45^{\circ}$   phases.

## B. Scan speed 

Table I made two predictions for scan speed: the first was that standard deviation would increase with increasing scan speed, the second was that mean pixel intensity would decrease with increasing scan speed.

Fig. 6 shows one sample for each scan speed configuration. As expected, the mark tends to be lighter for higher scan speeds because fewer pulses are made per each unit area. The fringes on logos and distorted shapes of the high-speed marks occur because we did not adjust Start TC with scan speed, so

the beginnings of some marks were poorly etched. Full-size, high resolution images are available on TrustHub.org.

1) Effect on standard deviation:  Fig. 7 illustrates the effect of scan speed on standard deviation. The plot does not show that standard deviation increased with scan speed as we predicted: at both low and high standard deviation values, the  $8000~\mathrm{mm/s}$   samples had low standard deviations. We believe this is because our prediction was geared only toward the standard deviation within etched characters while our analysis operated on regions that contained background. Our results

reflect that contrast between etched characters and background is generally lower for higher scan speeds while differences in the texture of etched characters are a weak signal by comparison. Additional experiments need to be conducted to support or disprove this new hypothesis.

2) Effect on mean intensity:  Fig. 8 quantifies the effect of scan speed on mean pixel intensity. For all scan speeds, very few pixels are close to black. Bins 2 and three provide noisy signals, likely because these bins are saturated with background pixels. Trends become clear starting with bin 4: high scan speeds have higher counts of moderate pixel intensity in bins 4 and 5, but as pixel intensity increases the lower scan speeds have higher pixel counts. This matches our prediction from Table I. Higher scan speeds impart less energy to the sample, allowing more background color to show through. Also, high scan speeds leave more space between adjacent etches. Conversely, low scan speeds impart more energy to the sample and leave darker marks.

## C. Peak power 

Table I made two predictions for peak power. The first was that increasing peak power would decrease standard deviation because etches would be more uniform, unless power was increased enough to leave burn marks which would increase standard deviation. The second was that higher power would correlate with lower mean pixel intensity because laser interaction with the sample is more intense and burn marks are more likely.

Fig. 9 shows samples etched for each peak power setting. As expected, increasing power makes a mark appear darker and, in the extreme case, more “burnt”. The lowest power setting did not impart enough energy to the laser sample for our chosen pulse frequency and scan speed to leave a visible mark, so we exclude it from the following plots. Full-size, high resolution images are available on TrustHub.org.

1) Effect on standard deviation:  Fig. 10 shows a clear increase in standard deviation for higher laser power: the lowest standard deviation bins have similar values, but as we move from left to right across the plot, corresponding to bins with higher standard deviation values, the 17.5W and 22.5W etches have higher pixel counts.

There are certainly burn marks on both the 17.5 and 22.5 W images. Thus, the portion of our hypothesis that predicted strong edges when there are burn marks applies. Our observations match our prediction.

2) Effect on mean intensity:  The mean intensities depicted in Fig. 11 do not match expectations as clearly. The 22.5 W images’ deep, dark burns are clearly visible as an unusually high pixel count in bin 1. For every other image in our study, there are very few pixels so dark. For the rest of the lowintensity and moderate intensity bins, the high-power samples continue to have the highest counts. This means they are generally darker, which agrees with expectations. For bins 7 and above, the 22.5 W images have the fewest counts and 17.5 W images consistently have slightly higher counts than the 10 W images. It was not expected that the 17.5 W

images would have the highest count of high-intensity pixels. Additional investigation of this result is needed, although this finding along with several others could be explained if our simple model that expects low pixel intensities for higher power absorption is inaccurate.

## VI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We proposed that laser marking could become a more effective anti-counterfeiting tool that it presently is. The large number of ways an operator can tweak an etch suggest that laser mark analysis could carry lots of useful information, but more granular analysis of etch properties is needed to tease out which of these properties can be detected and to make analysis more objective. Also, computer-aided property extraction is necessary for this analysis to be scalable and consistent.

Our work takes first steps toward closing these gaps. We etched a series of chips in a manner designed to isolate the effect of individual configuration changes on mark appearance and built an image analysis pipeline to measure the difference in part markings using standard image transformations.

This was an exploratory study and our analysis was rudimentary. We have made our data available so that others can build on our analysis and fine-tune future data collection. Several low-hanging fruit for improving our analysis include: 1) making semantic annotations of our laser marks instead of rectangular regions to separate properties of etched characters from the properties of the background, 2) Color-normalizing raw images to remove differences due to ambient lighting, and 3) trying different image transformations and parameters (e.g., different neighborhood sizes). Low-hanging fruit for future experiments include 1) using a green laser instead of a fiber laser, 2) etching more samples with single-line fonts (which are typical for part numbers) instead of hatched fonts, 3) performing experiments at different magnification levels to observe how observable features change, and 4) performing real-world case studies (e.g., with chips from Vendor A vs. Vendor B).
