## Techniques and Applications of Plasma Focused Ion  Beam in Silicon Die Delayering  

Xue Yao Chong 1,2  , Dionaldo Zudhistira 1   1 Advanced Micro Devices (Singapore) Pte Ltd, Device Analysis Lab, 508 Chai Chee Lane, Singapore 469032  2 School of Materials Science and Engineering, Nanyang Technological University, 50 Nanyang Ave, Singapore 639798  Phone: (65) 6796 9888, Email:  xueyao.chong@amd.com

Abstract —Plasma Focused Ion Beam (PFIB) has emerged  as a reliable silicon die delayering tool particularly for  advanced nodes devices. Xenon PFIB is shown to have high  controllability and precision in exposing BEOL layer, suitable  for SEM inspection using the least damaging electron beam.  Ion beam irradiation is known to physically damage sample  surface,   $\bf{30\;\;k V}$   Xenon beam penetrates deep into copper  metallization which negatively impact nanoprobing electrical  characterization. PFIB delayering with endpoint detection  (EPD) was reproduced with brief discussion on secondary  electron (SE) yield curve. Comprehensive understanding of  ion-matter interactions would facilitate deployment of PFIB  in silicon die delayering as semiconductor industry moves into  the angstrom era.

Keywords—Delayering, endpoint, failure analysis, focused  ion   beam   (FIB),   gallium,   implantation,   inspection,  nanoprobing, plasma focused ion beam (PFIB), sample  preparation, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), silicon die,  simulation, xenon

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

Xenon plasma focused ion beam (PFIB) system has  emerged as a promising tool for gallium-free TEM lamella  preparation, cross-sectioning, and precise micromachining  [1]. When compared to the well-established gallium focused  ion beam (FIB) tool, Xenon PFIB could achieve high beam  current for a faster milling rate, a reduction in physical  damage (i.e., lower amorphization depth), and elimination  of chemical interaction with the sample as xenon is an  intrinsically inert noble gas. Gallium ion beam is known to  induce significant amorphous damages to silicon at   $30~\mathrm{kV}$  with significant physical implantation [2,3,4]. In addition,  gallium may react chemically with copper within back-endof-line (BEOL) interconnects, forming copper-gallium  intermetallic phases which is undesirable in failure analysis  material characterization. Gallium is also known to diffuse  into grain boundary of aluminium, causing significant  embrittlement [5]. As a noble gas, Xenon has the advantage  of being chemically inert and stable. Xenon ion is also  atomically more massive than gallium ion, which results in  higher sputter rate when ion strikes the surface of a sample.  These advantages of Xenon ion beam have enabled  development of precise planar delayering of metal layers in  a silicon device using inductively coupled plasma source  within PFIB [6].

Manual silicon die delayering by hand uses a grinding  or polishing equipment with suitable polishing cloth and  polishing suspension (typically micronized diamond).  Delayering quality, uniformity and endpointing is highly  dependent on operator skill, size of the silicon dies and  properties of the layers to be removed. For a greater  uniformity, reactive ion etch (RIE) is sometimes deployed  to remove interlayer dielectric (ILD) uniformly across the  entire die. The aggressive scaling of interconnect layers at

advanced nodes has resulted in increasing challenges in  controllable   manual   delayering   and   endpointing,  subsequently causing difficulty in targeting specific  metallization or via layer especially at the lower layer of the  BEOL stack. Xe PFIB has been successfully demonstrated  in cases where delayering precision in nanometre scale is  desired [6]. This paper will discuss the techniques of Xe  PFIB on silicon die delayering and rationalize the  importance of a precision endpointing in conjunction with  application of an accurate layer exposure. Further, endpoint  detector (EPD) will also be explored on silicon device  delayering for automation development.

## II.   D ISCUSSIONS   

A.

Precise Layer Exposure for SEM Inspection

There is an increasing demand for uniform die  delayering and precise endpoint due to tight dimension of  BEOL in recent advanced silicon devices. Besides,  transistor degradation due to electron beam irradiation  during SEM inspection has been reported [7]. To protect the  electrical integrity of the transistor, acceleration voltage of  electron beam can be limited to   $500\;\mathrm{~V~}$   and below for  imaging at few final layers of BEOL and especially contact  layer. As such, great importance is placed to precisely  delayer silicon device to the desirable z-depth for effective  scanning electron microscopy (SEM) inspection. Embedded  defect cannot be resolved if it is out of the range of the  electron beam interaction volume as collected through  secondary electron [8].

Monte Carlo simulation of electron trajectory in solids  (CASINO) can be used to simulate electron beam  penetration and distribution when SEM imaging is  performed on copper interconnects and interlayer dielectric  for via layers [9].

## B.   Delayering for Nanoprobing  

Nanoprobing workflow using PFIB delayering has been  proven on advanced FinFET devices [10,11]. In practice,  PFIB delayering for nanoprobing would require care to  reduce damages from ion beam collision events, ion  implantation and dislocated lattice which causes vacancies  and subsequently amorphization. At the front-end-of-line,  trap charges and gate oxide damages are some of the  possible artefacts caused by high energy ion beam  irradiation. Such damages can be measured and quantified  in terms of transistor electrical characteristics including  saturation current, off current and threshold voltage changes  [12].

Compared to gallium ion beam, xenon ion beam causes  less damage. This is due to the larger atomic radius of xenon  which reduces interaction volume. Nevertheless, given  enough kinetic energy, xenon ion beam will still create  substandard surface for nanoprobing through poor contact  surface. The depth of ion interaction volume can be  predicted using Transport of Ions in Matter (TRIM)  simulation [13]. Assuming   $20\ \mathrm{nm}$   thick low-k interlayer  dielectric (ILD) of carbon-doped hydrogenated silicon

oxide (SiOCH) and a   $20\,\mathrm{\nm}$   thick copper interconnect,  xenon stopping depth of   $2\mathrm{\kV}$  ,   $12~\mathrm{kV}$   and   $30~\mathrm{kV}$   can be  estimated as follows.

Fig. 4 shows that Xe beam penetrates about 4 nm into  copper metallization layer at   $2\mathrm{\kV}$  . The depth of damage  zone is smaller than the typical landing depth of a  nanoprobe, which suggest that nanoprobing contact surface  volume is superior for  $2\;\mathrm{kV}$  -prepared surface as compared  to   $12\;\mathrm{\kV}$  and   $30\;\mathrm{~kV~}$  . Furthermore, the amorphization  damage of high energy incident beam will alter physical and  electrical characteristics of copper metallization, obscuring  the true nature of fabricated interconnects along with any  presence of defects. High incident ion beam energy with  high beam current is suitable for rapid milling due to high  sputtering rate. A switch to a low energy beam is necessary  for a surface with minimal damage for nanoprobing. The  target layer for the switch would depend on the thickness of  fabricated structure. For advanced node, the switchover  from high to low beam energy will have to be multiple  layers above target due to the aggressive shrink of physical  dimension in the back-end-of-line. As the delayering  process approach target layer, switching to   ${\leqslant}2~\mathrm{kV}$   Xe ion  beam is crucial as the final finishing beam of die delayering  for nanoprobing purpose. At contact level, right above the  front-end-of-line, irradiation of high energy ion beam was  shown to affect transistor performance significantly and  negatively in terms of off current, saturation current and  threshold voltage, according to the work done on Static  random-access memory (SRAM) by Choi and Kang [11].

The Xenon ion projected range can be calculated using  SRIM, with the result plotted above. Stopping range of an  ion indicates the maximum depth where the incident ion can  be found implanted within the sample. It does not convey  the amount and extent of damage caused by ion collisions,  ionization, or recoil events. The projected range is important  to dictate the necessity of switch from a high incident beam  energy to low bean energy during PFIB delayering. ## C.   Endpoint Detector (EPD)  

PFIB secondary electron (SE) yield from SE detector is  one of the possible quantifiable milling endpoint detections.  It was reported that SE signals gave good end point to stop  delayering process at a desired metal or via layer. It is  possible to target at the top of via, the middle of via and the  top of metal layer for a   $100~\mathrm{~\textmu~m~}\times~100~\mathrm{~\textmu~m~}$   uniform  delayering box [14]. Silicon die delayering with EPD  assistance has been reproduced with SE yield expressed  using 8-bit greyscale, representing the average intensity of  the defined delayering box.

The above chart shows SE yield plotted against time,  with a range of 0 to 255 (eight-digit binary) for the y-axis.  It is important to adjust SEM scanning brightness and  contrast correctly for the curve to be representable and  resolvable within the 0 to 255 range. If the thicknesses of  metal and via layer are known, it is possible to estimate the  z-depth of delayering through the EPD curve.

The silicon device was delayered from via x  to via x-2   layer, with two metallization layers removed. SE yield  increases from via to metal layer with sharp transition peaks  observed. Transition peak  a  represents the top metal cap  while peak   b  represents the underlying diffusion  barrier/liner layer. EPD is shown to be highly sensitive and  useful in accurate delayering termination. As PFIB  delayering is conducted in ultra-high vacuum, material  oxidization and corrosion concerns are reduced. Thus,  allowing safe exposure of cobalt-based metallization or  tantalum/titanium/tungsten-based diffusion barrier layer for  inspection.

## III.   F UTURE WORK 

PFIB offers a wide range of variables for large-area  delayering process beyond ion beam energy which is  described in this paper. Beam related parameters including  current, dosage and incident angle, as well as gas-assisted  etching related parameters such as gas type, gas flow and  concentrations are all worthy of further studies to achieve  optimum milling performance for specific needs. Milling  performance may also vary depending on the target  structure. A complete understanding of delayering  performance on logic, memory, group and stacked vias,  substrate, large structures such as I/O region is necessary.

## IV.   C ONCLUSION   

PFIB is becoming highly relevant to advanced node  silicon die delayering. Contrary to the simplicity of  conventional mechanical-based die delayering, PFIB  involves highly sophisticated Xe ion beam with  significantly more variables to be optimized to achieve good  planarity and intact device integrity. From a failure analysis  standpoint, any existing defects must be kept intact and not  influenced by any means during die delayering. Further  work is required to evaluate Xe ion beam and SEM electron  beam impact on advanced transistors, ILD, interconnects  and future new structures.
