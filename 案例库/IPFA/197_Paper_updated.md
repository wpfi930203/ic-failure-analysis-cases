## Failure Analysis on Superluminescent Diode  

Xuanyu Hu, Xubo Liu, Kai Wang, Pinhuan Li, Qichao Ding, Zhihong Mai

Hubei Jiufengshan Laboratory, 9 Jiulonghu Street, Wuhan, Hubei Province 430206, P. R. China  Phone:   027-59301999, Email: huxuanyu@jfslab.com.cn

Abstract—A super luminescent diode (SLED or SLD) is an  edge-emitting semiconductor light source that relies on super  luminescence. SLDs are ideal for use in Optical Coherence  Tomography (OCT) Imaging Systems. In this paper, we  reported the failure analysis plan and method for rejections  from the SLD lifetime test. The failure modes for the SLD  reliability test have been defined. A failure analysis flowchart  was designed to identify the failure location, from the package  to the chip. A scanning optical microscope (SOM), a liquid  nitrogen-cooled InGasAs emission microscope, and a liquid  nitrogen-cooled thermal emission microscope were used for  fault isolation. An optical microscope (OM) and a scanning  electron microscope (SEM) were employed to inspect the  hotspot locations. Subsequently, a physical analysis was  conducted on the failing location, and the failure mechanism  was studied. Planar and cross-section TEM laminae were  prepared using a dual-beam FIB. TEM EDX analysis was  conducted on the cross-section TEM sample. The EDX analysis  reveals that there is no obvious difference in element  distribution, suggesting that no thermal diffusion occurred and  the failure was solely due to material degradation.

Keywords  —  Super luminescent diode, optical coherence  tomography, reliability, lifetime test, failure analysis ，  failure  mode, failure mechanism.

## I.    I NSTRUCTIONS 

Super luminescent diodes (SLEDs or SLDs), an edgeemitting semiconductor light source leveraging super  luminescent, exhibit high-power output and brightness  coupled with low light coherence. Their full-width at half  maximum ranges from 5 to 750 nm, making them versatile for  various applications [1]. In recent years, SLDs have found  widespread use in diverse fields such as optical coherence  tomography (OCT), white light interferometry, fiber-optic  link testing, WDM PON systems, fiber-optic sensors, and  fiber-optic gyroscopes [2, 3].

One prime application for SLDs is Optical Coherence  Tomography (OCT) Imaging Systems. OCT, an emerging  biomedical imaging technology, enables the acquisition of  two-dimensional, cross-sectional, micron-scale images of  biological tissues. In this application, SLDs serve as the  crucial light source [4].

Lifetime testing is a standard practice in evaluating the  performance of light sources. Notably, SLDs achieve  specified output power at significantly higher injection current  densities than laser diodes (LDs) of similar design and  semiconductor structure. While reliability studies have been  extensively conducted on laser diodes, many of the insights  gained from these studies can be applied to SLDs. However,  it is crucial to recognize the distinct electrical and optical  characteristics that differentiate SLDs from LDs. The testing  and characterization of these light-generating devices remain  at the forefront of photonics technology research [5, 6]. In this  paper, we reported the failure analysis plan and method on the  rejections of the SLD lifetime test. The failure modes were  defined for the SLD reliability test. A failure analysis flow was

designed to identify the failure location from package to chip.  Physical analysis was applied to the failing location and the  mechanism was studied.

In this paper, we reported on our work regarding the  electrical and physical analysis of a failed unit from a lifetime  test. The failure mode was verified in the laboratory. Fault  isolation was carried out on the failed sample to identify the  failing location, and subsequently, a physical analysis was  conducted at the hotspot locations for defect analysis.

## II.   F AILURE MODE AND ANALYSIS PLAN 

The failure modes were classified according to electrical  and light properties, while the failure mechanism was  categorized based on physical structures within the package  and those present in the chip. Figure 1 illustrates the physical  structure of the chip. In the top view, the waveguide ridge is  visible under an optical microscope and can be pinpointed  during fault isolation. The cross-section view reveals that the  SLD substrate and cladding layers are composed of InP, while  the active layer is a superlattice of GaAs/InP. To avoid  damage to the sample due to high current, we initially  proceeded with low-current SOM fault isolation. If the hotspot  remains undetected, we will consider using InGaAs EMMI for  fault isolation with higher current and InSb for thermal EMMI  at extremely high current levels.

A failure analysis flow, as depicted in Figure 2, was  designed, progressing from non-destructive analysis to  destructive analysis. Our initial step was to verify the failure  mode prior to any electrical and physical analysis. This SLD  unit failed during the lifetime test, experiencing a   $30\%$   power  drop compared to a functional product. IV measurement was  conducted based on the test results. During failure mode  verification, it is advisable to consider low-current SOM fault  isolation to prevent further exacerbation of the failure mode.  This verification will assist us in understanding the electrical  characteristics of the failed unit, enabling us to determine the  biasing condition during fault isolation. SOM is suitable for  low-current fault isolation as it minimizes current-induced  sample damage during the process. InGaAs EMMI typically  requires a relatively higher current to achieve sufficient  emission light intensity. For very high current fault isolation,  the thermal effect becomes significant, and InSb thermal  EMMI can be employed. Once hotspots are detected, OM and  SEM are utilized for defect inspection prior to physical  analysis. Depending on the type of defect, delayering or crosssection analysis may be considered. For instance, if a burn  mark is observed, SEM inspection with EDX analysis is  usually sufficient for a conclusion. TEM analysis should be  employed for examining material degradation.

## III. SAMPLE PREPARATION AND EXPERIMENTS

When no abnormality was found in the non-destructive  analysis, the lid of the BTF package was removed by a  mechanical tool. The die was exposed for electrical fault  isolation, which can be done with a scanning optical  microscope (SOM), Infrared emission microscope, thermal  emission microscope, and photoluminescence microscope.      In the lab, our SOM possesses the capability of opticalbeam-induced resistance change, known as OBIRCH. The  OBIRCH model is Meridan, sourced from Thermalfisher. It  features a laser wavelength of 1340 nm and a maximum power

of   $300~\mathrm{\mW}$  . The scan resolution stands at 1024x1024.  Additionally, the Meridian is equipped with a liquid nitrogencooled InGaAs camera, which has a spectrum range spanning  from 900 to 1550 nm.

The thermal emission microscope is equipped with a liquid  nitrogen-cooled InSb detection with a pixel number of  512x512. Temperature difference detection sensitivity is 1-20  mK. The ideal thermal emission range is   $3–5\;{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$  .

We proceed with physical analysis at the failing location.  Delayering, cross-sectioning, SEM, TEM, and EDX analysis  are required for defect detection and analysis. The top metal  of the   $\mathrm{{Nu}/\mathrm{{Ti}}}$   alloy was removed using reversed aqua regia  (HNO3: HCl is 3:1). Optical Microscope inspection was  performed for burn mark inspection. Cross-sectioning at the  burn mark or the hot spot location was conducted using a Zeiss  Crossbeam 350 dual beam. For defect inspection, it is  necessary to maintain a certain thickness of the lamina. If a  burn mark is found, EDX analysis will be performed on the  TEM sample. Our TEM analysis was conducted using a JEOL  JEM-ARM300F.

In failure analysis, we typically measure the IV curve to ascertain  the failure mode. The output power of the light emission is measured only if  the fault isolation process fails to pinpoint the exact location of the failure.  During our fault isolation, we analyzed the electrical-related failure with a  focus on the higher input current failure mode. From the IV curve depicted  in Figure 3, despite the reported higher input current, no apparent  abnormalities were detected in the diode characteristics at a low forwardbiased voltage. We suspected that the failure might be a soft one occurring  at high operating voltages. To prevent damaging the chip at higher currents,  we continued with fault isolation at a low biased current.

Since no obvious abnormality was found during the failure  mode verification, and an SLD typically operates at a high  input current, high current fault isolation is required. We  initially employed the EMMI mode of the SOM for fault  isolation.

From the diode IV characteristics, during the SOM/EMMI  fault isolation process, the laser was incident on the chip's  backside to avoid the signal being blocked by the metal on the  front side, and microprobing was used to bias the sample. Note  that, as the chip was already encapsulated on the substrate, we  first needed to expose the backside of the chip through PFA  before positioning it. According to our failure mode  verification results, a constant voltage of 1 V was applied to  the sample. After fault isolation, we re-measured the IV curve  on the sample to ensure there was no additional damage. Two  electric luminescence (EL) hotspots were found on the sample,  as shown in Figure 4. The locations of the hotspots were close  to the emission surface of the SLD.

Using reverse aqua regia, the top metal layer was removed,  and neither the optical microscope nor the SEM revealed any  burn marks at the hotspot location. Figure 5 displays a topview SEM image of sample 1, clearly showing no visible  defects or burn marks.

## IV.   C ONCLUSIONS   

In this paper, we reported on the failure analysis plan and method  for a failed SLD unit from a lifetime test. The failure modes were defined for  the SLD reliability test, and a failure analysis flow was designed to identify  the failure location, from the package to the chip. As fault isolation tools, we

used a scanning optical microscope, a liquid nitrogen-cooled InGaAs IR  camera, and a liquid nitrogen-cooled InSb thermal emission microscope.  Physical analysis was applied to the failing location, and the failure  mechanism was studied. An optical microscope and SEM were used for burn  mark inspection at the hot spot. Planar and cross-section TEM samples were  prepared using a dual-beam FIB. TEM EDX analysis was applied to the  cross-section TEM sample. From the EDX analysis, we observed that there  was no obvious difference in element distribution, implying that no thermal  diffusion occurred and the failure was solely due to material degradation.
