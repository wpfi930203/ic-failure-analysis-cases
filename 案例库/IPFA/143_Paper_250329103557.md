## Enhanced Yield Prediction in Semiconductor Manufacturing: Innovative Strategies for Imbalanced Sample Management and Root Cause Analysis 

Pang Guo 1 , Yining Chen 2 , School of Micro-Nano Electronics. Zhejiang University, Hangzhou,Zhejiang ,China 1 Phone : (+86)13666072478, Email : pang.guo@zju.edu.cn 2 Phone : (+86)13003630090, Email : yining.chen@zju.edu.cn

Abstract —This paper presents a novel framework for predicting semiconductor wafer yields using Wafer Acceptance Test (WAT) parameters, addressing the challenge of unbalanced datasets commonly encountered in wafer manufacturing. WAT, essential for monitoring process stability and cost control, and Chip Probing (CP) testing, which assesses the functionality of each Die on a wafer, are integral to ensuring product quality. However, the yield data, indicating the proportion of functional chips post-CP testing, often suffer from data imbalance, skewing machine learning predictions towards the majority class and reducing model accuracy. To overcome this, the study leverages Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) to generate balanced samples by creating minority class samples for model training. This approach not only mitigates the costs associated with CP testing but also facilitates early fault detection in wafer manufacturing. Through extensive experimentation with real industrial WAT and yield data, the proposed method demonstrates a significant improvement in model performance, enhancing it by approximately   $\mathbf{\lambda_{7}}\%$   and achieving an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of over 0.95. Additionally, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) model is employed within the framework to rapidly analyze and identify faults impacting yield, further contributing to the efficiency of semiconductor production processes..

Index Terms —WAT, yield prediction, GAN, Faults analysis, SHAP

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

In semiconductor manufacturing, yield refers to the proportion of valid chips on a wafer to the total number of chips, a critical indicator of a foundry’s manufacturing capabilities and profit potential. This paper addresses explicitly post-CP test yield, where chip probing is a process that tests the functionality of each chip on a wafer. During semiconductor manufacturing, WAT, also known as process control monitoring, is commonly conducted, incorporating various electrical tests of the wafer to aid in process stability monitoring, yield improvement, and cost control [1]. Due to the potential correlation between WAT parameters and yield, recent years have seen numerous studies employing machine learning methods to predict yield data using WAT test data. Utilizing WAT parameters for yield prediction effectively reduces costs associated with CP testing. Additionally, it assists in detecting faults in wafer manufacturing, enabling timely fault analysis. This approach is a proactive method that enhances overall production efficiency in the semiconductor industry [2].

However, in the semiconductor industry, collected data often exhibit significant imbalances, primarily due to the prevalence of high-yield wafers, especially in mature factories. Conversely, products in the development phase present the opposite scenario. This imbalance poses challenges for predictive machine learning algorithms, as they inherently favour the majority class, leading to suboptimal model performance [3]. Various methods have been employed to address this issue, including sampling techniques, ensemble learning, and data synthesis. GANs train generators and discriminators in tandem to learn the data distribution [4], thereby generating training sets that mirror the original data’s distribution while introducing diversity. Despite the potential of GANs, their application in yield prediction based on imbalanced WAT datasets still needs to be explored. This paper proposes using generative oversampling methods to address the class imbalance issue in yield prediction. We introduce three generative approaches: vanilla GAN, conditional GAN (CTGAN), and Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty (WGANGP). We conducted an experimental analysis using imbalanced WAT and yield datasets collected from a genuine factory to evaluate the performance of the proposed GAN oversampling methods, comparing these techniques with other oversampling methods. The results convincingly show that GAN-based methods consistently outperform interpolation-based methods, enhancing the yield prediction model’s performance by approximately  $7\%$  , with AUC scores exceeding 0.95.

In the semiconductor manufacturing sector, adopting artificial (AI) methodologies for yield loss analysis has emerged as a significant trend in recent years. Traditional yield analysis methods, such as Statistical Process Control (SPC) [5] and outlier detection techniques [6], have been primarily utilized to identify the root causes affecting yield. While these methods have been effective for some time, the increasing complexity brought about by advancements in integrated circuits and the expansion of testing parameters has led to their limitations. In light of this, AI-based analytical techniques pay attention to their ability to manage the growing complexity and intricate parameter correlations, thereby reducing biases in analysis. This paper proposes using the SHAP model to identify the root causes affecting yield. By calculating SHAP values of a yield prediction model, it is possible to effectively pinpoint

the most impactful factors on yield, thus saving valuable time and costs in analyzing yield loss.

In conclusion, we propose a new framework for yield prediction and analysis designed explicitly for imbalanced data sets. We applied the GAN method for data generation on imbalanced data sets, used the produced balanced data set for yield prediction, and utilized the SHAP model to interpret the model to facilitate root cause analysis(As shown in Fig. 1). Thus, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:

1. The GAN approach is employed to handle the problem of dataset imbalance. This method deviates from traditional approaches, which typically use SMOTE for oversampling to deal with dataset imbalance; GAN employs a neural network to learn the sample distribution and generates a new dataset consistent with the original distribution.

2. We introduce a process of failure analysis using explainable machine learning. This involves using the SHAP model to provide an interpretation of the model. This approach significantly enhances engineers’ efficiency in analyzing the root cause.


## II. R ELATED  W ORK 

Utilizing WAT data for yield prediction has become increasingly popular over the past few decades, with numerous machine learning techniques applied to yield prediction models. Techniques such as SVM [7], random forests(RF) [8], and XGBoost [9]have been employed in the realm of yield prediction. However, these studies have largely overlooked the issue of data class imbalance in yield prediction. This issue has severely hindered the performance of yield prediction models, prompting numerous researchers to seek solutions. In [10], the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was used to oversample multimodal data in semiconductor manufacturing. In [11], a method combining SMOTE with Tomek links was adopted for oversampling, and the proposed method was validated to outperform using SMOTE alone. In [12], a method employing multivariate non-normal distribution, correlation analysis, and PCA transformation was used to model the variable distribution, thereby generating a large amount of data for small sample sizes. The aforementioned SMOTE-based methods enhanced model performance through data augmentation by interpolating neighbouring instances. Although this approach improved model performance, it could not guarantee that the distribution of the generated data matched that of the original data, and more data diversity was needed. Therefore, this paper proposes using models based on GANs to generate data, ensuring that the produced data have the same distribution as the original data and enhancing data.

In semiconductor manufacturing, failure analysis related to yield loss is crucial. However, with the increasing complexity of integrated circuits, traditional analysis techniques are prone to introducing biases [1], expanding the range of test parameters and exacerbating the interrelations among them. As a solution, AI-based analysis methods are gaining attention. For instance, a similarity-based feature reduction method deployed

in [13] effectively narrowed the range of root causes and achieved further analysis through the integration of domain knowledge. Additionally, the importance of model features has been explored for yield failure analysis [13]. Deep learningbased fault analysis methods have also been proposed. In [10], process sequence data were modularized, and attention weights for each module were calculated to identify the root causes behind yield drops. In [15], the receptive fields of the first convolutional layer of a CNN architecture were utilized to denote the importance of each variable. However, compared to traditional machine learning approaches, methods based on attention weights and CNN require extensive process data, leading to increased costs and more extended training periods. Moreover, a significant amount of research focuses on wafer defect and fault pattern analysis, aiming to localize chip failures and identify failure root causes [16]–[19]. To overcome the limitations of the methods above, this study employs the SHAP model to determine significant factors affecting yield

## III. Y IELD  P REDICTION AND  A NALYSIS  W ITH I MBALANCED  D ATASET  F RAMEWORK 

The yield analysis framework we proposed is shown in Fig. 1. In our framework, we must first collect WAT data from the semiconductor factory. WAT test data is a basic test process for controlling the manufacturing process of the semiconductor factory. We can use WAT to determine whether the process is stable, improve product output and control production costs [20]. At the same time, we are collecting CP testing data. CP testing refers to functional testing of each die on the wafer to verify its function [1]. Whether it is perfect or not. Since we aim to find the reasons for the decline in yield, we can convert the yield prediction problem into a binary classification problem, i.e., high or low yield. After data collection, the first step is to perform data cleaning, which includes data normalization, handling outliers, and addressing missing values. Outlier detection and missing value handling are conducted in this study using the median imputation method for outliers and missing values. Subsequently, we use min-max normalization to process data. Then, the dataset is divided into two subsets: a training set and a validation set, with proportions of   $70\%$  and   $30\%$  , respectively. Next, the GAN technique is utilized to oversample the minority dataset, generating a balanced dataset. The generated dataset is used for model training. Finally, we use SHAP to interpret the model and find the root cause.

They are commonly used for data augmentation and operate as unsupervised learning models capable of learning the distribution patterns within a dataset. GAN typically consist of two models: a generator model that aims to generate data similar to the distribution of original data and a discriminator model that attempts to distinguish between original and synthetic samples. This study employs GAN for data augmentation on the WAT dataset, which consists of tabular data with multiple features. Traditional oversampling methods fail to preserve the same distribution patterns as the original dataset, as exemplified by SMOTE [21]. Consequently, generating data for high-dimensional datasets poses a significant challenge. It is tough to generate new data while maintaining the distribution patterns of the dataset. As an emerging and promising data synthesis approach, GAN addresses these challenges.

This paper defines an imbalanced dataset, denoted as D, consisting of N rows with numerical columns D1....Dn. S1 represents wafers of high quality, while S0 represents wafers of low quality   $_\mathrm{(S1+S0=N)}$  ). When  $S1\!\ll\!S0$  , the dataset is considered imbalanced, indicating a significantly smaller quantity of low-quality wafers than high-quality wafers. However, in specific scenarios, such as during the research and development phase, low-quality wafers may exceed the quantity of high-quality samples by a considerable margin. Our objective is to address this imbalance through data augmentation using GAN models, aiming to achieve approximate equality between S1 and S0, i.e., a comparable number of samples for both categories while ensuring that the generated samples closely resemble the distribution of the original dataset.

After preprocessing the original dataset, which involves normalization, outlier handling, and missing value treatment, the data is divided into training and validation sets in a ratio of   $70\%$   and  $30\%$  , respectively. The training set is then inputted into the GAN for training. The GAN model is trained using backpropagation, where the discriminator aims to minimize prediction errors while the generator strives to maximize the discriminator’s error. In the GAN framework, the loss function is defined as follows:

(1)

$$\begin{array}{c}{{\underset{G}{\operatorname*{min}}\operatorname*{max}_{D}V(D,G)=E_{x\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}(x)[\log D(x)]+}}\\ {{E_{z\sim p_{z}}[\log(1-D(G(z)))]}}\end{array}$$
$G$   and    $D$   represent the generator and discriminator, respectively.    $p_{d a t a}$  represents the distribution of real data,    $p_{z}$  represents the prior distribution of input noise (In this work, we use a Gaussian distribution to fit the prior distribution of the data),    $G(z)$   represents the spatial mapping of the generator for generating data, and    $D(z)$   represents the probability that the data originates from the generator rather than real data. The discriminator is trained by maximizing the correct classification of real and generated data, while the generator is trained by minimizing    $l o g(1-D(G(z)))$  .

Vanilla GAN, though effective, often suffer from a phenomenon known as mode collapse, where the generator outputs a limited array of repetitively similar samples that the discriminator struggles to categorize as artificial. The Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) was developed to address this issue by substituting the Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) with the Wasserstein-1 distance in the loss function [22]. Despite this improvement, WGAN is still prey to the issue of vanishing gradients. This is demonstrated in Fig. 3(b). The WGANGP delivers a solution to this issue by implementing gradient penalty as an alternative to weight clipping [23]. Leveraging the differentiability of 1-Lipschitz functions, the

gradients in WGANGP are constrained to have a norm no greater than one at all points. This enables the enforcement of a Lipschitz restriction on the discriminator. Thus, the objective of WGANGP can be expressed in the following manner:

$$\begin{array}{r l r}&{}&{\underset{G}{\operatorname*{min}}\underset{D}{\operatorname*{max}}V(D,G)=E_{x\sim p_{d a t a}(x)}[\mathrm{Disc}(x)]-E_{z\sim p_{Z}}}\\ &{}&{\left[\mathrm{Disc}(\mathrm{gen}(z))-\lambda E_{x\sim p x}\left[\left(\|\nabla_{x}\,\mathrm{Disc}(x)\|_{2}-1\right)^{2}\right]\right]}\end{array}$$
(2)

The penalization coefficient,    $\lambda$  , is utilized in WGANGP to guide the generator towards the data distribution by employing a regularization term with a gradient penalty norm smaller than 1. This leads to smooth gradients in the discriminator. Another variant of GAN is CTGAN, which differs from regular GAN and WGAN in its treatment of non-Gaussian and multi-modal distributions in column data. Instead of using min-max normalization, CTGAN employs specific patternbased normalization. The structural diagram of CTGAN can be seen in Fig. 3(c). Specifically, CTGAN predicts the number of patterns using a variant Gaussian model and fits them into a Gaussian mixture. For continuous features, each value in the feature is encoded as a one-hot vector indicating the pattern and a scalar representing the normalized value of that pattern. CTGAN also adopts the loss function used in WGANGP for network training and incorporates two hidden layers of fully connected networks in both the generator and discriminator.

## B. SHAP 

SHAP is an explanation method based on cooperative game theory that calculates the optimal Shapley values to interpret individual predictions [24]. SHAP values can be employed for both global and local explanations of the model. Larger SHAP values indicate a greater impact of the corresponding feature. The mapping function   $\mathbf{x}\;=\;\mathbf{hx}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})$   is used to map the transformed input x’ back to the original input x, thus providing an explanation of the prediction. The explanation of SHAP is as follows:

$$\mathrm{g}\left(\mathrm{z}^{\prime}\right)=\phi_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\phi_{n}z_{n}^{\prime}$$
(3)

Where    $g$   is the explanatory model,    $N$   is the number of input features,    $n\:=\:1,2,.\,.\,.\,,N$   is the feature index,    $\phi_{n}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}$  bution Shapley value of the respective feature,  Z  $Z^{\prime}\in$    ∈  $\{0,1\}^{N}$  {  }    is the feature vector.

Utilizing interpretable machine learning approaches enhances the persuasiveness of models and enables the observation of indicators behind their decision-making process, thereby facilitating root cause analysis and providing guidance for yield improvement to engineers.

## IV. E XPERIMENT AND  A NALYSIS 

Aiming to substantiate the efficacy of the proposed model, we procured authentic WAT data and CP yield from semiconductor manufacturing sites. These datasets are characterized by an imbalanced distribution, predominantly comprising lowyield wafers. In more precise terms, we collected data from

1656 wafers, with the training set including 176 high-yield datasets and 983 low-yield datasets. The ratio of majority and minority datasets stands at approximately 5.6, which is considered imbalanced. Empirical results corroborate the effectiveness of our methodology in yield prediction.

## A. Oversampling of Minority samples 

In this segment, a comparative analysis of different oversampling algorithms and their subsequent model performances is conducted on two disparate datasets. Notably, various methodologies, including GAN, CTGAN, WGANGP, SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE, Random Over-Sampling (RandomOS), Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Approach (ADASYN), and Support Vector Machine-SMOTE (SVM-SMOTE) were implemented on each dataset. The results of these implementations are comprehensively illustrated in Table 1. The AUC score was selected as the key metric for judging the efficiency of the employed feature selection methods throughout the experimentation. The selection rationale stems from the inherent limitation of accuracy metrics that cannot effectively represent model performance for imbalanced datasets. On the contrary, AUC is considerably less influenced by the dataset’s balance, offering a holistic assessment of the model’s performance [3]. It computes the area beneath the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve by tracing the actual positive rate on the Y-axis against the false positive rate on the X-axis. This calculation signifies the probability of a model correctly classifying a randomly selected positive sample higher than a randomly selected negative sample.

In addition, we have chosen several other metrics to measure model performance, including accuracy, recall rate, and F1 score. The definitions of all these indicators are shown in Table I (True Positives (TP) denote instances where the model correctly predicted the positive class, False Negatives (FN) refer to instances the model incorrectly predicted as the negative class. At the same time, they are positive, and False Positives (FP) illustrate instances the model incorrectly identified as the positive class when they are negative.).

## B. SHAP 

Within the models above, given that the combination of CTGAN and CatBoost has the highest F1 and AUC scores, we have opted to employ the SHAP model to provide an interpretative analysis of this framework. The SHAP value represents the degree of influence of the variable on the model. As shown in Fig. 3, A positive SHAP value indicates a positive effect of the variable on yield, a negative SHAP value indicates a negative effect on yield, and blue and red indicate the size of the variable’s value.

With such an intuitive graph, we can identify those variables primarily impacting how the model makes yield rate judgements. Moreover, we can also demonstrate exactly how these variables impact yield, showing the correlation between the variables’ magnitude and their effects on yield.
