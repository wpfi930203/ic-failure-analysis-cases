## Trench Oxide Interface States & BTI Reliability      in IGBT Device  

Zhi Lin Sim 1,2 , Chun Wei Khor 2 , Yuan Gao 2 , David EH Goh 2 , Voon Cheng Ngwan 2  1  School of Materials Science and Engineering, Nanyang Technological University, 50 Nanyang Aveune, Singapore 2  Technology Development and Introduction, STMicroelectronics, 28 Ang Mo Kio Industrial Park 2, Singapore  Phone :   $(+65)$   6427 7548, Email:  eng-hui.goh@st.com

Abstract — For trench-gated power devices, the interface  between gate oxide and silicon substrate in the trench module is  one of the key contributors to long-term device reliability  performance, and this has become increasingly important when  devices are scaled down to reduce power loss.  Until now, there  has been little reported work on trench oxide interface states  and their impact on reliability performance. In this work, we  have successfully characterized trench oxide interface states  such as the quantification of interface trap density as well as  reliability BTI study. A physical model was proposed to explain  the BTI test and its impact on the entire trench module,  including the sidewall and bottom of the trench.

Keywords— IGBT, trench, interface states, oxide traps, BTI

Therefore, it is critical to understand the interface state  property and the impact on BTI degradation to achieve highperformance trench-gate IGBT with long-term reliability  guaranteed. In this work reported herein, the interface  properties   and   reliability   are   characterized   through  capacitance-voltage (C-V) measurements and BTI tests  respectively.

Among all the power semiconductor devices, an Insulated  Gate Bipolar Transistor (IGBT) is widely employed  particularly in low to medium-frequency applications thanks  to its intrinsically low conduction loss and fast switching  speed. In recent years, the IGBT market has experienced  continuous expansion mainly driven by the booming electric  vehicle demands [1].

The trend of IGBT technological development has already  brought Trench Field-Stop (FS) to the focus of interest, as it  has replaced the early generation of planar Punch-Through  IGBT due to their superior performance in terms of lower  conduction loss, higher current density, and smaller die size  [2]. Besides the better electrical performance, due to the highpowered plasma etch process, the trench module naturally  introduces higher interface states and border traps compared  to its planar counterpart, which could degrade the long-term  reliability like bias temperature instability (BTI). The impact  of trench oxide interface states can be amplified as IGBT is  further scaled to reduce power loss [3].

Typically, in terms of functionality of circuit design,  IGBTs are turned on when the positive gate voltage exceeds  its threshold voltage (V th ). In contrast, 0V is applied to the gate  to turn off the IGBT. However, the fast-rising voltage across  the drain and source can induce a voltage at the gate input,  potentially triggering false self-turn-on events [4,5]. A  practical suppression method is introducing a negative gate  voltage during turn-off. Building on these as shown in Fig. 1  and 2, both positive and negative gate stress were applied to  assess BTI degradation on the gate oxide of the trench. A  negative gate bias stress is applied, and a negative shift in  $\mathrm{V_{th}}$  is observed which if large enough, can lead to a significant  increase in collector-to-emitter leakage current (I ces ) in the  ‘OFF’ state. Conversely, subjecting to high positive gate bias  stress causes  $\mathrm{V_{th}}$   to increase and in turn bring about an increase  in collector-emitter saturation voltage (V cesat ) in the ‘ON’  state.

## II.   E XPERIMENTAL 

The trench-gate FS IGBT Device Under Tests (DUTs)  were fabricated with  $5.0\upmu\mathrm{m}$   trench depth,  $4.0\upmu\mathrm{m}$   trench pitch,  and   $1100\mathrm{\AA}$   gate oxide. The n-type drift was lightly doped  with   $1.5\mathrm{x}10^{14}$    atom/cm 3 . Prior to gate oxide growth, a thin  layer of sacrificial oxide was grown and removed to ensure  the quality of gate oxide interface. Finally, after metal  deposition, forming gas annealing was applied to passivate

the interface states. The fresh samples were annealed at the  same temperature and time with   $5\%$   and   $15\%$   H 2  content.  Subsequently, C-V measurements and BTI reliability tests  were conducted.

The evaluation of the interface traps started from highfrequency and low-frequency C-V measurements at  $150^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   by  sweeping from inversion to accumulation as a function of  gate voltage (Vg). The measurement was operated under  alternating current (AC) with a small amplitude level of 20  mV. Subsequently, the density of interface traps was  extracted based on the low-high frequency capacitancevoltage method [6].

For the BTI reliability assessment of trench gate oxide,  the BTI measurement cycle is as shown in Fig. 3. V th  was  defined using the constant current method when reaches  1mA. First,  $\mathrm{V_{th}}$   was measured before and after different NBTI  or PBTI stress voltages at   $175^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   for 10000s. Second, a 0V  was applied for 10000s followed by a reverse polarity  recovery of   $\pm5\mathrm{V}$   for the same duration. Lastly, a postrecovery   $\mathrm{V_{th}}$   was measured again. The transition time from  measurement to stress was fully automated by the test  program.

The impact of forming gas annealing on the interface  traps is depicted in Fig. 4 where the interface trap density   $\left(\mathrm{{Dit}\right)}$    for   $5\%$   and   $15\%$   H 2  is compared. Using the low-high  frequency method, as shown in (1), the   $\mathrm{Dit}$   was calculated,

$$\begin{array}{r}{D_{i t}=\left.\frac{C_{o x}}{q}\right.(\frac{C_{l f}\big/_{C_{o x}}}{1-\left.C_{l f}\right/_{C_{o x}}}-\left.\frac{C_{h f}\big/_{C_{o x}}}{1-\left.C_{h f}\right/_{C_{o x}}})}\end{array}$$
where   $\mathrm{Dit}$   is the interface trap density   $\mathrm{(cm^{\cdot2}e V^{\cdot1})}$  ,   $\mathrm{C_{ox}}$   is the  oxide capacitance per unit area  $\mathrm{(F/cm}^{\dot{2}})$  , q is the magnitude of  electron charge, C lf  is low-frequency capacitance (F) and  $\mathrm{Chf}$    is high-frequency capacitance (F).

The average D it  has reduced by  ${\sim}40\%$   when   $\mathrm{H}_{2}$   content in  the alloy is increased from   $5\%$  to   $15\%$  . This aligns with our  previous work [7] as higher hydrogen concentration can  intrinsically passivate more interface traps in Si-based  devices.

## IV.      D ISCUSSION 

C-V result in Fig. 4 shows that   $15\%$   H 2  has lower D it   compared to 5% H 2 , i.e.   $15\%$   H 2  annealing intrinsically  passivates more interface states. Moreover, it is worth noting

that the increase in negative   $\mathrm{Vg}$   translates to higher D it  due to  more charges responding to the measurement. At -5V, the  overall   $\mathrm{D_{it}}$   value is at   $10^{12}$   order while in comparison, the   $\mathrm{D_{it}}$    value for the planar Si-SiO 2  interface is at   $10^{1\bar{0}}$   order [8]. The   ${\sim}2.$  -order higher   $\mathrm{Dit}$  for trench-gated power devices could be  related to the plasma etch process that produces a significant  amount of dangling bonds at the trench surfaces. This effect  can be significant when gate oxide thickness is scaled further  to turn on at a much lower gate voltage or logic level (5V)  [3].

Our results show that in PBTI, the degradation is more  pronounced at lower hydrogen concentrations   $(5\%\;\mathrm{H}_{2})$  ), and  in NBTI, the degradation is more pronounced at higher  hydrogen concentrations   $(15\%~\mathrm{H}_{2})$  . From these results, we  believe that this phenomenon is more related to the hydrogen  passivation. Higher concentrations of hydrogen initially  passivate more interface traps and these interface traps are  reactivated under NBTI stress, leading to a larger shift in   $\Delta\mathrm{V_{th}}$  . It is consistent with the understanding that NBTI  degradation is related to hydrogen species release via an  electrochemical reaction from Si-H bonds at the   $\mathrm{Si}/\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$    interface [9]. In contrast, the degradation is less pronounced  at higher hydrogen concentrations as PBTI degradation is  attributed to electron trapping instead of interface state  generation.

As the trench in the IGBT device usually extends beyond  the body region and ends in the drain drift region, applying  positive or negative gate bias on the device would have  different implications. To better explain the BTI mechanism  in trench IGBT, as shown in Fig. 9(a), PBTI can be modeled  as positive gate bias stressing the trench sidewalls while in  Fig. 9(b) NBTI will be responsible for stressing at the bottom  of the trench. During the PBTI stress, charge buildup due to  the inversion of electrons forming an n-channel in the IGBT  while in NBTI, the bottom of the trench is stressed since there  is an inversion of holes in the drain drift region that forms a  parasitic p-channel MOSFET. In Fig. 8,   $\Delta\mathrm{V_{th}}$  is more  pronounced at higher hydrogen concentrations, i.e. 15% H 2  after NBTI stress. This is due to the dissociation of the Si-H  bond in the oxide-silicon interface and more hydrogen is  released into the trench bottom oxide and trapped therein  [10].

In summary, when the IGBT is stressed with positive gate  voltage, the electrons are injected from the inverted channel  and trapped in the trench sidewall oxide. The positive shift in   $\mathrm{V_{th}}$   indicates the electron traps in interface states or nearinterface broader traps [11]. As more charges build up, the  shift in   $\mathrm{V_{th}}$   increases with increasing stress voltage.  Conversely, for NBTI stress with higher stress bias level  causing a higher magnitude of  $\mathrm{V_{th}}$   drift due to the dissociation  of Si-H bond in the trench bottom oxide, leading to  regeneration of interface traps [12]. It reflects as   $\Delta\mathrm{V_{th}}$  increases with increasing stress voltage after both PBTI and  NBTI stress.

## V.   C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, the trench-gate IGBT interface properties  were characterized through C-V measurements and BTI  reliability tests. The density of interface states at trench oxide  IGBT was quantified through the low-high frequency  method.  $15\%\,\mathrm{H}_{2}/\mathrm{N}_{2}$   annealing has been shown to effectively  passivate the interface traps, achieving a   $40\%$   reduction in  average   $\mathrm{D_{it}}$   values. Meanwhile, the comprehensive BTI  impact was assessed for the trench module, including the  sidewall and bottom of the trench. The physical mechanism  for PBTI and NBTI degradation for IGBT devices was  proposed. PBTI degradation is attributed to electron traps in  interface states or near-interface broader traps at the trench  sidewalls while NBTI degradation involves Si-H bond  breaking and hydrogen release at the trench oxide bottom.
