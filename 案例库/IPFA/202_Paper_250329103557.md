## Unsupervised Domain Adaptation with Pseudo Shape Supervision for IC Image Segmentation 

Yee-Yang Tee, Xuenong Hong, Deruo Cheng, Tong Lin, Yiqiong Shi, Bah-Hwee Gwee School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, Nanyang Technological University, 50 Nanyang Ave, Singapore Email:  { yeeyang.tee, xuenong001, deruo.cheng, lintong, yqshi, ebhgwee } @ntu.edu.sg

Abstract —Deep learning (DL) techniques have achieved excellent results for IC image segmentation, a critical task in hardware assurance, but they require a large amount of labeled training data to perform well. Due to the domain shift problem of DL techniques, the data collection and data labeling process has to be repeated on new IC image datasets which is extremely timeconsuming. Domain adaptation is a promising approach that aims to tackle the domain shift problem by implementing models that can be trained on an existing source dataset and then applied to a new target dataset effectively. However, the reported domain adaptation techniques do not fully utilize the unlabeled images for training or are prone to model collapse when training on unlabeled images. To address these challenges, we propose pseudo shape supervision (PSS), a domain adaptation framework for IC image segmentation that effectively leverages unlabeled target images for training whilst avoiding model collapse. Within our PSS, we propose a novel shape consistency loss for supervision on unlabeled images, by utilizing weak pseudo-labels that are generated by thresholding. Cross domain mixing is performed between the unlabeled target images and the synthetic images to reduce the domain gap. Our experimental results demonstrate that our proposed PSS outperforms the reported techniques on IC image datasets and our ablation studies show the importance of our novel shape consistency loss.

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence and global digitization trends, the demand for integrated circuits (ICs), which are the fundamental unit of digital computation, has grown massively in recent years. ICs are critical for areas such as the finance, military, and infrastructure sectors, therefore, it is of utmost importance to ensure the security and functionality of ICs. Hardware assurance via circuit extraction is one of the most reliable approaches for this task [1], [2]. The circuit extraction process involves package removal, sample preparation, microscopic imaging, circuit annotation, netlist recovery and circuit analysis [3], [4].

Circuit annotation is a critical step in the circuit extraction process as it identifies the locations of the metal lines and vias in the IC images, which can then be converted into a netlist. Typically, circuit annotation is performed as a pixelwise image segmentation task, as demonstrated in Fig. 1, whereby white and black pixels in the segmentation result represent the metal line and background classes respectively.

Recently, deep learning (DL) techniques have become the defacto standard for IC image segmentation due to their superior accuracy as compared to conventional techniques such as image processing and machine learning [2], [5]–[7]. However, fully supervised DL techniques are highly data dependent and require large amounts of labeled training data, which can be costly to obtain. Furthermore, DL techniques usually suffer from the domain shift problem [8], [9] which necessitates the collection and labeling of data for all new IC devices that need to be analyzed. To illustrate the domain shift problem, Fig. 1 (a) depicts good segmentation performance of a DL model that was trained on IC images from a specific IC device, and applied to IC images from the same device. The same DL model nearly completely fails when it is applied to IC images from another IC device or layer due to differences in the image properties, as seen in Fig. 1 (b).

One highly promising approach to tackle the data dependency and domain shift problem in IC image segmentation is domain adaptation [9], [10]. It involves leveraging information from existing labeled datasets (denoted as the source dataset)

In domain adaptation techniques, the target images do not necessarily need to be labeled, which can lead to significant cost savings. Tee et al. [9] proposed a domain adaptation technique for IC image segmentation whereby a labeled dataset of source images, obtained from a previously analyzed IC device, was first translated into images similar to the target device using the CycleGAN [11] method. Subsequently, a segmentation network was trained on the translated images and then applied to the target device images for testing. However, the performance of existing methods is far from satisfactory due to the domain gap between the synthetic target images and the real target images. Furthermore, model collapse often occurs when training on unlabeled images, leading to trivial model outputs [12].

In this paper, we propose Pseudo Shape Supervision (PSS), a domain adaptation framework which combines unlabeled, target images and synthetic images for training of a segmentation network. Strong image level augmentations and feature level augmentations are applied to the unlabeled images and consistency losses are applied for the training of a segmentation network. Domain mixing is performed between the real and synthetic images to reduce the domain gap. Based on our domain knowledge of IC images, we introduced a novel shape consistency loss that provides a high level supervision for the pseudo labels generated based on the unlabeled IC images, which we demonstrate to prevent model collapse on the unlabeled images.

## II. M ETHODOLOGY 

The unsupervised domain adaptation task for IC image segmentation involves a labeled sour   $\mathcal{D}^{S}=\{X^{\bar{S}},Y^{\bar{S}}\}$   an unlabeled target datas   ${\mathcal{D}}^{T}~=~\{U^{O}\}$   {  }  for training.  $X^{S}$    denotes the source images,  $Y^{S}$    denotes the corresponding source masks, and    $U^{O}$    denotes the target images. Fig. 2 depicts an overview of our proposed PSS, a domain adaptation framework which consists of an image translation stage and a segmentation stage. In the image translation stage, synthetic images (that are similar in appearance to the target IC images) are generated with source masks as input. These synthetic images are subsequently combined with the real unlabeled target images for training in the segmentation stage. The following sections describe the image translation and segmentation stage in further detail.

## A. Image Translation 

We adopt the CycleGAN [11] framework for the image translation stage. Source masks are translated to synthetic target image   $({\bar{Y}}^{S}\,\rightarrow\,X^{T^{\prime}}$  ) where    $X^{T^{\prime}}$    denotes the synthetic target images. This preserves the critical information from

The image translation network is trained on the source masks and target images and then applied to the source masks to generate a set of synthetic target images    $X^{T^{\prime}}$  . The synthetic dataset is given by  $\dot{\mathcal{D}}^{T^{\prime}}=\{X^{T^{\prime}},Y^{S}\}$   and utilized in the segmentation stage.

## B. Image Segmentation 

In the segmentation stage, a segmentation network    $S$   is trained on the labeled sy  c dataset,    $\{X^{T^{\prime}},Y^{S}\}$  , and the unlabeled target images  {  $\{U^{O}\}$  } . Firstly, the synthetic images and masks are utilized for supervised segmentation training with the cross entropy loss, as given by:

$$L_{s u p}=C E(S(X^{T^{\prime}}),Y^{S}))$$
(1)

where    $\mathit{C E}$   denotes the well-established cross entropy loss for segmentation [15], and    $S(X^{T^{\prime}})$   denotes the output probabilities of the segmentation network. For unlabeled image training,  $S$   is applied to the unlabeled images,  $U^{O}$  , to obtain pseudo labels    $P^{O}$    and the high confidence pixels are retained for subsequent training.    $P^{O}$    is utilized to supervise the outputs from two training branches, namely the  strong  branch indicated in red on Fig. 2 and the  feature  branch indicated in purple on Fig. 2. In the  strong  branch, image augmentations such as random contrast adjustments, random brightness adjustments, and Gaussian blurring [16], [17] are applied to    $U^{O}$    giving  $U^{S}$  . To close the domain gap between synthetic images and unlabeled images, we perform cross domain mixing [18] using the  CutMix  [19] technique. The  CutMix  operation is performed by cropping a random region from    $X^{T^{\prime}}$    and pasting it onto  $U^{S}$    and the corresponding mask region is cropped from the source mask    $Y^{S}$    and pasted onto    $P^{\bar{O}}$  . The strong consistency loss,    $L_{s t r o n g}$   is given by:

$$L_{s t r o n g}=C E(S(C u t M i x(U^{S},X^{T^{\prime}})),C u t M i x(P^{O},Y^{S}))$$
In the  feature  branch, we adopt the feature perturbation step from [22] and apply a dropout with a probability of  $50\%$   at the intermediate features after the bottleneck layer. The segmentation network outputs from the perturbed features are denoted as    $P^{F}$  . The feature consistency loss,    $L_{f e a t}$   is computed as the cross entropy loss between the pseudo label  $P^{O}$    and  $P^{F}$  .

With just the consistency regularization losses    $L_{f e a t}$   and  $L_{s t r o n g}$  , there is a significant possibility of model collapse

whereby the segmentation network could gradually produce trivial outputs, such as predicting the background class for all pixels [12]. We propose a shape consistency loss that provides a high-level supervision for    $P^{O}$  . Based on our domain knowledge of IC images, weak pseudo labels    $Y^{w e a k}$  for IC images can be obtained with Otsu thresholding [7], [23]. The shape consistency loss is then given by:

$$L_{s h a p e}=C E(P^{O},Y^{w e a k})$$
(3)

The overall loss for the segmentation network is given by:

$$L_{S}=L_{s u p}+L_{s t r o n g}+L_{f e a t}+\lambda_{s h a p e}*L_{s h a p e}$$
(4)

where    $\lambda_{s h a p e}$   is a weighting factor that controls the importance of the shape consistency loss.

## III. E XPERIMENTAL  R ESULTS 

## A. Dataset 

Three datasets of IC images obtained via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) from different IC devices or different layers were utilized in our experiments. Following the standard practice of unsupervised domain adaptation [9], [10], we have a source dataset for training and target dataset for evaluation. Device-1, Metal 2 is designated as the source dataset and there are 1,000 images and masks for training. Device-1, Metal 3 and Device-2, Metal 6 are designated as target datasets for evaluation. For each target dataset, 2,000 images were randomly split into training and testing sets with a 50:50 ratio, resulting in 1,000 training images and 1,000 testing images. ## D. Ablation Study 

In order to understand the importance of the various components including loss terms and domain mixing, we performed an ablation study by removing one component at a time from our proposed PSS. The models were evaluated in terms of the number of circuit connection errors and the results are presented in Table III. It can be observed that the best IC segmentation performance is achieved when applying all the components in our proposed PSS. We also note that the removal of  $L_{s h a p e}$   leads to a significant decrease in performance. Upon further inspection of the prediction results from the model without  $L_{s h a p e}$  , nearly all pixels in the IC image were

predicted as the metal line class, indicating a model collapse issue which leads to multiple short circuit errors.

## IV. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we have proposed PSS for unsupervised domain adaptation. Our proposed PSS incorporates domain mixing of unlabeled target images and synthetic images to close the domain gap, and introduces a novel shape consistency loss for a high-level supervision of the unlabeled training branch. Our experimental comparisons have demonstrated the superior performance of our PSS for IC image segmentation as compared to the reported techniques. The effectiveness of our novel shape consistency loss was also demonstrated in our ablation studies.
