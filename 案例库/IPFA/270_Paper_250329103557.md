## The effects of localized rework on microstructural  evolution of interfacial IMC in mirrored BGA  electronic packaging  

May Shin Chang 1 , Mei Ling Low 1 , Bee Chin Loke 1  1 Department of Failure Analysis Engineering, Western Digital (SanDisk Storage Malaysia Sdn. Bhd.),   Plot 301A, Persiaran Cassia Selatan 1, 14110 Simpang Ampat, Penang, Malaysia   Email :  may.shin.chang@wdc.com

Abstract —The hot air rework soldering process is a wellestablished practice in PCB assembly for rectifying solder  interconnections and minimizing defects. However, there  remains a limited understanding of the microstructural  evolution occurring in reworked, adjacent, and mirror-side  components of a mirrored BGA electronic packaging. This  paper aims to address this gap by analyzing the microstructural  evolution of interfacial intermetallic compounds (IMCs) in these  components during the hot air rework soldering, employing  scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The findings reveal that  the localized rework process caused grain coarsening in the  reworked   component   and   its   adjacent   and   mirrored  components. This phenomenon is attributed to an increase in Cu  concentration in the solder melt, promoting larger Cu 6 Sn 5  IMC  grain growth. Interestingly, the assessment of Cu 6 Sn 5  grain size  in the mirrored components of the PCB reveals larger grains  compared to the reworked side. This discrepancy can be  attributed to the slower cooling rate experienced by the  mirrored components during localized reflow, leading to grain  boundary diffusion of Cu 6 Sn 5  grains, as evidenced by observed  necking and grain coalescence.

Keywords— Cu 6 Sn 5  interfacial IMC grain, localized rework,  solder separation

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

In the landscape of electronics packaging technology,  where optimizing performance and space efficiency in  portable electronic devices are necessities, mirrored ball grid  array (BGA) design module configurations stand out as a  pivotal technique [1]. This approach enables the attainment of  the high density and functionality demanded by advanced  electronic products. However, the intricacies of the  manufacturing process, coupled with variations in component  sizes, can occasionally result in defects in solder joints during  high-volume assembly reflow processes. As a result,  implementing a hot air localized rework process becomes  essential. This process effectively rectifies defects in the  mirrored BGA design module configuration while minimizing  damage to nearby components and reducing yield loss and  scrap rates [2]. This significantly maximizes cost efficiency.  Addressing these issues is crucial to ensuring proper  soldering, reliable joints, and reducing defect, thereby  sustaining the overall quality of solder joints connecting  components to the printed circuit board (PCB) of electronic  products.

Recognizing the importance of the rework process for  repair and cost reduction, there remains a knowledge gap  regarding the microstructural evolution within solder joints in  mirrored BGA components on copper (Cu) substrate PCBs.  The use of lead-free SAC solders with higher melting  temperature   $(217\,^{\circ}\mathbf{C})$  , result in increased heat transfer through

PCBs and exposes adjacent and mirrored components to  thermal stress, often causing secondary reflow of mirrored  BGA solder joints, especially in thin PCBs. This elevated  temperature during rework also triggers an interfacial reaction  between the solder and substrate, resulting in Cu diffusion and  altering the composition and microstructure of the solder joint,  including the   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   intermetallic compound (IMC) layer,  affecting its mechanical properties [3]. These changes  significantly impact solder joint reliability and quality, with  additional concerns arising from the formation of brittle   $\mathrm{Cu_{6}S n_{5}I M C s}$  . The resultant microstructure plays a crucial role  in the long-term reliability performance of the solder joints  after the rework process, emphasizing the need for effective  management of heat distribution during localized rework.  However, variations in temperature gradients among  components presents a significant challenge, complicating  reliability considerations and potentially causing warpage  issues in the adjacent and mirrored components [4].

Therefore, this paper aims to enhance the understanding of  localized rework processes involving components and their  adjacent and mirrored components on a PCB. Specifically, it  focuses on understanding the morphology of the interfacial   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   IMC changes in the solder joint of the reworked BGA  component substrate and its adjacent and mirrored  components, particularly those associated with SAC305 BGA  components on Cu substrates package and PCB. The  investigation includes measuring the grain size. By  elucidating these analyses, the aim is to provide insights into  the evolution of the IMC layer under hot air localized reflow  conditions.

## II.   M ETHODOLOGY 

In the present work, the printed circuit board (PCB) used  was a double-sided assembly. The components employed was  a fine-pitch ball grid array (FBGA) Samsung 16GB DDR4  SDRAM package, attached with SAC305 solder balls, as  detailed in Table I and Fig. 1. Both PCB and package were  featuring copper (Cu) with an organic solderability  preservatives (OSP) surface finish substrate. During the  assembling of mirrored BGA design module configurations,  the components were placed on the PCB and underwent  reflowed. The module was underwent two reflow cycles to  surface mount multiple packages on both sides of the PCB.

The components were then subjected to ex-situ  microstructural observation to analyze the localized rework  effect to the microstructural evolution of the interfacial IMC  on the reworked component and its adjacent and mirrored  components as indicated in Fig. 2(a-f). Prior to microstructural  observation, each component sample were carefully removed  from the PCB, then underwent ultrasonic clean in an acetone  bath, followed by deep etching to remove the bulk solder and  expose the microstructure of the IMC layer. Microstructural  analysis was conducted at the top-view of the IMC using a  scanning electron microscope (SEM; model: Hitachi  SU3500). Multiple locations within the interfacial IMC layer  were examined, and the IMC grain size was measured  accordingly.

Moreover, the reworked component exhibited a distinct  Cu 6 Sn 5  morphology   with Ag 3 Sn precipitation, differing from  its adjacent and mirrored components. The primary solder  joint of the reworked component displayed an elongated  scalloped   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   morphology with a higher area fraction of   $\mathrm{Ag}_{3}\mathrm{Sn}$   precipitated on the   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   surface, as shown in Fig.  5(b), while adjacent components exhibited a more rounded  scalloped shape with a much lower area fraction of   $\mathrm{Ag}_{3}\mathrm{Sn}$  , as  shown in Fig. 5(a, c). This difference was attributed to the  attachment of the primary component during the first reflow  cycle of rework, while adjacent and mirrored components  underwent two additional reflow cycles during faulty  component removal and new component attachment.

Temperature readings from thermocouple wires indicated  that the adjacent and mirrored components and their solder  joints were exposed to high temperatures during faulty  component removal and subsequent reflow soldering of the  new component replacement, exceeding   $230^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  , temperatures

above the liquidus temperature of SAC305 solder. The  additional two reflow cycles subjected adjacent and mirrored  components to elevated temperature, leading to the dissolution  of   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   grain in the solder melt, accelerated reaction  kinetics, and promoted Cu diffusion from the component and  board substrates into the molten solder. Consequently, this  resulted in higher Cu saturation in the adjacent and mirrored  component solder joint due to increased Cu diffusion from the  Cu substrate, resulting in diffusion-driven growth of the   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   grain. Hence, a more rounded scalloped  $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   grain  morphology was observed on the component substrate of the  adjacent and mirrored components during re-solidification of  the solder melt during cooling. Furthermore, a much lower  area fraction of   $\mathrm{Ag}_{3}\mathrm{Sn}$   was observed in the adjacent and  mirrored components' solder joint, postulated to be due to  particles dissolution in the solder melt during re-melting and  potentially formed in the bulk solder during the resolidification process. A similar observation was made in Ref  [5], where varying size and distribution of   $\mathrm{Ag}_{3}\mathrm{Sn}$   precipitates  on the  $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   interface in SAC/Cu solder joints during the   $1^{\mathrm{st}}$    and  $4^{\mathrm{th}}$   reflow cycles.

The microstructure  evolution in the solder joint of the reworked component, and  their adjacent and mirrored components on a PCB can be  attributed by the different composition of the solder, where Cu  dissolution from the Cu substrate in the solder melt during the  rework process altered the melting and solidification  temperature of the solder melts of adjacent and mirrored  components   during   subsequent   reflow   cycles.   The  solidification sequence at the  $\mathrm{SAC}305/\mathrm{Cu}$   interface begins as  the molten solder cools, initiating the   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   heterogeneous  grain nucleation in the oversaturated SAC305 solder melt on  the Cu substrate. Growth proceeds through a ripening process,  leading to the formation of individual scalloped grains. This is  followed by the growth of   $\mathrm{Ag}_{3}\mathrm{Sn}$   and/or β-Sn phases.  Additionally, it is noteworthy that temperature readings from  thermocouples placed at the solder joints and components  indicated higher temperatures for mirrored components due to  bottom heating during the rework process and slower cooling  rate compared to the reworked side and same side adjacent  components. Therefore, the solidification process of the solder  joints in the mirrored components is a gradual phenomenon  after   the   reflow   rework   process,   not   occurring  instantaneously. The reworked module was allowed to cool on  the bottom heater by air-cooling upon the automatic shutdown  of the heater at the end of the rework process. This reduces the  heat transfer during the cooling process attributed to the  slower cooling rate experienced by these solder joints  compared to the reworked component side.

During the solidification of the solder joints in mirrored  components, the    $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   grains nucleate and grow,  progressing through a ripening process, resulting in scalloped  grain formation. With prolonged solidification time of the  solder joints due to the slower heat transfer, the grain  coarsening occurs as the effect of ripening reduces and grain  boundary diffusion becomes prominent. Hence, this promote  the grain boundary diffusion in the   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   grains. Previous  reports have also demonstrated that a slow cooling rate leads  to the formation of larger grain sizes [7, 8]. The mechanism of  grain boundary diffusion occurs during slow solidification  rate, causing neighboring grains come into contact, forming  necks or bridges, and facilitating coalescence with  neighboring grains. This process involves the diffusion of  atoms across grain boundaries, leading to a reduction in  overall grain boundary energy. Consequently, larger grains

grow at the expense of smaller ones, resulting in grain  coarsening and the potential consumption of smaller grains.  Therefore, the elevated temperature accelerates Cu diffusion,  while a slower cooling rate further emphasize grain  coarsening by promoting grain boundary diffusion of   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$  grains and causing the coalescence with neighboring grains in  the solder joints of mirrored components.

In comparison, the adjacent components on the reworked  side experienced faster air-cooling compared to the mirrored  component, inducing higher thermal stresses in the package.  This rapid contraction can cause differential cooling rates  across the package, resulting in uneven contraction and  potential warpage [6]. Therefore, these internal stresses within  the package potentially lead to the solder separation observed at the interface between the interfacial IMC and bulk solder at  the component side, as shown in Fig. 4, where the solder balls  (A9, N9) at the edge of component exhibit a higher area of  separation at the IMC/solder interface compared to the  intermediate solder ball (G9).

## IV.   C ONCLUSION 

In high-volume production, localized reflow rework plays  a crucial role in minimizing yield loss and scrap rates.  However, handling and adjusting parameters during the  rework process are important as they directly impact adjacent  and mirrored components. In present work, the morphology of  the   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$    intermetallic compound (IMC) during localized  reflow rework process on SAC305 BGA components with Cu  surfaces. Analysis revealed a significant  $76\%$   grain coarsening  in the   $\mathrm{Cu}_{6}\mathrm{Sn}_{5}$   of the reworked component and its adjacent and  mirrored components, attributed by the Cu diffusion from the  substrate of both component and PCB. The hot gas rework  process during component removal and attachment of a new  component is the most damaging step for the same-side  adjacent component. During component removal, high  temperature can initiate the temperature onset of the liquid  phase and lead to solder separation at the interface of IMC and  bulk solder in adjacent components to the reworked  components.  This separation results from thermal gradients,  causing warpage due to accelerated heat, leading to higher  thermal stresses across the package and potential warpage.  The bottom heater can reduce package warpage during rewor  process, but the slower cooling rate during the solidification  process on the bottom heater affects the kinetics of nucleation,  growth, and grain boundary migration, resulting in grain neck  formation and coalescence, hence coarser grain size in the  mirrored components. In summary, this finding highlights the  intricate interplay between solidification dynamics, cooling  rates, and grain evolution in Cu 6 Sn 5  solder joints.  Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for optimizing  rework reflow soldering processes and enhancing the  reliability of electronic assemblies.
