##     Investigation of the Reliability of Different HighResistance States in 1T1R Devices  

Yu-Hsuan Yeh 1 , Yung-Fang Tan 2 , Ting-Chang Chang 1  1   Department of Physics, National Sun Yat-Sen University, No.70 Lien-hai Road,   Kaohsiung, Taiwan  2   Department of Maerials and Optoelectronic Science, National Sun Yat-Sen University, No.70 Lien-hai Road, Kaohsiung, Taiwan  3  Department of Physics, the Center of Crystal Research, and also with College of Semiconductor and Advanced Technology Research,  National Sun Yat-sen University, Kaohsiung, 804, Taiwan  Phone : (886)0989-753-875, Email : melodyyeh0416@gmail.com

Abstract —This research introduces a novel method, distinct  from previous approaches, to achieve various high-resistance  states. When operating in a high-resistance state (HRS) in a  onetransistor-one-RRAM (1T1R) configuration, a significant  voltage drop occurs across the transistor. This phenomenon  makes it challenging to obtain diverse high-resistance states at  different cut-off voltages. The proposed method addresses this  issue, enabling the implementation of distinct high-resistance  states. However, certain reliability issues still need clarification  and further investigation, as discussed in the manuscript.

Keywords—1T1R, High resistance state, Reliability

## I.    I NTORDUCTION 

As wearable devices and smart home technologies become  integral parts of our daily lives, the demand for and emphasis  on embedded memory have been steadily increasing. [1,2]  With the shrinking of Moore's Law, traditional embedded  flash memory has struggled to meet these escalating demands.  [3,4] Consequently, many have shifted their focus towards  developing emerging memory technologies. Among the  various emerging memory technologies, Resistive random  access memory (RRAM), known for its cost-effectiveness and  superior performance, has emerged prominently and is now in  mass production. [5-7]

In practical applications, RRAM is often integrated with  a transistor a one-transistor-one-RRAM (1T1R) configuration,  with a single RRAM connected to the drain terminal of a  transistor. The on/off ratio of the RRAM memory window can  impact circuit design, affecting the ease of memory state  interpretation. Therefore, the adjustability of the memory  window size holds significant importance in circuit design.  Typically, in the operation of a single RRAM cell, the memory  window can be easily modulated by varying the reset voltage   $\mathrm{(V_{reset})}$  . [8,9] However, in the 1T1R configuration, the  involvement of the transistor entering the saturation region  results in channel pinching. This necessitates the use of  alternative operational methods to arbitrarily control the  memory window. In this manuscript, a detailed explanation of  the operational method is proposed. Furthermore, the physical  model to explain the degradation in reliability caused by larger  windows has been proposed. This provides a more in-depth  clarification of the operational methods and physical  mechanisms underlying 1T1R configurations.

## II.   E XPERIMENT AND  M ETHOD 

The experimental sample in this study integrates an  NMOS transistor and an   $\mathrm{HfO}_{2}$  -based RRAM as the 1T1R  device. The NMOS is fabricated using the standard   $28\ \mathrm{nm}$  CMOS process, and the RRAM with a cell size of   $150\!\times\!150$   $\mathrm{nm}^{2}$   is fabricated on the drain of the NMOS. The top and  bottom electrodes of the RRAM are respectively deposited as

TaN. The thickness of the switching layer is   $10\mathrm{nm}\,\,\mathrm{HfO}_{2}.$  ,  deposited through atomic layer deposition (ALD), as  illustrated in Fig. 1.

All electrical characteristics were measured using an  Agilent B1530 fast current–voltage (I–V) measurement  system, an Agilent B1500 semiconductor parameter analyzer,  and a Cascade Microtech M150 measurement platform.

## III.   R ESULT AND  D ISCUSSION 

Firstly, basic electrical characterization measurements  were conducted on a single RRAM device. Following a high  voltage forming process and under a 1 mA compliance current,  a stable memory window was obtained, as depicted by the  black line in Fig. 1(b). The set voltage and reset voltage were  2.5V and -2V, respectively. To achieve a larger on/off ratio,  the reset voltage can be increased to -3V, causing more  significant conductive filament retraction, as shown by the red  line in Fig. 1(b). Therefore, in the operation of a single RRAM  device, a larger memory window can be easily achieved by  increasing the reset voltage.

Subsequently, operating under the 1T1R structure, the  device's drain terminal was subjected to a positive bias of 1.2  V to form a conduction path for the filament. Then, a positive  bias of 1.7 V was applied to the source terminal to induce  filament retraction. After repeating this set/reset process 100  times, the resulting overlapping plot is shown by the black line  in Fig. 1(c). However, when increasing the source voltage  $(\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{s}})$  to   $1.9\,\mathrm{~V~}$   or even 2.1 V, a larger memory window was not  achieved, as indicated by the blue and red lines in Fig. 1(c).

To further confirm, the read voltage was set at 0.5 V, reading  the device's high-resistance state (HRS) and low-resistance  state (LRS), extracting the corresponding current values,  dividing by 0.5 V, and converting to the present resistance  values. These 100 data points were represented in a statistical  box plot as shown in Fig. 1(d), confirming that the window  did not increase with the increase in source voltage.

Due to the preparation of 1T1R devices, a pad called the  "pi-out terminal" has been pulled out for testing at the  connection point between the transistor and the RRAM.  During the reset process, with the drain terminal grounded, the  voltage at the pi-out terminal represents the cross voltage of  the RRAM. Therefore, this terminal can be utilized to confirm  how much voltage the pi-out terminal reads as the source  voltage increases. From the results in Fig. 2(a), it can be  observed that as the source voltage increases, the voltage read  by the pi-out terminal shows a saturation phenomenon. This is  due to the saturation of the transistor channel. When the  transistor enters to the saturation region, the pinch-off effect  causes a depletion region near the source-side channel, leading  to a voltage saturation at the pi-out terminal, as depicted in Fig.  2(b). Additionally, from Fig. 2(a), it can be seen that as the  gate voltage   $(\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{g}})$   increases from   $1.7~\mathrm{V}$   to  $2.3\;\mathrm{V}$  , the saturation  voltage at the pi-out terminal also rises. In other words, the  cross voltage of the RRAM is larger. Therefore, if there is a  desire to modulate the memory window in the 1T1R structure,  it can be achieved by altering the gate voltage. The results in  Fig. 2(c) indeed show that with an increase in   $\mathrm{V_{g}},$  , a higher  HRS can be obtained. Creating a statistical box plot, as shown  in Fig. 2(d), using the resistance values read at   $0.5~\mathrm{V}$  , reveals  that with an increase in  $\mathrm{V_{g}},$  , a higher HRS is achieved.

To further clarify the differences, a current conduction  mechanism fitting was applied to the HRS of the I-V curve.  The Schottky emission conduction mechanism was fitted  using the formula:

$$I=A A^{**}T^{2}\mathrm{exp}(\frac{-q(\varphi_{B}-\sqrt{q V/4\pi d\varepsilon_{i}}}{k T})$$
where  $\mathbf{A}^{**}$   is the Richardson constant,  $\mathbf{k}$   is Boltzmann constant,    $\varphi_{\mathrm{{B}}}$   is the Schottky barrier height,   $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{i}}$   is dielectric constant, and  d is the Schottky barrier thickness, which is the switching gap  of RRAM, and the T is the temperature. The result in Fig. 3(d)  shows a linear relationship between ln(I) and   $\mathrm{V}^{0.5}$  , indicating  that it fits Schottky emission conduction mechanism well. The  slope is proportional to the reciprocal of Schottky barrier  thickness, and the intercept is proportional to the Schottky  barrier height. Next, present the fitting results of the three  1T1R devices in Table 1. As shown in Table 1, it can be  observed that with an increase in   $\mathrm{V_{g}},$  , the intercept becomes  more negative, indicating an increase in Schottky barrier  height. This results in electrons facing greater difficulty in  crossing the barrier, leading to a higher HRS. Additionally, as   ${\mathrm{V_{g}}}$   increases, the slope becomes smaller. Since the slope is  inversely proportional to the Schottky barrier thickness, this  implies a larger Schottky barrier thickness, contributing to  more significant filament retraction. The current fitting results  are consistent with the experimental measurement results.

## IV.   C ONCLUSION 

In summary, a method to obtain different HRS in 1T1R by  applying varying   ${\mathrm{V_{g}}}$   during the reset process is proposed.  While this method allows for the attainment of distinct HRS,  it is observed that these states exhibit different retention  reliabilities. The higher the resistance state, the worse the  retention, attributed to unstable filament recombination. This  result highlights the trade-off relationship between memory  window and reliability, emphasizing the need to consider this  perspective when defining the operational conditions.
