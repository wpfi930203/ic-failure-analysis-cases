## New perspective on understanding contact between  metal and oxide through atomistic modeling: a case  study on tin oxide in contact with common metals  

Yujia Tian 1, 2 , Devesh Kripalani 1 , Ming Xue 2 , Swee Lee Gan 2 , and Kun Zhou 1\*  1 School of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, Nanyang Technological University, 50 Nanyang Avenue, Singapore 639798,  Singapore, Singapore  2 Infineon Technologies Asia Pacific Pte. Ltd., 168 Kallang Way, Singapore 349282, Singapore, Singapore  \* Email :  kzhou@ntu.edu.sg

Abstract —Remarkable efforts have been devoted to the  detection, location, and resolution of electrical contact failures.  However, because of the complex interplay of physical,  mechanical, and electrical factors as well as the continuous  miniaturization of electronic devices to the nanoscale, current  methods that rely on conventional contact resistance  measurements face significant limitations. By adopting the  approach of atomistic modeling, our study tackles this persistent  issue by providing a new perspective on fundamentals of contact  mechanisms. As an example, the contact between common  metals and tin oxide is systematically evaluated and compared  by examining the charge transfer and bonding at the interface.  The obtained theoretical results serve as the principles for  explaining the contact behavior of such materials systems and  provide more insights and practical guidance on materials  selection, troubleshooting and problem solving, as well as device  design.

Keywords—metal–semiconductor   contact,   electronic  properties, Schottky barrier, metal-induced gap states, atomistic  modeling, first-principles calculations, two-dimensional materials

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

Over the years, extensive efforts have been devoted to  detecting and locating the site of electrical contact failure  within the test environment of integrated-circuit packages [1,  2]. However, so far, there is no breakthrough for the  characterization and practical resolution of such failures  because their detection or location is generally based upon the  testing of the contact resistance of two surfaces, which in turn  is largely dependent on various physical, mechanical, and  electrical factors.

With the poor accuracy and consistency of the test results  based on current methods, there is a great deal of test time,  yield loss, and monetary loss, along with significant field  reliability failure. Hence, there is a huge incentive for  establishing a comprehensive theory or model to associate all  the influencing factors during testing.

There are mainly two types of models studying the contact  failure in the literature. The first focuses on understanding its  statistical dynamics. For example, Correcher et al. [3]  developed a probabilistic model, which computes the  probability of intermittent failure at any time, and a temporal  model, which allows on-line prediction of the best time to  repair or substitute the faulty part. These models are highly  relevant in device maintenance but require either historical  data or actual data as the input, without considering the  physics behind the failure. In contrast, the second type of  model incorporates the failure mechanism by using classical  macroscopic theories of electrical contact [4-6]. For example,  Lyu et al. [4] introduced a model to diagnose the intermittent

fault of a helicopter electrical connector by establishing the  relationship between its vibration stress and contact damage.  Such models can be used to easily identify the fault but are  only applicable to the specific part, based on which the model  was developed.

With the continuous miniaturization of electronic devices  to the nanoscale, quantum effects start to dominate, rendering  theories based on classical physics inadequate in providing a  holistic understanding of the device failure. A clearer  understanding on contact mechanisms at the atomic level is  thus required.

In this paper, we adopt the approach of atomistic modeling  based on first-principles calculations to analyze the contact  between metal and oxide. By taking the contact junction  formed between common metals (gold: Au, nickel: Ni,  platinum: Pt, or palladium: Pd) and tin monoxide (SnO) as an  example, the charge transfer and bonding at the interface and  the electronic behavior of the materials system are  systematically evaluated. These findings provide new insights  into understanding the contact performance of Ni/Pd/Au metal  finish on printed circuit board in addition to the analysis based  on classical physics [7]. While this example manifests external  contact at the system level between these metals and the  natural oxide formed on tin surface finish [8], it can also be  extended to represent internal contact.

This work serves as a robust theoretical basis for  understanding the contact performance of such materials  systems in the quantum regime. It also provides thorough  guidance on selection of the metal species for different  applications. The established model allows the incorporation  of physical, mechanical, and electrical factors that may affect  the materials properties for easy parametric studies, which will  be highly significant in facilitating the failure analysis and  rational design of next-generation electronic devices.

## II.   C OMPUTATIONAL  D ETAILS 

Four to six layers of metal atoms are usually sufficient to  model a bulk metal in contact as only the surface layers are  involved in the interaction with the other material [9-11]. SnO  is a typical van der Waals (vdW) layered material (Fig. 1(a)  and (b)) and can thus be easily modeled with just a monolayer.  It has been shown that the behavior at the metal–molybdenum  disulfide (MoS 2 ) interface was largely unchanged when  increasing the number of layers of  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   from one to two [12].  As shown in Fig. 1(c), a slab model is constructed by stacking  four or five metal atomic layers on top of monolayer SnO  (mSnO) to form a heterostructure that describes the contact  system.

## III.   R ESULTS AND  D ISCUSSION 

## A.   Charge Transfer and Bonding at the Interfaces  

Quantum tunneling is a phenomenon in which a particle  like an electron passes through a potential energy barrier  without having enough energy to enter or surmount it based  on classical physics. It is the source of current leakage in verylarge-scale integration electronics, cold emission from a metal  surface under a voltage bias in electron microscopes [14], and  current controlled by the channel of field effect transistors [15,

16]. A low tunnel barrier height and short tunnel barrier width  indicate high probability of electron transmission across the  barrier, and in the context of a contact interface, this electron  transmission leads to current flow across the interface.

As shown in Fig. 2(a), the tunnel barrier height at the  metal–mSnO interface is obtained as the difference in the  average potential in the  xy  plane between the metal and tin  atoms at the contact. It has values of 10.74, 13.05, 16.47, and   $11.88~\mathrm{eV}$   for Au, Ni, Pt, and Pd, respectively. The average  vertical distance   $d$   between the two materials is also the  maximum width of the barrier [17] and is plotted in Fig. 2(b)  as the gray bars. The values are quite similar with the Ni  contact showing the lowest value. Considering that the atoms  of the bulk metals have different sizes, the sum of atomic radii  s  of tin and the corresponding bulk metal as well as the ratio   $d/s$   are also plotted in the same figure to indicate the extent of  overlap between the materials. The ratio  $d/s$   is below   $100\%$   for  all the contact systems, manifesting the strong interaction  between the two materials. It decreases in the order of Au, Ni,  Pt, and Pd.

The bonding strength not only reflects the robustness of  the interface structure but also indicates the amount of residue  left behind when the two materials are separated, which may  be a potential source of contaminants. To quantify the bonding  strength, the interlayer binding energy   $E_{\flat}$   at the interface can  be computed based on

$$\begin{array}{r}{E_{\mathfrak{b}}=\frac{E_{\mathrm{metal-mSnO}}-E_{\mathrm{metal+mSnO}}}{A},}\end{array}$$
( 1 )

where   $E_{\mathrm{metal-mSD}}$   is the total energy of the metal–mSnO  heterostructure,  $E_{\mathrm{metal+mSD}}$   is the sum of the total energy of the  isolated bulk metal layers and mSnO fixed in the  heterostructure lattice, and  $A$   is the interface area.

For all the bulk metals, the calculated   $E_{\flat}$   values are  negative, indicating that their heterostructures are stable and  can therefore be experimentally fabricated or formed by  contact. Their magnitudes are presented in Fig. 2(c), which are  all one order of magnitude higher than that of typical vdW  materials systems such as Au–graphene   $(-62.3\ \mathrm{\dot{meV}}/\mathrm{\AA}^{2}$  ;  $d=$  3.07 Å,   $s\doteq2.21\mathrm{~\AA~}$  ) [13] and graphene–monolayer   $\mathrm{GeN}_{3}$   (–  $13.4\,\mathrm{meV}/\mathring{\mathrm{A}}^{2}$  ;  $d\,{=}\,3.5\,\mathrm{\AA}$  ,   $\mathit{\bar{s}=1.9\bar{9}\;\AA}$  ) [18]. The strong bonding  here can be attributed to the lone-pair electrons of Sn that form  bonds with the metals. As a bigger magnitude corresponds to  stronger bonding, it can be interpreted that Pt forms the most  robust contact with mSnO while Au leaves the least residue  on mSnO upon separation of the two contacting materials.

Although the bonding is strong, the geometry of mSnO is  almost not affected, as shown in Fig. 1(c). Comparing Fig.  2(b) and (c), it is seen that the bonding strength increases as  d / s  decreases, except for the case of Pd. A low   $d/s$   value  generally indicates strong interaction between the atoms that  binds them tightly. Since Pd has fully occupied 4d orbitals and  no other shell electrons available for covalent bonding,  although it corresponds to the lowest  d / s  ratio among all the  four metals studied here, its bonding with mSnO is relatively  weak.

To understand the charge transfer at the interface between  the metal and mSnO, the electron density difference   $\Delta{\uprho}$   is  computed as

$$\Delta\uprho=\uprho_{\mathrm{metal-mSDO}}-\uprho_{\mathrm{metal}}-\uprho_{\mathrm{mSDO}},$$,
( 2 )

where ρ metal–mSnO , ρ metal , and   $\uprho_{\mathrm{mSD}}$   are the electron density of  the metal–mSnO structure, metal layers, and mSnO,  respectively. Its plane-averaged values along the   $z$   direction  perpendicular to the metal–mSnO interfaces are plotted in Fig.  3.

It can be observed that as   $d/s $  decreases, charge  redistribution involves more layers of the metal atoms. The  top metal layer is not affected in all the cases presented here,  which reaffirms that the model with four layers of metal atoms  is sufficient to represent the response of the bulk metal in such  contact systems.

The electron accumulation as indicated by the yellow  isosurfaces at the contact interfaces in Fig. 3(a–c) highlights  the covalent nature of the bonding between the two contacting  materials. In these plots, the s orbital of tin becomes depleted  as shown by the cyan color, and it hybridizes with the orbitals  of the metal atoms to form such covalent bonds. Since Pd has  no available shell electrons for sharing, electrons are  significantly rearranged instead as shown in Fig. 3(d). Such  charge transfer analysis provides the fundamentals for  explaining the different contact behaviors of various materials  systems.

The quantum tunneling properties assessed above  determine the probability that an electron tunnels across the  interface, where the barrier is created solely because of the  difference in potential between the different materials and  their spatial separation. There is another barrier, known as the  Schottky barrier, that affects the contact resistance at a metal– semiconductor interface. As shown in Fig. 5, when two  materials are put into intimate contact, because of their  different work functions   $\Phi$  , equalization of the Fermi level  $E_{\mathrm{f}}$  occurs spontaneously with the movement of major charge  carriers (holes in the case of a p-type semiconductor) from  higher energy levels to lower energy levels. With charges  accumulating at the materials surfaces, an electric field is set  up, which forms an energy barrier preventing further  movement of the major charge carriers.

Schottky barriers have rectifying characteristics, allowing  the material to be used as a diode. The key parameter of a  Schottky barrier is the Schottky barrier height (SBH). It is  usually predicted by the Schottky–Mott rule [20, 21] to be  proportional to the difference between the work function   $\Phi_{\mathrm{m}}$

of the metal and the electron affinity   $\chi$   of the semiconductor  (2.38 eV for mSnO).

Fig. 6 presents the calculated work functions of the metals  in their pristine states (consistent with values from the  literature [22-26]) and in contact with mSnO. The work  functions of the formed heterostructures show a consistent  trend with those of the pristine metals, indicating that the  former can be predicted based on the latter. All the values of  the pristine metals are lower than that of mSnO (5.16 eV),  except for that of Pt. Hence, theoretically, Schottky barrier  should form for the contact with all the other metals. However,  empirically, it has been found that there is only a weak  correlation between the metal work function and the SBH  [27]. The exact value still needs to be obtained by more  accurate calculations or experiments.

Since the electron SBH is lower than the hole  SBH for the contact with all the metals, the contact is n-type.  The electron SBHs for the contact with the different metals are  presented in Fig. 6. ## IV.   C ONCLUSION 

This work provides a new perspective on understanding  the contact between a metal and an oxide. By considering the  metal–SnO contact as an example, the contact performance at  the interface is analyzed through atomistic modeling, and the  underlying mechanisms are evaluated by examining the  charge transfer and electronic properties of the optimized  structures. The metallization phenomenon, where the band  gap of the oxide vanishes upon contact with metals,  demonstrates the potential of tuning the oxide for lower  contact resistance. Oxidation of the metal surface (tin in this  case) is therefore shown to be not as detrimental to achieving  good contact and may not always be the cause of failure,  thereby challenging the conventional belief in the necessity of  physically puncturing through the surface oxide layer or its  complete removal.

While the contact with Au is shown to be the most  favorable electrically, its mechanical bonding is the weakest.  In the context of Ni/Pd/Au metal finish, while Au provides the  best electrical contact properties, its thin cosmetic layer is  easily removed by wear and tear during the long service life  of the device. When Pd is exposed and in contact with the  oxide, the contact resistance may increase. Since Ni serves as  a promising candidate balancing both the electrical and  mechanical aspects, when it is eventually exposed, it is able to  offer reasonable performance with stable and sustainable  contact. The ranking of these metals in terms of the bonding  strength and electronic properties provides guidelines on their  order of failure. The results obtained in this work can be  applied to analyze the external contact between a metal pin  and the oxide of a surface finish; they may also represent the  internal contact between the metal gate and channel material  of a transistor.

The validity of the results can be experimentally  demonstrated in future work by plotting the  $I{-}V$  curve of each  contact system, which provides information on the quantum  tunneling characteristics and Schottky barrier. The lower the  barrier, the greater the magnitude of the tunneling current.  Other factors such as the contact force and humidity can also  be easily investigated during the measurements and modeled  accordingly.

Note that the current work is based on the assumption of  perfect interfaces, which may require the prevention or

removal of surface impurities and annealing. Contamination  of the surface may cause surface reactions, which can affect  the performance of the contact system. Nevertheless, the  impact of impurities can be easily studied by introducing them  in the existing models as part of future work.

The approach of atomistic modeling helps to quantify the  degradation and locate the root cause of material-related  failure in various devices. The exact failure mechanism can  therefore be unveiled for a more holistic understanding.  Importantly, a robust computational model of the contact  interface can also serve as a useful platform for preliminarily  exploring possible solutions and strategies to mitigate failure  before real-world testing and full-scale deployment. The  established model in this work can be modified to  accommodate external forces, impurities, electric fields, etc.  to assess their impacts on the contact behavior, which can be  related to classical-physics experiments such as contact  resistance measurements in the Kelvin test. It can also be  extended to study the oxide–oxide contact for a complete  understanding in the case of surface oxidation of both  contacting materials, which opens a new avenue for exploiting  the advances of atomistic modeling in contact engineering.
