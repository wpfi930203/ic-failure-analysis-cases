## Novel rapid and deposition-free strategy for FIB  cross-section preparation  

Susanne Beuer 1 , Mathias Rommel 1   1 R&D Semiconductor Devices   Fraunhofer Institute of Integrated Systems and Device Technology IISB  Erlangen, Germany  Email: susanne.beuer@iisb.fraunhofer.de, mathias.rommel@iisb.fraunhofer.de

Abstract — In this work a novel focused ion beam (FIB)  cross-section preparation method of “flying cube” is presented,  which allows time savings of   $\mathbf{60-85\%}$   of the time required for  cross-section preparation compared to the standard processes  applied so far. Furthermore, it enables high-quality crosssections even for samples with thick non-conductive or poorly  conductive layers (e.g., photoresist, PMMA or SiO 2 ) and  samples with cavities or pronounced topographies. Thereby it  gets along with no deposition of impurities on the wafer surface  other than the sputtered and redeposited material. Due to the  great flexibility, it is possible to extend the field of application  beyond the field of electronics and semiconductors.

Keywords—   cross-section   method,   strategy,   FIB,  contamination free, artefact-free, flying cube

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

Focused ion beam systems (FIB) are widely used tools  throughout the entire value chain in the semiconductor  industry. Starting with process control in production lines,  through defect analysis in the backend to failure analysis after  breakdowns by lamella or cross-section preparation for e.g.,  transmission or scanning electron microscopy (i.e. TEM or  SEM) inspection. Since the early 1990s, Platinum, and later  also Tungsten, have been used as protective layers in these  processes by means of gas-induced deposition [1]. This has  many advantages, such as protecting the sample surface from  damage by the ion beam or avoiding edge rounding on the  uppermost layers of the sample due to the Gaussian ion beam  tail [2]. Unfortunately, such protection layers also go along  with several disadvantages: In addition to the time-consuming  electron beam-induced (ebid) (200nm, \~8-10min, FEI Helios  G1) and subsequent ion beam-induced deposition (ibid)   $(2\upmu\mathrm{m})$  ,  \~12min, FEI Helios G1), the deposited material itself (i.e., Pt  or W) usually forbids further processing of the wafers being  investigated for process control due to the potential  contamination of processing tools during subsequent  processes.

In order to remove this contamination, various tests were  carried out and removal strategies developed [3,4], which,  however, require additional removal time. As an alternative, a  carbon precursor could be used for depositing the protective  layer, but this leads to even longer deposition times due to  lower deposition rates (ebid by a factor of 2.4, FEI Helios G1)  and lower contrast in the SEM images.

Not only is contamination through deposition considered  critically in the semiconductor industry, the use of Ga ions for  sputtering is also criticized. E.g., in subsequent hot processes,  these injected ions could lead to further diffusion and disrupt  the functionality of surrounding components. Against that  several publications (e.g., [5, 6]) show that the contamination  caused by cross-sectioning using Ga FIB is only a local and  negligible factor, for most applications. The influence of the

range and quantity of injected gallium ions [7] is therefore  dependent on the individual case and application and should  be considered accordingly.

Especially with the growing need for precise crosssectioning of wide bandgap materials such as silicon carbide  (SiC) or diamond, due to their hardness, FIB preparation  artifacts such as rippling [8] or “curtaining” become critical  and might hinder unambiguous interpretation of crosssections. In order to reduce these effects in a Xe plasma-FIB  (PFIB), it is possible to deposit a mixture of different  precursors (i.e. Pt and C) to harden the protection layer [9].  Obviously, also this deposited material must be removed, and  the sample thoroughly cleaned before further processing to  avoid contamination. Alternatively, a rocking sample method  [10] can be used after standard Pt-deposition, which reduces  curtaining effects by changing the angle of incidence of the  ions by tilting the stage sideways during the preparation  process. However, this method is also associated with  additional time required [11].

Several manufacturers of FIB systems with integrated liftout system [11, 12] suggest methods of applying a type of hard  mask (e.g. pieces of Si cut out using FIB) moved to the  position to be observed by the needle and fixing it to the  surface using deposition (C or Pt) or e-beam adhesive. In  addition to the higher cross-section quality, another advantage  of this process is the possibility of minimizing contamination  on the wafer using carbon precursor gas for deposition.  However, preparing such masks, fixing them to the lift-out  needle and to the sample afterwards, and removing the hard  mask after cross-section preparation only provide small time  advantage over the standard preparation method using  deposition. Thus, so far, no cross-sectioning approach using  FIB is available which targets at optimizing preparation time  and contamination avoidance together [13].

Our newly developed “flying cube”-strategy combines the  advantages of cross-section quality improvement by use of a  hard mask, significant time saving compared to conventional  processing and avoiding additional deposition and removal  steps.

## II.    M ETHODS   

In this method, a silicon hard mask is used, which is  positioned above the subsequent cross-section position using  a lift-out needle without contact (from   ${\sim}100\mathrm{nm}$   up to a few  microns distance above the surface) and held there. This  avoids edge rounding of the uppermost layers on the sample  and provides the advantage of curtaining-reduced crosssections by using a hard mask for their preparation.

The Si-cube is attached to the lift-out needle perpendicular  to the direction of the incident ion beam (see Fig. 1). For this  purpose, the Si-cube size required for the cross-section width  is selected from a “cube-reservoir-frame” mounted on a  standard SEM pin stub.

The Si-cubes are connected to the frame by bridges, which  need to be cut after attaching the Si-cube to the lift-out needle.  Hereby, various developed strategies enable contaminationfree attachment to the needle without gas deposition. E.g., a  lift-out needle tip shaped staircase wedge, which is sputtered  into the surface of the Si-cube, enables the contact surface to  be enlarged from a point to a line when the needle is  subsequently inserted. The stable connection between the  needle and Si-cube is then created with a U-shaped sputter  pattern around the tip, in which the redeposition of the  sputtered material is forced with increased dwell time to fill  the remaining gap. For additional connection stability for  larger Si-cubes dimensions and, therefore, higher weight    $(>\bar{2}00\upmu\mathrm{m}$   width for use in Xe plasma-FIBs) an additional Cdeposition on top of the contact area would also be possible.  Once the Si-cube has been cut free, the “flying cube” can be  positioned over the sample (see Fig.2).

The use of commercially available Si-cubes of different  thicknesses and dimensions makes it possible to use them in a  wide variety of FIB systems with all kinds of lift-out systems.

The great time saving related to this method is not only  due to the elimination of long gas-induced deposition, but also  to the fact that many times higher ion currents (6.5nA rough  cut, 2.8nA fine cleaning, FEI Helios G1;   $0.5{\mathrm{-}}1\upmu\mathrm{A}$   rough cut,  60nA fine cleaning, FEI Helios G4 PFIB CXe) for the crosssection preparation can be used, as the hard mask can reduce  the effects of the beam tails of the Gaussian beam shape on  the prepared cross-section.

After cross-section preparation, the “flying cube” is  moved to the parking position again using the lift-out system  and can be reused several times (at least 15-20 times,  depending on the dimension of the Si-cube and the depth of  the cross-section) due to the minimal removal of the cutting  edge, without having to be fixed to the needle again in a timeconsuming process   $(\sim15\mathrm{min})$  .

Through this novel cross-sectioning strategy it is possible  to get along with no deposition of impurities on the wafer  surface other than redeposition of the sputtered material, but  also to save   $60–85\%$   of the time required for cross-section  preparation compared to the standard cross-sectioning process  (depending on the selected FIB system i.e. Ga (see Fig. 3), Xe  (see Table I and Fig. 4)).

Even for samples with thick non-conductive or poorly  conductive layers (e.g., photoresist, PMMA (Fig. 5) or SiO 2 )  it is possible to prepare high-quality cross-sections just by  getting the Si-cube in contact with the sample surface by  lowering the lift-out needle without applying pressure. Thus,  the typically occurring charging problems during preparation  can be minimized. cavities or high topographies (Fig. 6, Fig.  7), which cannot be prepared using standard cross-sectioning  methods due to the poor side wall and undercut covering, or  only with great difficulties, constitute no problem. The quality  of the prepared cross-sections even makes it possible to  perform an instant dopant-contrast SEM analysis without  further low-kV cleaning steps on SiC (Fig. 4).

A Si-cube thickness of approx.   $4–5\upmu\mathrm{m}$   has proven to be  optimal for a Ga FIB. This results from a trade-off between  the shortest possible sputtering time of the additional hard  mask material and the thickness required to produce curtainfree surfaces even for   $10{\mathrm{-}}15\upmu\mathrm{m}$   deep cross-sections. Since a  Xe plasma-FIB usually works with significantly higher  currents and sputtering rates [14], thicker cubes (approx. 25-  $30\upmu\mathrm{m})$  ) are used here, which enable cross-sections in SiC that

are at least  $80\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$   deep with outstanding quality (Fig. 8) even  when using very high ion beam currents   $(0.5{\mathrm{-}}1\upmu\mathrm{A})$  ).

## IV.    C ONCLUSION AND  O UTLOOK 

This work shows the superiority of our newly developed  “flying cube”-strategy compared to conventional crosssection preparation approaches. Hereby the combination of  the advantages of cross-section quality improvement by use of  a hard mask, significant time savings and avoidance of  additional deposition and removal steps was demonstrated.  This combination makes it possible to save considerable costs  not only in the semiconductor industry. The automation  integrated into the FIB system software would be the next  logical step.

This revolutionary strategy is currently in the patent  application and offers great potential for the use in all standard  FIB systems (e.g., Ga- FIBs, Xe plasma-FIBs, AuGeSi-FIBs)  with lift-out systems. By using a Ga-free FIB (i.e. Si- or Xeion FIB) in conjunction with this process, it is even possible  to   continue   processing   without   hot-process-relevant  impurities in the semiconductor.

Research and development facilities can also benefit from  the use of this novel strategy, as the further use of small  number of substrates after a cross-section is also very  important financially.

Further investigations have been started to quantify the  obviously better quality of the cross-section by determining  the side wall damage compared to the standard methods (i.e.  TEM lamellae analyses). First results show slightly lower  depths of impacting Xe ions into the sidewall with the “flying  cube”-strategy.   Also   continuing   development   and  improvements are in the testing phase and will be presented in  the future.
