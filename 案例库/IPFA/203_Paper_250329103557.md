## Application of ICP-MS to identify the source  of  Metallic Contamination  

S.P. Neo 1 , W.T. Yeo 1 , C.W. Loh 1 , R. Zaw 2 , V.J. Suazo 2 ,  J. Nilesh 3 , I.J. Sohn 3 , Ramesh Rao 1 , Z.Q. Mo 1  1 QRA-PFA, GLOBALFOUNDRIES Pte Ltd  2 Lithography operation, GLOBALFOUNDRIES Pte Ltd  3 Facilities Operation, GLOBALFOUNDRIES Pte Ltd   60 Woodlands Industrial Park D Street 2, Singapore 738406  Phone : (+65) 66701524, Email : sohping.neo@globalfoundries.com

Abstract — Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry  (ICP-MS) is being widely used in semiconductor industry to  analyze the metallic contents of chemicals used in the wafer  fabrication process. In this paper, application of ICP-MS to  identify the types and source of metallic contaminations in  solvents used in the lithography step, which subsequently  translated to become particles affecting the lithography process  and causing the wafers to fail was discussed. Two case studies  were presented in this paper.

Keywords — ICP-MS,   Resist   reduction   consumption,  lithography

## I. INTRODUCTION  

Metallic contaminations on silicon wafers significantly  impact the yield and reliability of the integrated circuits and  semiconductor devices. Some effects that have been reported  include threshold voltage deviation, hot carrier degradation,  gate oxide integrity damage, junction leakage, retention time  failure and film growth defects [1][2]. As technology  advances, maintaining low contamination levels becomes  increasingly critical. Metallic contamination on wafers can  originate from the equipment used in the fabrication process  such as the valves, chambers, seals, and heating elements [3].  They can also be introduced through chemicals used in  cleaning, etching or deposition processes.  The sources of  metallic contamination in chemicals can be multiple:  from  the drums, tanks or systems containing them, pipelines  transporting them, valves of the system corroded or due to  humans hooking up drums to the system [4].  ICP-MS is the  technique widely employed in the semiconductor industry for  measurement of metallic impurities in process chemicals [5]  [6]. The sampling point of the chemicals for ICP-MS analysis  can be at the incoming stage, the central supply system or  directly from the point of use which is  from the tool [4]. Most  analyses conducted using ICP-MS instrumentation are  quantitative, achieved through calibration with standards.

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICPMS) is a  powerful analytical technique used for elemental  analysis. It can detect and quantify about 80 elements from  the periodic tables [7].  Analytes concentration of most  elements could be determined at parts per billion (ppb) and  parts per trillion (ppt) levels, thus making it ideal for  measuring the purity of chemicals used in semiconductor  manufacturing. ICP–MS can be used for qualitative, isotopes  ratio, semi-quantitative analysis, and quantitative analysis. With its ability to simultaneously detect and quantify  multiple elements with high sensitivity and precision and fast  analysis time, ICP-MS has become an indispensable tool in a  wide range of scientific disciplines and industries   uses. In

ICP-MS, an inductively coupled plasma is used to ionize the  samples and singly charged ions thus created are directed into  a mass filtering device known as the mass spectrometer  where the ions are sorted based on their mass-to-charge ratio  (m/z). In general, ICP-MS consists of the following parts: (i)   Sample introduction system which consists of a nebulizer to  convert the liquid samples into a fine aerosol and spray  chamber which only allows small droplets   $(\mathord{<}10\upmu\mathrm{m})$   to enter  the plasma (ii) ICP torch and RF coil to generate the argon  plasma to convert the elements in the sample aerosol to ions   (iii) Interface to extract the ions into the vacuum system (iv)  Ion lenses to separate the positively charged ions from  undesired neutral species and photons and guide the desired  ions into the quadrupole (v) Collision/reaction cell to remove  interferences that can degrade the detection limits achieved  (vi) Mass spectrometer (MS) to sort ions based on their massto-charge ratio   $\left(\mathrm{m/z}\right)$   (vii) The electron multiplier detector  (viii) data processing. The layout is depicted in Fig. 1.

In this paper, two case studies where wafers showed  defectivity due to the metallic contamination present in the  solvents used in the lithography process were presented.  Lithography, in semiconductor manufacturing, is a process  by which a pattern is transferred from a photomask to the  surface of a wafer. It involves the following critical steps:  wafer preparation, photoresist spin coat, prebake, exposure,  post-exposure bake, development, and post bake as shown in  Fig. 2. Before the deposition of the photoresist, anti-reflective  coating (ARC) will be deposited. Anti-reflective coatings  reduce reflectivity at resist interfaces, thus providing better  line width control with minimal loss of resist performance.

Reducing resist consumption (RRC) [9] is widely used in  process to reduce the cost of resist per wafer as it can reduce  the consumption of the resist by   $75\%$   or more. By using  solvent to pre-wet the wafer surface prior to the resist  deposition step, the amount of photoresist used can be  reduced and the photo resist can be coated on wafer easier  than normal coating method. RRC is applicable not only at  the resist coating step but at the Bottom ARC (BARC)  coating as well.  However, by introducing additional steps to  the process, it will expose the wafers to additional defectivity  issue. In this paper, two case studies with metallic  contamination in the RRC solvents leading to defectivities on  the wafers will be presented. The RRC solvents used in the  two case studies were BER and MEK.

## II     E XPERIMENTAL  S ETUP     

Calibration and Standards Preparation:  Solvent BER and  multi-elements standard were used. Sample 1: 10g of BER  was weighed into a precleaned vessel. Sample 2: 10g of the  same BER containing 100ppt spiked multi-element standard  solution was weighed into another precleaned vessel. Both  samples undergo closed environment evaporation to dryness  at   $120\mathrm{{oC}}$   using hot block evaporator. Upon cooling, 10g of   $1{:}20\,\mathrm{kHz}_{3}$   (Tamapure Nitric acid  $55\%$  ) solution was pipetted  into each vial to dissolve the residue. External standard  calibration was engaged in this case. Standard solutions were  prepared by spiking a multi-element standard solution (highpurity standards Certified Reference Material) into five precleaned PFA bottles, each containing   $1{:}20~\mathrm{{HOO}_{3}}$   solution to  give spike levels of 0, 50, 100, 250 and 1000 ppt. All the  solutions were prepared before analysis to maintain its  freshness. The calibration standards and samples were then  introduced into the ICP-MS and the percentage of recovery for  the 100ppt spike was calculated. All preparation and analyses  were performed in a Class 1000 clean room. The steps were  repeated for solvent MEK.

Nexion350 from Perkin Elmer as shown in Fig. 3 was the  ICP-MS used for the analysis. The detection limit of the  instrument is 5 ppt thus any readings below the detection limit  would not be reported.

Although the incoming BER metallic data as shown in  Table II were within the control limit but the pipelines  carrying the solvents and the system containing it could have  contributed contaminants to it. A check on the ionic filter for  the RRC on the bad tool, LAL763, showed that the age of the  filter was 4450 days, significantly longer as compared to that  of the good tool, LAL766, which was 1332 days. Analysis was  also performed on the BER from other similar types of tools  but with different lifespan of the filters. The metallic contents  of the BER with different lifespan of the ionic filters were  shown in Table II.

Based on the table, it was evident that the concentrations  of the metallic elements like Zn, Ca, Na, Ni and K increased  as the filters’ age lengthened. As the filter ages, the efficiency  of the filter would decrease.  Based on the data, it was highly  suspected that the ionic filter in the bad tool, LAL763, had lost

its efficiency due to aging. Consequently, the filter allowed  high metallic content to pass through, which was translated  into particulate contamination during wafer dispensing,  ultimately blocking the lithography process. The filter at  LAL763 was changed, and the wafer particle check performed  after the change showed the defectivity count dropped to  below the specification limit level as shown in Fig. 12. It was  thus confirmed that the aged ionic filter at LA763 was the  cause of the pitting at the active. The mechanism of the  formation of the pitting was illustrated in Fig. 13.  Micro  bubbles generated in the resist during spin coating due to ion  trap in the RRC solvent resulted in the pitting of the active.
