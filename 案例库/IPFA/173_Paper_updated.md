## A printable metallic ink containing biphasic alloys  for highly-reliable stretchable circuits  

Zhe   $\mathrm{Y}\mathrm{u}^{1}$  , Yiwen Luo 1 , Junhao Shen 1 ,  Xin Gao 2,3 , Jie Shang 2\* , and Xing   $\mathrm{W}\mathbf{u}^{1,4\ast}$    1 In Situ Devices Center, School of Integrated Circuits, East China Normal University, Shanghai 200241,China.  2 CAS Key Laboratory of Magnetic Materials and Devices, Ningbo Institute of Materials Technology and Engineering, Chinese Academy of  Sciences, Ningbo 315201, China.  3 Faculty of Chemical Engineering, Kunming University of Science and Technology, Kunming 650500, China .  4 State Key Laboratory of Infrared Physics, Shanghai Institute of Technical Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai 200083,  China.  Corresponding Author: Prof. J. Shang, Email: shangjie@nimte.ac.cn; Prof. X. Wu, Email: xwu@cee.ecnu.edu.cn

Abstract —Liquid metals are ideal materials for fabricating  stretchable circuits in wearable devices, becoming printable  metallic inks with high conductivity by blending with solid  nanoparticles. However, challenges related to reliability arise,  including variation of printing patterns, and electrical damage  during repeated stretching. Here, we report a novel metallic ink  containing biphasic alloys, obtained by annealing the liquid  metal-based mixture. The solid-phasic alloy adheres to the  surface of nanoparticles, creating an anchoring effect with  liquid-phasic alloy to enhance the reliability of stretchable  circuits based on this novel printable metallic ink.

Keywords—liquid metal, printable metallic ink, biphasic alloy,  reliability, stretchable circuits

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

The emergence of wearable devices has sparked extensive  research and attention towards stretchable circuits for device  interconnection [1,2]. Liquid metals represented by eutectic  gallium indium (EGaIn) are promising materials for  fabricating stretchable circuits due to their advantages of  metal-level conductivity and water-like flow [3,4]. By mixing  hard metal nanoparticles (e.g. Cu, Ag, Ni, etc.) without  organic solvents, it can be converted into a metallic ink with  high conductivity [4-6], which overcomes the high surface  tension and severe selective wetting of liquid metals in the  process of printing pattern. It also is named as semi-liquid  metals [4]. Most printable inks require the addition of organic  matter to bond the constituent materials, resulting in a  conductivity attenuation [7]. However, this metallic ink has  obvious shortcomings in reliability, mainly as follows: the  linewidth of circuit pattern will increase over time after  printing; the resistance will change irreversibly after repeated  stretching. It arises from the weak interface force between  liquid metals and hard nanoparticles that allows liquid metals  to flow slowly within the gap under the stimulation of small  external forces (including the gravity of itself), triggering the  reconstruction of nanoparticles.

Recently, it has been reported that solid-phasic alloy is  introduced into liquid metals to form biphasic liquid metals  for the development of neuromorphic circuits [8], inspiring  us to utilize the strong interaction between biphasic alloys in  order to solve the reliability problems faced by printable  metallic ink. The common methods for producing solidphasic alloy in liquid metals include surface oxidation, and  cooling crystallization [9,10]. Although these methods are insitu treatments, the conductivity and operating temperature  range of metallic ink will be limited. Therefore, there is an  urgent need to explore a more convenient and practical

method for obtaining metallic ink containing biphasic alloys  (MICBA) to verify the role of the new ink in improving the  reliability of stretchable circuits.

In this paper, we propose a low-temperature heat  treatment strategy to induce solid-liquid phase transition in  metallic ink, resulting in the formation of biphasic alloys.  This method is more general and only requires annealing the  mixture of EGaIn and silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) at 200   $^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   for 2 hours. During this process, the In component  separates out from the liquid-phasic alloy and transforms into  a solid-phasic alloy on the surface of nanoparticles. The  determined composition of the solid-phasic alloy is Ag 9 In 4   doped with a small amount of AgIn 2 , which has also been  documented in other studies [5]. An anchoring effect is  produced between the biphasic alloys, ensuring that the  pattern of the printed circuit can remain unchanged for a long  time. The resistance of the conductive line only changes by   $38.37\%$   at the strain of   $60\%$  , and it returns to the original  value with stress unloading. Finally, we demoed that a  stretchable circuit based on MICBA can withstand various  complex deformations while maintaining stable operation of  the connected devices. This research provides a novel method  towards developing highly reliable metallic ink and promotes  the application of stretchable circuits.

## II.    M ATERIALS AND  M ETHODS   

## A.   Fabrication of MICBA and Stretchable Circuits  

The preparation process of MICBA is shown in Figure 1a.  Firstly, EGaIn was added with   $100\;\mathrm{nm}\;\mathrm{Ag}\;\mathrm{NPs}$  , whose mass  was one-third of EGaIn that consists of   $24.5\%$   In and   $75.5\%$  Ga by weight. Subsequently, they were mechanically mixed  by using a stirrer for  $0.5\,\mathrm{h}$   to obtain a homogeneous mixture,  i.e. regular metallic ink. Then, the mixture was placed in a  tube furnace with   $\mathbf{N}_{2}$   atmosphere protection for annealing at   $200~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   for 2 h. Finally, the MICBA with high reliability was  obtained after air cooling. The stretchable circuit was  fabricated by stencil printing, which uses a rubber scraper to  apply ink on the stencil with the hollow pattern to obtain a  patterned circuit on the substrate. In this study, polyethylene  terephthalate (PET) was used as the flexible substrate,  polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and Ecoflex were used as the  stretchable substrate. Their thickness is about   $100~\mathrm{~\upmum}$  ,  without any particular surface treatment.

## B.   Morphological and Component characterization  

The morphology characterization of MICBA was  completed using optic microscopy and scanning electron

microscopy (SEM), and its component characterization was  completed through the testing of X-ray diffraction (XRD),  and Energy Dispersive Spectrometer (EDS).

## C.   Electrical and Mechanical characterization  

The electrical characterization of MICBA was tested  using the four-terminal method with the loaded DC of 100

mA. The mechanical characterization was completed using a  universal tensile testing machine, which can be combined  with the electric meter for in-situ dynamic characterization.  The test samples were a long strip with the length of   $6~\mathrm{{cm}}$  ,  the width of 2 cm, and the thickness of   $500\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$  . The width of  the conductive line is 2 mm.

## III.    R ESULTS AND  D ISCUSSION 

The conventional preparation of MICBA requires first  obtaining semi-metallic particles as the second phase, such as  a hard particle formed by graphene oxide wrapping EGaIn. In  contrast, we can induce the generation of a second phase  in  situ  inside the metallic ink through low-temperature  annealing, which has obvious advantages in manufacturing  cost and efficiency. Figure 1b and Figure 1c show the  morphology changes over time (3 days) of MI-based  conductive line and MICBA-based conductive line,  respectively, while the ambient temperature and relative  humidity were controlled at   $25\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   and  $40\%$  . Their linewidths  are all   $100~\mathrm{~\upmum}$  . It can be clearly found that there is a  significant increase in linewidth and roughness for MI-based  conductive lines, whereas MICBA-based conductive lines  always maintain the original morphology. Please note that the

conductive lines shown here are not encapsulated, so there  will be color differences caused by the oxide layer on the  surface after three days. Since the electrical property of the  conductive line is closely related to its morphology, the  resistance of the MI-based conductive line changed by  $6.07\%$    during the test, while that of the MICBA-based conductive  line only changed by   $0.19\%$   (Figure 1d), indicating that the  electrical reliability of ink is improved by 31.9 times. The  experimental results shown in Figure 1e further amplify the  difference in inks before and after annealing. Two drops of  ink with the same volume (MI on the left, and MICBA on the  right) were dropped on the plastic plate, and then the plastic  plate was placed vertically to observe the flow of the ink  droplets. Because of the fluidity of MI and MICBA, they will  slowly slide downward under the gravity, whereas MI  droplets slide faster, such as slipping off the plastic board at  the  $72^{\mathrm{nd}}$   hour.

The improvement of morphological reliability of ink by  annealing treatment has been well demonstrated, but it is not  yet known whether biphasic alloys are formed inside it, so  XRD tests were conducted on samples under different  annealing times. As shown in Figure 2a, the XRD spectrum  of MI (annealing for 0 h) shows a characteristic of  amorphous liquid alloy, that is, a huge broadened diffraction  peak at the 2θ of  ${30}^{\circ}$   to   ${40}^{\circ}$  . As the annealing time increases,  this diffraction peak gradually weakens, while some  characteristic peaks symbolizing solid-phasic alloy gradually  increase. According to the standard spectrums, it can be  found that these characteristic peaks mainly come from  Ag 9 In 4  and others comes from AgIn 2 , proving the existence  of biphasic alloy in the annealed sample and that the  composition of solid-phasic alloy is Ag 9 In 4  doped with a  small amount of AgIn 2 . Considering that the printing ink  should posses specific rheological properties [7], the  annealing time should be controlled at about 2 h, because the  broadened diffraction peak almost disappears in the spectrum  of sample annealing for 3 h, meaning the liquid-phasic alloy  is seriously consumed during the solid-liquid phase  transition. When the annealing temperature is raised to 300   $^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   for   $0.5\mathrm{~h~}$  , the sample state is similar to that at   $200~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   for   $^{3\mathrm{~h~}}$  , resulting in a large amount of second phase alloy and  segregation in the liquid phase alloy. Consequently, it is not  recommended to accelerate annealing, which will weaken the  time window for regulating the second phase alloy.  Furthermore, the change of surface morphology of MI and  MICBA samples before and after stretching was observed by  SEM (Figure 2b). Before stretching, a small amount of Ag  NPs protrudes on the surface of the MI sample, while the  graininess in the MICBA sample is more obvious. After  stretching, the Ag NPs in MI collapse and are replaced by  scratches of the oxide layer, which follow the horizontal  stretching direction. In contrast, the NPs act like anchors to  firmly stabilize the morphology of MICBA, inducing an  undulate wrinkle for stress releasing.

The potential reason for the difference in morphology  changes between MI and MICBA before and after stretching

is described in Figure 3a. Lacking the strong interaction  between Ag NPs and liquid metals in MI, its internal  components will undergo orientation reconstruction during  stretching, which leads to the attenuation of morphology  reliability. In the MICBA, the anchoring effect produced by  biphasic alloys imparts structural elasticity, allowing the  material morphology to maintain the original topological  structure during stretching and reversibly recover after  releasing the stress. To prove this theory, the rotational  rheology tests were performed on MI and MICBA (Figure  3b). The storage modulus of both is higher than their loss  modulus at low shear stress, and becomes lower at high shear  stress, which is the basic rheological property of ink that  meet the requirements of printing technology. The shear  stress corresponding to the same storage modulus and loss  modulus is the yield stress of the material, which is  equivalent to the internal material interface force. When the  storage modulus and loss modulus are the same, the  corresponding shear stress is the yield stress of the ink,  equivalent to its internal interfacial force. It is obvious that  the interfacial force of MICBA (4274 Pa) is larger than that  of MI (1300 Pa) from the test results. In addition, MI and  MICBA were ultrasonically dispersed in ethanol solution for  6 h to destroy the bond between liquid metals and Ag-based  NPs. Note that the Ag-based NPs are coated with a layer of  Ag outside on the Ni NPs, which facilitates the subsequent  separation of Ag-based NPs from the solution by using  magnetic field. As shown in Figure 3c, the ethanol solution  of the MI was more turbid than that of the MICBA, meaning  there are a large amount of liquid metals escaping from the  Ag-based NPs to form tiny droplets in solution. The Agbased NPs dispersed in ethanol solution were extracted  through magnetic separation and high-speed centrifugation,  and their morphology characterizations and elemental  distributions are shown in Figure 3d. It can be found that  there is almost no liquid metals residue on the surface of Agbased NPs of MI, while the Ag-based NPs of MICBA are still  wrapped by liquid metals (marked by white arrow), further  reflecting the robustness of anchoring effect.

Figure 4 shows the results of electrical properties of the  MICBA-based stretchable circuit. The conductive lines  exhibit low resistance levels   $(\mathord{<\!0.8}\ \Omega)$  , which decrease with  the increase of line width (Figure 4a). Their average  conductivity is   $4.08\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{S/m},$  , which is slightly lower than  that of metallic gallium   $(\sim\!7\times10^{6}\;\mathrm{S/m})$  , still maintaining the  metal-level conductivity [3]. The inset of Figure 4a is an  actual photo of test samples, where each conductive line has  the same length (5 cm) and thickness   $(20~\upmu\mathrm{m})$  . Moreover,  MICBA possesses an outstanding capability to print complex  patterns (Figure 4b) with a resolution accuracy up to   $50~\upmu\mathrm{m}$  (Figure 4c). In terms of dynamic electricity (Figure 4d), the  resistance of the sample only increased by   $38.37\%$   when the  strain reached   $60\%$  , and almost returned to its original state  along the loading curve after unloading the stress with the

hysteresis coefficient of   $9.12\%$  . Compared with MI-based  conductive lines, the dynamic stability and load-unload  coincidence of MICBA-based conductive lines are 4.07  times and 2.33 times higher, respectively, which is smaller  than the increase shown in Figure 1d and may be affected by  the packaging of the polymer. This excellent performance is  inseparable from the wrinkle structure observed in Figure 2b,  so the electrical response of the sample remains unchanged  during 1000 cycles of stretching (Figure 4e), where there is  less than   $6\%$   fluctuation in the response value. Meanwhile,  the resistance of the MICBA-based sample was recorded in  Figure 4f during a 5-turn twisting from angle of   $0^{\circ}$   to   $1800^{\circ}$  .  It is always less than  $0.6\;\Omega$  , exhibiting the dynamic stability  of the sample under complex deformation. Figure 4e shows  the actual testing photos.

Finally, we demonstrated the utilization of a MICBAbased stretchable conductive line to connect micro-LED,  which does not require welding, simply insert the pin of the  micro-LED into the liquid metal and package it with Ecoflex  (Figure 5a). The temperature endurance of the MICBA-based  sample was measured, because the high operating power of

the LED results in continuous heat production. The  resistance of the sample increases with the increase of  temperature, exhibiting a metallic characteristic, but the  increment is only   $26.78\%$   from  $20~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   to   $120~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  (Figure 5b).  With a unique soft interface and temperature endurance, the  MICBA-based conductive line connected with the micro

LED can work normally as a stretchable device. In Figure 5c  and Figure 5d, it can be found that the temperature and power  of the device continue to rise and tend to saturation without  stretched strain, and then they will fluctuate with the  stretching, but whether it is  $30\%$  ,   $60\%$  , or  $90\%$   of the strain,  the change rate is not more than   $3\%$  . Figure 5e shows the  patterned MICBA-based stretchable circuit to connect 10  micro-LEDs into a five-pointed star, which is outlined with

dotted lines. Our device is subjected to longitudinal  stretching and transverse stretching to simulate various types  of deformation that wearable devices will encounter in  practical application. Notably, there is no visible change in  the brightness of these 10 micro-LEDs throughout the  experiment. These tests fully validate that we fabricate an  outstanding, highly reliable stretchable device based on  MICBA.

## IV.    C ONCLUSION 

Herein, the mixture of EGaIn and Ag NPs is annealed at   ${200}\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   for 2 h to be a metal ink containing biphasic alloys, in  which the additional solid-phasic alloy is Ag 9 In 4  doped with  AgIn 2  on the surface of NPs. Thanks to the anchoring effect  between biphasic alloys, the new metallic ink exhibits  excellent reliability on the morphology and electrical  properties. For example, the stretchable circuit printed with  it can maintain the stability of pattern for 3 days, and its  resistance changes only   $38.37\%$   at large deformation.  Compared to conventional samples based on MI, its electrical  reliability is improved by 31.9 times in the unpackaged state  and 4.07 times in the packaged state. In the application  demonstration, highly reliable metal ink containing biphasic  alloy is successfully used to interconnect 10 micro-LEDs in  a wearable device, which can withstand various complex  deformation and its working parameter changes do not  exceed   $3\%$  . It not only provides a convenient method for

preparing biphasic liquid metals, but also elucidates the  mechanism by which biphasic alloys improve the reliability  of metallic ink, contributing to the application development  of stretchable circuits.
