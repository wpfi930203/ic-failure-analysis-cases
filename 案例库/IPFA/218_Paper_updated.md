## Optical-based defect Inspection Techniques for SiC  Wafers  

Kaichao Deng, Ge Jiang, Qiong Zhang, Hu Xuanyu Qichao Ding, Zhihong Mai   Hubei Jiufengshan Laboratory, 9 Jiulonghu Street, Wuhan City ，  Hubei Province 430206, P. R. China  \*Phone : (086) 27--59301999, Email:  dengkaichao@jfslab.com.cn

Abstract —Silicon carbide (SiC) has been recognized as a  wide band gap semiconductor since 1991, making it an ideal  material for high-temperature operation and high-power  applications. Furthermore, SiC exhibits exceptional thermal  conductivity and possesses a significantly higher breakdown  electric-field strength. It has been demonstrated that the  performance of SiC devices is significantly influenced by the  presence of detrimental defects, which are formed during the  crystal growth process. The non-destructive defect inspection  techniques capable of identifying and locating defects have  emerged as an indispensable requirement within the  manufacturing   process.   In   this   work,   we   present   a  comprehensive flow of SiC defect inspection technologies such  as Raman, FTIR, and CL to identify the location of defects and  their impact on SiC devices.

Keywords—Silicon carbide (SiC), defect inspection, nondestructive, Optical-based inspection.

I.

## I NTRODUCTION 

SiC, a wide-bandgap semiconductor, emerges as a  promising candidate to develop the next-generation power  devices attributed to a wide bandgap (\~3.26 eV for 4H-SiC),  and SiC-based devices can operate at higher electric fields  and higher temperatures with better reliability over Si-based  power electronics. Pedersen, Leone [1] However, there are  always many defects in SiC, wafers which, directly, affect  the performance of SiC devices and increase the production  cost. Consequently, defect inspection is important for  manufacturers.[2-5]

There are many surface characterization techniques  applied to inspect SiC defects, such as scanning electron  microscopy (SEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM),  transmission electron microscopy (TEM), etc.[6, 7] The  approaches provide inspection accuracy with nanometres but  without high throughput. Furthermore, KOH (potassium  hydroxide) is usually used to visualize surface defects by  etching them to a visible size under the optical microscope  with rapid scanning capabilities with high through.[8]  However, this is a destructive approach that cannot be used  in in-line mass production. Consequently, good defect  inspections of SiC should provide a non-destructive  approach and rapid scanning capabilities with high  throughput without loss of inspection accuracy, especially in  pursuit of in-line mass production.

In this paper, we reported a system of optical-based  inspection analysis plans and methods for SiC. The  inspection method is a non-destructive approach with high  throughput and accuracy. The analysis plan was defined for  the SiC qualification test. A qualification analysis flow was  designed to identify the defect location from wafer to device.  Defect analysis was applied to the defect location and the  mechanism was studied.

In our study, we  the Raman spectrum to inspect defects  of SiC owing to a powerful tool for identification of the  characterization of the defects in SiC.[11, 14] Figure 4A  shows the Raman scattering spectra of 4H-SiC, and Raman  peaks caused by the TO and LO modes appear at  wavenumbers of approximately 770–800 (TO mode) and   $960{-}970~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$    (LO mode), respectively. The Raman peak of  LO mode is affected by the defect density in the monitored  region. As the defect increases, the LO peak shifts toward the  high wavenumber side with peak broadening, as shown in  Figure A red line. As not affecting of LO by defects, the  defects on SiC can be presented by height ratio of TO/LO  Raman bands, as shown in Figure 5B.

The defects with nanometers on SiC will be inspected  by CL. The CL spectrum is an inspection approach with  nanoscale spatial resolution owing to the CL instrument  combined with SEM. Figure 7a shows a CL spectral of SiC,  and the hyperspectral intensity in the range of  $200\sim1000\;\mathrm{nm}$    is decreased as SiC defects are presented. Consequently, the  decrease can indicate defect in SiC surface. Figure 7B shows  a SEM image of SiC.  The SiC defect in SEM image is not  presented. Interestingly, a CL image with hyperspectral  mode can clearly identify its defects, shown in Figure 7C,  and a   ${\sim}100~\mathrm{nm}$  defect was identified (yellow spot shown.).  The results show a nanoscale spatial resolution for CL  inspecting SiC defects.
