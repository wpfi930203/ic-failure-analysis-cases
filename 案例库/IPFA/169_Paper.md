## Reliability of Reconfigurable Field Effect  Transistors: Early Analysis of Bias Temperature  Instability   

Giulio Galderisi 1 , Thomas Mikolajick 1,2 , and Jens Trommer 1  1 NaMLab gGmbH, Nöthnitzer Str. 64a, 01187, Dresden, Germany  2 Chair of Nanoelectronics, Technische Universität Dresden, 01187, Dresden, Germany  Email :  giulio.galderisi@namlab.com

Abstract  —  Bias temperature instability is a common reliability issue in Metal Oxide Semiconductor Field Effect Transistors used in silicon integrated circuits. When transferred to industrial processing platforms, also emerging electron devices  need to be characterized under gate bias stress, in order to reveal their behavior in realistic application scenarios. In this  work, we present an early evaluation of bias temperature instability on three-gated Reconfigurable Field Effect Transistors.  These emerging nanoelectronics devices allow to set and dynamically change their polarity into either n-type or p-type conduction modes, enabling also a multi-  $\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{V_{T}}$  behavior. The polarity control feature, opposite to conventional MOSFETs where the device nature is set to either n-type or p-type by the fabrication,  implies that both conduction modes of a Reconfigurable Field  Effect Transistor can be influenced by stress profiles of a given  polarity. Our experiments showed that NBTI has a stronger effect on both conduction modes of such a transistor, independent  of the V T  mode in which the device is operated, as compared to  PBTI. Moreover, n-type programmed devices showed a greater  degradation of their threshold voltages. Finally, we observed  that the high-V T  mode is more prone to be subjected to subthreshold slope degradation compared to the low-V T  mode.

Keywords  —  Reconfigurable Field Effect Transistors, RFET,  emerging devices, reliability, bias temperature instability, BTI.

## I.    I NTRODUCTION  

The use of electron devices in integrated circuit applications  requires a knowledge that goes beyond the understanding of  their basic properties and fundamental electrical characteristics. More specifically, it is of major importance that a device  shows not only good pristine performances, but also preserves its behavior for the whole intended product lifetime.  To evaluate these reliability features, the degradation of the  devices under test is generally accelerated under different  stress conditions. While this kind of analysis is commonly  performed for technologies that are used in commercially  available products with large-scale processed CMOS devices,  it is important to carry it out also and especially for all of  those emerging ones that are in view of being transferred to a  higher technological manufacturing level [1]. However,  emerging devices processed in laboratory facilities usually  suffer from high variability and yield issues. For these reasons, the analysis of their reliability features is often hindered  or not even considered in the early development phase. Reconfigurable Field Effect Transistors (RFETs) [2], [3], are a  family of CMOS-compatible emerging devices whose integration on scaled industrial platforms has recently started [4],  [5] after a long phase of extensive exploration [6]. Nowadays,  RFETs have been proposed for different applications, ranging from hardware security [7-10] to neuromorphic computing [11], [12], and analog circuit design [5], [13]. Given the

interest in using these devices for more circuit-oriented application scenarios, it is thus needed to acquire knowledge about  their reliability features. Moreover, already analyzing those  properties at this early integration stage can also serve as an  important anchor of trust to attract interest in the specific  technology.

Reliability of CMOS devices has been deeply investigated  along with all the issues that arise from scaling the transistor  dimensions, and that can possibly have an impact on the gate  oxide degradation mechanisms. For pre-45 nm technology  nodes [14], where   $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$   was employed as gate oxide [15],  negative bias temperature instability (NBTI) was found to be  the most impacting degradation mechanism for p-type  MOSFETs [16], [17], while both n-type and p-type  MOSFETs suffered from hot carrier injection (HCI) [18].  With the introduction of high-k gate oxides, such as   $\mathrm{HfO}_{2}$  ,  also positive bias temperature instability (PBTI) became a reliability concern in terms of threshold voltage shift due to  charge accumulation and trapping in the gate dielectric [19- 21]. To the best of our knowledge, reliability properties of  RFET devices have never been analyzed systematically or reported in literature so far. In fact, their nature of emerging  devices, still rather limited to laboratory-based technologies,  polarized the attention of the researchers mainly on their advanced features and functionalities. For this reason, rather  limited attention has been devoted to more industrially-relevant metrics, such as reliability. Nevertheless, considering  the recent developments in RFET devices research and manufacturing, the times are now ready for exploring in detail  those properties.

This paper is organized as it follows: in Section II, a brief  explanation of the basic principles upon which the RFET is  based introduces the reader to this emerging technology and  its special features. Details about the physical characteristics  of the devices employed in this work are also presented. In  Section III, the electrical characteristics of the fabricated devices used for the reported analysis are presented and discussed. After having explored features and characteristics of  the considered technology, in Section IV we discuss some  RFET circuit applications for which it is relevant to investigate device reliability. In Section V, a short review of bias  temperature instability (BTI) in standard MOSFET devices is  presented to introduce some possible degradation scenarios  that are considered for RFETs, given their unique properties.  This last part introduces the results of our experiments on BTI  degradation of RFET devices, presented in Section VI. The  last section finally summarizes and concludes the paper.

## II.   R ECONFIGURABLE  F IELD  E FFECT  T RANSISTORS   

Reconfigurable Field Effect Transistors (RFETs) are CMOS  compatible emerging devices based on intrinsic channels,  Schottky barrier source/drain contacts, and characterized by  structures with at least two multiple independent gates [22].  They have been investigated employing different material systems, such as silicon, germanium [23], [24], silicon-germanium [25], and 2D materials [26], [27], as well as geometries,  where stacked nanowires [28], FinFETs [29], and FDSOI [4],  [5] channels where explored. Independent of the specific implementation, and of the nomenclature chosen to describe their  inherent polarity control features, RFETs can be defined as  that family of devices that can deliver, within the same transistor, both n-type and p-type electrical characteristics with the  possibility to reversibly switch between the two modes during  operation. The control over the device polarity is achieved by  the modulation of the drain side Schottky barrier that is  formed between the silicon channel and the intruded silicide  contact, through an external voltage applied to the drain gate  (DG). This voltage blocks the injection of the undesired polarity carriers in the channel: holes for n-type operation and  electrons for p-type operation, respectively. In the specific  case of the three-gated RFETs analyzed in this work, the other  two gates are responsible for switching the transistor on and  off in two different threshold  $\left({{\bf V}_{\mathrm{T}}}\right)$   modes: the central gate triggers the so called low-  ${\mathbf{}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode, while the source gate (SG)  controls the high-  ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode. The two modes differ for the fact  that the SG controls the other Schottky barrier, while the CG  overlaps only the channel and acts on a purely thermal barrier  for the carriers. The physical differences between the two barrier types gives rise to the two switching modes [30]. A schematic depiction of the described device is presented in Fig. 1,  together with scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of  one of the fabricated transistors and the measurement pad  structures surrounding it. For this work, we realized RFETs on  50 nm wide and 10 nm high silicon nano-channels patterned  on a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) substrate with a 20 nm thick  buried oxide (BOX). The central gate is   $300\ \mathrm{nm}$  large and  spaced of   $150\;\mathrm{nm}$   from the outer ones, that are   $1\ \upmu\mathrm{m}$   large to  abundantly account for silicidation length variability without  introducing silicidation control areas [31] or special annealing  steps [32]. As a result of this design choice, we obtained a very  high yield and a very low device-to-device variability across  fifty transistors fabricated on the same chip [33], thus enabling  us to test their reliability features for the first time. A detailed  presentation of the baseline process route employed for the  fabrication of the devices used in this work is available in [34].

## IV.   RFET   C IRCUIT  A PPLICATIONS  

As already introduced, the use of RFETs has been recently  proposed in many trending topics in electronics, showing  how their inherent reconfigurable and polymorphic features  can lead to several benefits, given the specific application. In  the field of neuromorphic computing, it has been shown that  a single RFET can be operated to work as a synaptic device,  performing XNOR operation in binary neural networks  (BNNs) [38]. Since BTI has been recognized as an application-specific problem for neuromorphic computing already in  CMOS-based neurons suffering from aging due to the high  voltages especially employed to interface them with non-volatile memory (NVM) synapses [39], the importance of evaluating the reliability properties also of RFETs in this scenario  becomes clear. Another relevant topic in which RFETs have  been largely proposed is hardware security: in general, thanks  to their inherent camouflaging properties [8], they can be  used to create highly tiled and multi-functional polymorphic  logic gates [40]. Their use has been proved beneficial also in  mitigating delay [9] and power side-channel attacks [10],  [41], [42]. Lastly, analog circuit design has been recently explored to create adaptive systems able to minimize the transistor count while expanding the functionality range. A single  RFET has been used to realize and dynamically switch between frequency doubling, shifting and following operations  [5]. Also, adaptive half-wave rectification capabilities have  been shown using only one RFET [43]. When multiple reconfigurable transistors are combined into fundamental analog  circuital blocks, many interesting characteristics arise from

their unique properties. In particular, when operated in saturation regime, the drive current of a RFET is linearly influenced by the programming voltage. This feature can be exploited to configure the gain of a differential half circuit, or  to modulate the bias current of a current mirror built using  two RFETs. Differential pairs can exploit RFET features in  many ways: integrating a RFET-based current mirror in such  a fundamental analog building block, allows to modulate the  output swing of the differential amplifier. Moreover, the programming voltage can be used to compensate for PVT variations, when the differential pair is build using RFETs. Finally,  RFETs can also be integrated as dynamically adjustable active loads, with the chance to modulate their resistance  through a programming voltage signal [13]. All those analog  properties require a deep understanding and modelling of the  degradation phenomena caused by the effects of BTI, as it has  been shown for MOSFETs [44], [45].

V.

## D EGRADATION  M ECHANISMS AND  P OSSIBLE  S 

NBTI degradation in p-type MOSFETs featuring  $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$   as gate  oxide was found to be caused by the breaking of Si-H bonds  located at the   $\mathrm{Si}/\mathrm{Si}\mathrm{O}_{2}$   interface [46]. This phenomenon,  caused by the applied electric fields, leads to the generation  of interface traps with the resulting accumulation of positive  charge, that is irreversible and accounts for the permanent  part of the degradation arising from the applied stress [47].  As a consequence of this, a shift in the threshold voltage of  the transistor can be generally observed. Also PBTI stress  profiles in p-type MOSFET can create positive charges in the  oxide [48]. However, the relaxation after this kind of stress  follows a different behavior with respect to NBTI, depending  on the magnitude of the electric field across the oxide, and in  any case produces a much smaller permanent degradation in  an operation regime that is not generally employed in any application scenario [49].  NBTI in n-type MOSFETs, always  with   $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$   gate oxides, has not been investigated in greater  depth: this is because in the standard operation regime of nMOSFETs positive voltages are used, and due to the reported  insignificance of the threshold voltage shift in that scenario  [50],[51]. Nevertheless, this kind of stress can still have an  impact on the device performances [52], and it has been  mainly investigated to shed light on the role of competitive  traps contributing to turnaround points of NBTI stressed devices of any polarity [53], [54]. The last scenario of PBTI  stress in n-type MOSFETs has also been shortly reported as  negligible [55].

What is interesting to notice, and functional to the upcoming  discussion, is that BTI stress profiles with opposite polarities  with respect to the actual operation of a given MOSFET  (namely, NBTI for n-MOSFETs and PBTI for p-MOSFETs)  are generally neglected or mainly addressed in literature to  gather further information about the origin of the degradation  mechanisms in the realistic operation regimes (namely PBTI  for n-MOSFETs and NBTI for p-MOSFETs). The uniqueness of RFET technology in these regards lays in the fact that  all those four scenarios are actual cases in which a device can  be operated. In fact, if a RFET is programmed to work as ntype and operated for a certain amount of time as such, it will  experience some effects related to PBTI stress, that will have  a certain relevance on the shift of its   ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$  . Nevertheless, the  same device could be dynamically reconfigured to work as a

p-type device. This means that the PBTI that previously degraded its n-type features will also have a functional effect on  its p-type features. The same reasoning holds true for the opposite case of a p-type programmed device, stressed in its operational regime (i.e. with NBTI profiles) and then reconfigured to n-type. If all of this is considered together with the  fact that three-gated RFETs present two possible   $\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{T}}$   modes,  characterized by different interfaces (a pure metal-oxidesemiconductor one for the low-  ${\mathbf{}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode and a hybrid metaloxide-metal/metal-oxide-semiconductor one for the high-  ${\mathbf{}}\mathbf{}\mathbf{}\mathbf{}\mathbf{}\mathbf{}^{\mathbf{}}$  mode), a total of eight functional cases of possible BTI degradation scenarios can be identified. In the low-  ${\mathbf{}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{T}}$   operation  mode of a three-gated RFET, the same metal-oxide-semiconductor (MOS) interface system as in MOSFETs is considered. Nevertheless, the conduction phenomenon triggered by  the gate voltage does not rely on the formation of an inversion  channel near the surface, but is related to the band bending  inside a channel that is actually intrinsic [56]. In any case,  since the material system is consistent, we can expect NBTI  to have a strong effect on low-  ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$   operated and p-type programmed devices, similarly to the p-type MOSFET case, giving rise to a significant shift of the threshold voltage towards  more negative values. This type of stress might also induce  on the same device, but programmed as n-type, either a corresponding shift towards more negative values for its   ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$  , in  a sort of  imprint  behavior, or a shift of its  $\mathrm{I_{D}/V_{G}}$   curve in the  direction of more positive values of   $\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{T}}$  , with the n-type and  the p-type transfer characteristics moving apart from each  other. For the same reason, PBTI degradation is assumed to  have less impact for both n-type and p-type programmed low ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$  devices. In the high-  ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$  case, a hybrid metal-oxidemetal/metal-oxide-semiconductor (MOM/MOS) interface is  created, whose combined properties have not been investigated in literature. In fact, it is quite uncommon to find silicides close to the semiconducting channel and therefore below the gate of a transistor, since MOSFETs nowadays rely  on doped raised source/drain areas moving the silicide contact away from the channel [57]. However, the MOS component of the hybrid interface should be influenced by BTI  stress as in the case of the low-  ${\mathbf{}}\mathbf{\cdot}\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode, leaving open the  question about how the MOM component and the interaction  between the two will influence the degradation of the device  electrical properties.

## VI.   R ELIABILITY  A NALYSIS 

Before presenting the results of our analysis, it is important  to illustrate the experimental measurement routines employed. The scope of the experiments was to evaluate the permanent component of a given polarity BTI stress on both ntype and p-type characteristics of a selected device. The first  step of the measurement routine consists in the evaluation of  the pristine I D /V G  n-type and p-type characteristics of the selected device in a specific  ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode: this means that the same  device is first programmed as n-type, via the biasing of its  drain contact with a positive (1 V) voltage, the grounding of  its source contact, and the application of a positive programming voltage (4 V) at its drain side gate (DG). Let us now  assume that the low-  ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode is the targeted mode: in this  case, also the source side gate (SG) is biased at  $4{\mathrm{~V~}}$  , and the  switching input that turns on and off the transistor is applied  to the central gate (CG), sweeping from 0 to 4 V. Once this  characteristic is measured, the same device is programmed in

the p-type polarity, again in the low-  ${\mathbf{}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode, with a  grounded source contact, a negative drain bias (-1 V), negative programming voltages at source and drain side gates (-4  V), and a switching input at the central gate, varying from 0  to -4 V. If the high-V T  mode is considered, the same switching input is applied at the source side gate. Once the pristine  transfer curve is measured, a unipolar constant stress profile  is applied to the gate responsible for the switching of the transistor in the selected  ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode, with a magnitude equal to the  absolute value of the on-state voltage and a sign given by the  chosen BTI polarity (namely, 4 V for PBTI stress profiles and  -4V for NBTI ones). The first stress profile applied runs for  10 s. Since we are only interested in the permanent component of the BTI degradation, a 10 s relaxation time (t r ) is always allowed after each stress period for the devices to recover from the temporary component of the degradation. After this point, a full  $\mathrm{I_{D}/V_{G}}$   curve is measured, in the considered   ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode, and for both polarities, that reveals the effects of  the previously applied stress profile. A sketch representing  the described measurement routine is presented in Fig.3.  Threshold voltages are extracted for each curve following the  same methodology employed for evaluating the   ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$   of the  pristine device. The same stress-relaxation-measurement routine is then repeated for increasing times in order to extract  three points per time decade, until a   $10^{4}$   s cumulative stress  time (t s ) is reached. Using this measurement routine, all eight  possible stress profiles were extracted. The results are further  evaluated only for devices that do not break during the experiments, and that show a constant subthreshold slope (SS).

## VII.   C ONCLUSION 

The study of device reliability is of fundamental importance  for the application of any transistor in any circuit scenario. In  particular, BTI has been shown to have a large impact on the  performances of integrated circuits [61], [62]. Despite this,  reliability concerns are often not addressed for emerging device concepts due to their usually low yield and high variability in laboratory-based technologies. Here, we have performed and presented an early but comprehensive analysis of  the effects of BTI on the degradation of RFETs performances.  Both threshold voltage shift and subthreshold swing degradation were analyzed, giving insights into reliability issues related to the dynamic reconfiguration of the device for the very  first time. In fact, differently than classical MOSFETs,  RFETs are subjected to both PBTI and NBTI stress during  operation. Moreover, the three-gated RFETs analyzed in this  work present different threshold operation modes that are differently influenced by operation under stress. We analyzed  all those features and concluded that both stress modes increase the absolute   ${\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{T}}$   of both operation modes. Moreover,  NBTI generally affects to a greater extent the performances  of RFETs, independently of the   $\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{T}}$   mode in which they are  operated. The analyzed devices showed the tendency to suffer  from a greater degradation in their n-type programmed features. Finally, high-  ${\mathbf{}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{T}}$  operated RFETs are reported to be  more prone to subthreshold slope degradation. Next steps after this early analysis should focus on deriving a full degradation model including voltage, temperature and area scaling.
