## Dopant Analysis of SiC MOSFET with Scanning  Probe Technique   

Ting Zhang, Yan Liu \* ，Anli Yang，Yupu Wang，Ke Xiao, Bing Yang, Qichao Ding, and Mai Zhihong JFS Analytical Centre, Hubei Jiu Feng Shan Laboratory, 9 Jiulonghu Street, Wuhan, Hubei Province 430206, P. R. China  \*Phone: +86 27-   59301999, Email: liuyan@jfslab.com.cn

Abstract —Silicon carbide metal oxide semiconductor  field effect transistor (MOSFET) has the advantages of high  frequency, high withstand voltage, high efficiency, etc., thus  has received widespread attention in recent years. As SiC  MOSFET devices evolve, it becomes increasingly important  to accurately predict the device's electrical performance. To  achieve this, the doping concentration and profile of the  MOSFET active region must be monitored at the nanometer  scale. Scanning Probe Microscopy (SPM) technology is  crucial in semiconductor failure analysis, providing highresolution imaging and surface characterization to identify  defects caused by abnormal doping. Scanning capacitance  microscopy   (SCM),   scanning   diffusion   resistance  microscopy (SSRM) and scanning microwave impedance  microscopy (sMIM) derived from Atomic Force Microscopy  (AFM) have become the current mainstream methods for  two-dimensional doping characterization since they can  provide high-resolution doping information efficiently and  directly. In this article, we characterized the doping  concentration and type, as well as the profile of the SiC  MOSFET by SCM, SSRM and sMIM, through systematic  experiments. And the performance of the three technologies  in SiC MOSFET characterization was compared. In addition,  based on the problems existing in current characterization  methods, we also conducted a series of experiments to  overcome or eliminate their shortcomings, which provides an  important reference for the characterization of SiC  MOSFETs in the future. Appropriate methods can be selected  according to different purposes and needs.

Keywords—SiC, MOSFET, Dopant, SCM, SSRM, sMIM.

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

Silicon carbide metal oxide semiconductor field effect  transistor (SiC MOSFET), as a wide-bandgap semiconductor  (WBG) power device, has the advantages of high frequency,  high withstand voltage, high efficiency, etc. It is widely  demanded in high-voltage applications, so it has attracted  widespread attention [1].

Characterizing the doping quality in SiC MOSFETs is  paramount for optimizing device performance and reliability.  Doping distribution, encompassing dopant concentration and  spatial arrangement, exerts a profound influence on  fundamental electrical properties such as carrier mobility,  conductivity, and threshold voltage. These parameters  directly impact device functionality, efficiency, and  robustness, making precise control and characterization of  doping quality indispensable for achieving desired device  specifications. [2]. Through thorough characterization,  engineers can identify the root causes of failures attributed to

doping-related phenomena and devise targeted corrective  actions to improve device robustness and longevity.

The common doping profile characterization techniques  currently include Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (SIMS),  Passive Voltage Contrast (PVC), and Scanning Probe  Microscopy (SPM). SIMS is a versatile technique extensively  employed in the characterization of dopant profiles within  semiconductor materials. One of its primary advantages lies  in its high sensitivity and ability to provide detailed  information about dopant concentration and depth profiles  with exceptional spatial resolution. Generally, SIMS can  achieve detection limits in the ppm to ppb range for many  elements in semiconductor materials. However, SIMS testing  typically requires larger spot sizes, which limits its  application of doping structures at the nanoscale.  Furthermore, the information obtained by SIMS is usually in  the form of depth profiles, providing 1D information and not  allowing for the direct acquisition of a 2D map of the doping  distribution. Additionally, its destructive nature and complex  sample preparation limit its applicability for non-destructive  analysis [3-4].

PVC technology is a non-destructive analytical technique  used in failure analysis. It operates by detecting voltage  contrasts across conductive layers within integrated circuits  (ICs) and semiconductor devices. This technology is  particularly valuable for identifying electrical defects such as  open or short circuits that may occur during manufacturing or  operation. By visualizing voltage differences with high  sensitivity and resolution, PVC enables engineers to pinpoint  the location of defects within ICs, facilitating precise fault  localization and subsequent corrective actions. [5-7]. It was  noticed that the performance of the detector has a significant  impact on the quality of dopant images, and it may be difficult  to simultaneously obtain the profiles of these doped regions  due to the different barrier levels in n- and p-doped areas,  which require different optimized working distance [8].

SPM offers high-resolution imaging capabilities with  nanometer-scale spatial precision, allowing for direct and  non-destructive measurement of dopant profiles. Moreover,  SPM can furnish semi-quantitative data regarding dopant  concentration and carrier density, facilitating precise  characterization and optimization of devices.

SPM encompasses various techniques such as Scanning  capacitance   microscopy   (SCM),   scanning   diffusion  resistance microscopy (SSRM), and scanning microwave  impedance microscopy (sMIM). These methods, derived  from Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), each possess distinct  principles, along with their respective advantages and

drawbacks, making them valuable tools for characterizing  doping profiles at the nanoscale [9].

This paper outlines our ongoing endeavor to analyze  dopants in SiC MOSFETs using SCM,SSRM and sMIM  .Despite facing numerous challenges in sample preparation,  our  investigation demonstrates the feasibility of employing  SCM, SSRM, and sMIM for dopant analysis in SiC  MOSFETs.

## III.    R ESULT AND  D ISCUSSION   

SCM employs microwave radio frequency (RF) signals to  detect electrical charges within semiconductors, necessitating  the formation of a MOS structure between the conductive  probe and the sample. This structure is typically achieved by  creating a thin oxide layer on the sample surface. By applying  an AC bias voltage to the probe, the dc/dv of the test area can  be determined. As the probe scans regions with varying  doping characteristics, it generates a two-dimensional carrier  distribution map. Presently, SCM is the dominant method for  characterizing doping profiles, offering direct insights into  doping type, concentration, and profile [10]. However,  SCM's drawback lies in its need for additional sample  preparation. Establishing an oxide layer of optimal quality  and thickness is crucial for accurate SCM signals. Unlike  silicon, forming a natural oxide layer with good quality on  SiC proves challenging in ambient conditions. Oxygen  plasma treatment, with appropriate parameters, aids in  achieving reliable SCM signals for doping analysis in SiC.  Nonetheless,   SCM   analysis   predominantly   remains  qualitative due to the difficulty in eliminating stray  capacitance effects [11-12].

Figure 1 shows the results of our experiments in  optimizing the SCM characterization of SiC MOSFET. To  solve the problem that SiC is difficult to form a dielectric  layer in the air, we use oxygen plasma to treat the sample  surface. It can be seen from Fig.1 that for the sample without  oxygen plasma treatment, only the type of doping can be  seen, but the profile of the doped area is barely discernible.  And the SCM signal is significantly improved after the  oxygen treatment. The sample with 15 minutes of oxygen  plasma shows the best profile resolution. The p-doped areas  and the n-doped area can be identified in the active area. After  20 minutes of processing, the resolution of the dopant  distribution in the sample decreased. This may be due to an  excessively long oxygen plasma treatment time, leading to an  increase in oxide layer thickness and consequently reducing  the resolution. The results demonstrate that SCM can well  characterize the doping type and profile of SiC MOSFET  after optimized pre-processing.

We further magnified the SCM results to observe more  doping details. The test results are shown in Figure 2. For  comparison, we provided the PVC results of the same region  in Figure 3. From Figure 2, the doping profiles and  concentration changes of both P-type and N-type are clearly  visible. We selected four P-type doping positions and three  N-type doping positions to extract the electrical signal values  of SCM, as shown in Table 3. It can be observed that the  electrical signals in regions P1-P3 did not show significant  differences, while the value in region P4 was close to zero.

According to the principle of SCM, this suggests that the  concentration in this area may be close to E20 (the detection  upper limit of SCM) or E15 (the detection lower limit of  SCM). Based on experience, this region should have a low  doping concentration. Similarly, for N-type doping, the  differences in SCM electrical signals also indicate changes in  doping concentration. According to Figure 3, the PVC result  shows the doping profile of the sample clearly. However, it  is worth noting that PVC is less sensitive than SCM in  characterizing doping concentration. For example, lowdoped P regions cannot be distinguished in the PVC image.  Similarly, regions with different doping concentrations in the  N region were not identified through image contrast.

SSRM is an extension of tunneling atomic force  microscopy (TUNA), which broadens the allowed current  range of measured resistance and therefore can be used for  semiconductor materials. Because resistance and carrier  concentration have an approximately linear relationship,  unknown regions can be quantified through a sample or  region with a calibrated doping concentration. Compared  with SCM, SSRM has more quantitative advantages. When  the dopant concentration changes by an order of magnitude,  the resistance will also change by an order of magnitude [13].  However, SSRM can only obtain the information of doping  concentration and cannot distinguish the doping type. In  addition, this technique usually needs a hard tip (diamondcoated tip) to break through the oxide layer on the sample  surface to get the intrinsic properties of the sample.  Measuring in an inert atmosphere or vacuum can reduce  surface oxidation and environmental effects, resulting in  more accurate resistance information. For SiC, it is difficult  to obtain a good signal pattern when performing SSRM  testing in air, even for freshly prepared cross-section samples.

sMIM is a technique in near-field SPM technology. In this  method, an AFM cantilever is used as a microwave source  capable of extracting local capacitance (sMIM-C) and  resistance (sMIM-R) information from the echo signal. It is  worth noting that compared with SCM and SSRM, the sMIM  probe has a shielded structure that reduces interference from  the surrounding environment and thus improves electrical  resolution. Its detection limit can be broadened to E14-E20.  In addition, unlike SCM, sMIM does not require direct  electrical contact with the sample, instead, it relies on  capacitive coupling between the probe and the sample, thus  simplifying the sample preparation process [16-17].

However, it's crucial to acknowledge that sMIM is highly  sensitive to surface topography, demanding very careful  sample processing to obtain accurate electrical signals.  Additionally, similar to SSRM, sMIM signals only provide  doping concentration and profile, without the information of  doping type. By applying an AC bias, sMIM can act as SCM.  But this mode also requires a MOS structure. Moreover, the  dc/dv signal is obtained through calculation in this mode,  potentially leading to a reduction in the electrical resolution.

The sMIM results are presented in Figure 5. In Fig5 (a),  the P1, P3 and N2 can be observed, while the P2 and P4  region is relatively difficult to discern. Additionally, due to  interference from surface morphology, the profile of the N1  doped region cannot be well identified. By applying an AC  signal, the SMIM-C results were obtained. As shown in Fig5  (b) and (c), the SMIM-C mode reduces the influence of  morphology,and enhances the doping forfile of the sample.

The SCM, SSRM, and sMIM results of the SiC MOSFET  indicate that SCM provides direct information about the  doping type, concentration, and distribution, with minimal  morphology influence. Good SCM data can be obtained with  a simple oxygen plasma bombardment treatment. However,  the technique has been proven to be challenging for  quantitative analysis, as it can only indicate differences in  doping concentration qualitatively. Additionally, SCM  exhibits a weak response to doping concentrations near  detection limits, necessitating experiential or alternative  methods to determine doping levels. SSRM can only provide  information on doping profiles and concentrations, but its  data is difficult to obtain under atmospheric conditions.  Although surface modification techniques such as surface ion  polishing may improve results, ideal outcomes are still  difficult to be obtained, leading to quantitative analysis  failures. Environmental control may help overcome this  issue. sMIM's sensitivity to surface morphology imposes  strict requirements on surface roughness, as any changes in

morphology are reflected in the sMIM signal. Converting  SMIM signals to sMIM-C can weaken this effect, but the  results are not as effective as SCM. For doping  characterization, a single technique can not provide  comprehensive information. Improving sample preparation,  changing equipment operation environments, and integrating  multiple characterization methods are future directions for  doping characterization.

## IV.    C ONCLUSION   

Doping quality characterization plays an important role in  the failure analysis of SiC MOSFETs. As the most direct  technique for doping profile characterization, the application  of SPM's technique in SiC devices has received wide  attention. In this paper, the SCM SSRM and sMIM  techniques are selected to systematically study and compare  the performance of these three techniques in the  characterization of doping distribution in the cross-section of  SiC MOSFET, and the following conclusions can be drawn.

1.   SCM   is   currently   the   easiest   SiC   doping  characterization technique to achieve. With oxygen plasma  surface treatment, good SCM signals as well as clear  information on doping profiles and concentration changes  can be obtained. However, it is difficult to achieve the   quantitative needs.

2. SSRM performed in air is difficult to obtain good  doping profiles, and even after surface treatment it is still  difficult to obtain the desired results. Although SSRM is  defined as an ideal tool for doping concentration  quantification in an atmosphere-controlled environment, it is  not as effective as SCM in air.

3. Although the probe of sMIM has been improved and  should have higher resolution, it is very sensitive to surface  topography and is prone to interference, resulting in less  effective results than SCM in practical applications.The  acquisition of atomically flat topography is difficult, which  limits sMIM from utilizing its advantages.


In conclusion, each technique has its own advantages and  disadvantages, it is difficult to rely on a single detection  technique to achieve a comprehensive doping analysis, the  future can be improved through the sample preparation  technology, the control of the test environment to achieve a  more accurate doping characterization.
