## Augmenting Metrology: A Framework for  Interactive 3D Data Visualization and Contextual  Analysis in Semiconductor Manufacturing   

Md Ashiqur Rahman Laskar a , Mohammad Zaid Kathadi a , Md Fuad Hossain a , Srijan Chakrabarti a , Filippo  Ozino  Caligaris a , Giuseppe Leonetti b , Gianluca Milano b , Umberto Celano a\*

a  School of Electrical, Computer and Energy Engineering, Arizona State University, Tempe, AZ, 85287 USA  b Advanced Materials Metrology and Life Sciences Division, Instituto Nazionale di Ricerca Metrologica, Torino, Italy

\*Corresponding author: Umberto Celano   Email :  umberto.celano@asu.edu

Abstract  — This work presents the first application of an  Augmented Reality (AR) and Virtual Reality (VR) environment  for the navigation and analysis of complex 3D metrology  datasets in chip manufacturing and debugging. Our platform  overcomes the limitations of traditional 2D data visualization by  facilitating seamless access, navigation, and co-registration of  multi-modal datasets acquired through tomographic scanning  probe microscopy, 3D rendering software, and finite element  simulations. Our goal is to demonstrate the functionality and  advantages of such a platform for the analysis of complex 3D  datasets, typically generated during the process of failure  analysis (FA). First, we describe our platform with details for  the access and navigation of existing datasets. Second, we offer  the seamless presentation of various co-registered datasets  acquired by a combination of tomographic scanning probe  microscopy, multiple FA techniques, and 3D finite elements  software simulations. Our first-person perspective enables the  enhancement of data navigation for each of these tasks.  For  example, with the simultaneous observation of static 2D  information about the sample under study e.g., graphic data  stream (GDS) floorplan or scanning electron microscopy  (SEM), while offering 3D interactive inspection of an inherently  tomographic dataset.

Keywords—    Augmented Reality, Augmented Metrology,  Virtual reality for semiconductors, AR-VR data navigation,  tomographic AFM.

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

Semiconductor manufacturing thrives on precision.  Every nanometer deviation can jeopardize the functionality  of the final product, necessitating robust metrology for in-line  and post-production defect detection and analysis.[1] While  traditional data visualization tools offer valuable insights,  they often fall short in providing an intuitive, contextual  understanding of complex metrology datasets. Moreover, as  a larger number of dedicated metrology equipment is  generating native three-dimensional tomographic dataset, the  need for improved data navigation has become a clear priority  of the failure analysis community. This gap presents an  opportunity for Augmented Reality (AR) and virtual reality  (VR) to design systems that can revolutionize how we  interact with and interpret semiconductor metrology data. We  believe this is the ultimate pathway leading to faster  identification of critical issues and improved decisionmaking. Particularly, correlative environments for multiscale and multi-modal analysis require an intuitive  integration of images from any sources, including but not

limited direct inspection tools, finite element simulations, and  various imaging methods.

Moved by this, we have started to interface tomographic  dataset with AR and VR environments, and we report here on  the first immersive metrology data analysis flow merging  tomographic atomic force microscopy (AFM), with electrical  data, and finite elements simulations. Our platform can  navigate 3D interactive tomograms while comparing data  from other FA characterization methods, thus offering instant  feedback on the device under study. The result is not only an  immersive environment for scientific data navigation, but it  is a robust tool for analysis and visualization of data that can  yield improved interpretation of device physics, and FA  experience.

## II.   VALENCE CHANGE MEMORY :  A CASE STUDY 

As a case study, here we focus on the integration of the  analysis flow for resistive switching memory devices. Widely  adopted by the community for application in storage,  neuromorphic, and in-memory computing, these devices  continue to attract the attention of the community to address  existing reliability issues that are often limiting their  application.[2] Here, valence changes memory (VCM) cells  are used as a reference material system exploiting the  conductive filament (CF) inside a thin NbOx layer as the  structure to test our platform and 3D data navigation and  reconstruction. The VCM cells are fabricated by growing 15  nm of   $\mathrm{NbO_{x}}$   with anodic oxidation, starting from a metallic  Nb film (i.e.,   $250~\mathrm{~nm}$   thick). Chemical and structural  characterization of the   $\mathrm{NbO_{x}}$   films can be found in previous  works.[3] Electron-beam lithography is used to pattern thin  Au electrodes with diameter 500 nm and thickness 10 nm.  Like others VCM cell the device allows for reversible and  controlled formation of the conductive filament tunable by  the operating current, which is then employed for  tomographic reconstruction. Therefore, when programmed in  low-resistive-state (LRS), this device contains an nm-sized  object with a relatively high conductivity embedded into an  insulating matrix. In this configuration, the top electrode is  accessible to the AFM tip, that can be used to stress  electrically the VCM, as previously reported for the case of  several memory devices.[3] For the analytical method, we use  our 3D sensing methodology which is based on the use of  contact-mode atomic tip-induced material removal, where  abrasive wear has been re-imagined as a valid option to  achieve tomographic sensing (scalpel SPM).[4] The  experimental use of this approach has found successful  application when combining AFM with various secondary  sensing modes, e.g., electric current, resistance, capacitance,  enabling the characterization of electrical properties of thin  dielectrics, solar cells and piezoelectric materials, to name but  a few.[5, 6] Fig. 2 shows the schematic of the device under  study, with details from multiple analytical methods that we  used to collect information on the device fabrications  including, Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and  conductive atomic force microscopy (C-AFM) are presented  to qualify the device fabrication (Fig. 2a,b). The procedure  for CF’s reconstruction is shown in Fig. 2c. The measurement  starts by setting the desired state of resistance in the VCM  cell, and later by removing the top electrode (Au) to acquire  in-depth profiles of the CF with the method of scalpel SPM.  The electrical profiles presented in Fig. 2d are acquired in the  location of a dot cell (cfr. AFM image in Fig. 2a,b), after the  Au electrode is removed. All profiles are collected at the  same location while removing with our diamond coated probe  (CDT-NCHR,  $\mathbf{k}{=}92\;\mathrm{N/m}$  ) a total depth of 15 nm. By stacking  the obtained dataset, we can infer the shape and size of the  CF and prepare for a 3D reconstruction by layers  interpolation.

At this point of the analysis, the process of correlating  physical, electrical, and 3D datasets becomes important and  complex. For example, the user could find benefits from the  correlation between the electrical information of the CF, its  resistance state, the current involved in the switching, and the  3D shape and size. Similarly, the 3D tomogram can be best  inspected only if its reconstructed shape, size, and  dimensions, can be manipulated, typically not possible in a  static report of the analysis.

In Fig. 3 we show how to access this information in a  non-static way through our AR-VR metrology platform. VR  and AR user’s views of the scene are visible in Fig. 3. Here,  the same headset view can display at the same time static  information of the prior FA analysis, including electrical IVs, SEM, or XPS, alongside the reconstructed 3D tomogram  (i.e., grey polyhedron). For compatibility with existing  headsets, and to test both AR and VR experiences we  developed our data navigation with both Hololens 2  (Microsoft) and Meta Quest 3 (Meta). Clearly, the VR user

has access to a virtual room using a headset immersing them  in the data analysis environment, while the AR experience is  collocated with the surrounding environment (Fig. 3, left  inset). For both user’s the displaying experience includes  access to the tomogram navigation a gesture similar to a  pinch-to-zoom function on a touch screen; offer the user  holds the controller grip buttons and moves their hands in to  scale their size up and moves their hands out to scale their  size down. The user can interact with the tomogram using  simple touch commands to zoom, rotate and slice through the  tomogram in 3D space.

The 3D structure obtained by the interpolation of the  dataset in Fig. 2d is a complex 3D geometry visualizing the  inner structure of the VCM cell in the low-resistance-state,  i.e., conductive filament.  Note, for the sake of visualization  we emphasize contrast in the z-direction, i.e., the 15 nm thick   $\mathrm{NbO_{x}}$  is effectively shown as   $50~\mathrm{~nm}$  , ca. a 3 times  magnification.

Multiple works have shown how shape, size and  geometry of the CF are key elements to interpret the device  operation in VCMs.[2, 7] Therefore, the direct observation in  correlation with electrical measurements such as switching IVs and other physical analysis is used here as a valuable  example. Fig. 4a shows an example where we allow covisualization in the same environment of structural, chemical,  and electrical information respectively with the view of Xray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS), local electrical  information obtained from C-AFM, reconstructed 3D dataset  from scalpel SPM, among others.

Here, the user traverses the space in a virtual  environment that can be loaded with multiple static analysis  reports to correlated with the 3D dataset. Fig. 4a includes  energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), SEM, current-voltage  (I-V) spectroscopy etc. Meanwhile, the inspection of the 3D  tomographic dataset can be performed in VR exploiting some  of the main advantages such as, active resizing, tomographic  data slicing, dimensional measurements, to name but a few.  These manipulations allow a user (Fig. 4b) to not only  visualize the tomogram in 3D but to choose different angles

and zoom conditions to maximize the value when confronting  tomographic information with other FA read outs. A clear  example is reported in Fig.4c, where we report on the  possibility of using a 3D mesh generated from a portion of  tomographic dataset of the reconstituted filament as the input  for a finite element thermal analysis flow using ANSYS.

In this area, multiple works have discussed the importance of  studying thermal effects of operating VCM cells to  understand and control variability.[8] However, it remains  complex to perform the simulations on real experimental  dataset of the filament’s shape, and even more on a complete  3D reconstruction of the CF. Fig. 4c shows an example where  the scalpel SPM tomogram is used to generate a usable 3D  mesh for thermal analysis simulations. The treatment of the  results from the thermal analysis and thermal effects inside  the   $\mathrm{NbO_{x}}$   CF is clearly beyond the scope of this work.  However, we emphasize how the user has the ability to  compare the position and orientation of both the original and  simulation results, thus visualizing data from multiple 3D  sources in our AR-VR platform. Here, for reference, heat  transfer in the top part of the CF is shown while applying a 1  V d.c. voltage across the two electrodes (with a limit up to   $100\,\mathrm{\mA}$  ), thus mimicking some basics operations of the  filament while electronic current flows in the LRS state.

## III.   C HALLENGES FOR  AR  AND  VR  ENVIRONMENTS 

Given the clear advantages, in our holistic view future  data navigation systems for metrology, FA, and process  control, should be executed in some form of AR or VR

environments. However, many challenges currently exist in  visualizing these datasets in a single point-of-access. First, in  order to develop methods that can seamlessly import and preprocess metrology data from various sources, data integration  should be improved or designed with AR-VR navigation  systems in mind. At present, complex file format and  metadata   require   extensive   data   treatment   before  visualization. Second, the information encoded in the raw  files, for example the current encoded in the pixels of our CAFM images, is often obfuscated, or encoded with  proprietary formats by tools vendor, limiting our ability to  merge multiple streams of data in a single environment.  Third, AR-VR visualization, in order to design interactive 3D  visualization of the metrology data overlay for an intuitive  understanding of the information contained in the voxels.  Finally, on the side of platform development, Fig. 3 presents  the comparison between AR and VR data navigation systems.  Clearly, each option can have pros and cons, and the choice  for one option is still very application specific. We limit to  emphasize that both platforms would benefit from a stronger  integration with existing libraries for data analysis, and  computer visions such as those available through Phyton with  libraries like OpenCV and others. We also observe that the  user can find it difficult to maintain a sense of scale and  dimensions without familiar references when comparing  multiple inputs. Future works will target two areas of  improvement, namely (1) the direct access to online data  streams from functioning tools, and (2) a speech-based  integration with Python libraries for the fast integration of  machine learning algorithms and active data manipulation.

## IV.   C ONCLUSIONS   

In summary, this work explores the integration of AR  and VR into the semiconductor manufacturing workflow for  enhanced metrology data analysis. We discussed recent  advances in the development of a dedicated platform for the  navigation of tomographic dataset. Given the growing  complexity of correlative metrology in the semiconductor  industry, i.e., the needs for multiple readouts to obtain a  quantitively decision, we think AR and VR will have a  critical role in FA and materials characterization.   In tandem  with existing multi-scale, multi-modal software for images  navigation (e.g., Atlas), we believe that this is an effective  way to interface with complex metrology tools, such as  modern microscopes. Starting from a simplified analysis flow  based on VCM cells, where we merged SEM, C-AFM and IVs electrical characteristics with 3D tomographic AFM and  finite element simulations. Furthermore, we reported on the  visualization of 3D tomograms in AR and VR, by comparing  two navigation systems.  As a result, we were able to show  the merging of different analytical capabilities into a single  immersive environment. The current interface allows the user  to access data off-line, but future works are focusing on the  direct access of on-line metrology data for the direct access  to reconstruction capability in real-time for the tomogram  generation.
