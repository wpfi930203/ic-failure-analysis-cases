## Application of EBAC on Small Nets  

Satish Kodali   , Esther P. Y. Chen   Electrical Failure Analysis, GLOBALFOUNDRIES Inc,400 Stone Break Rd Extension, Malta, NY, US -12020  Phone : (001) 518-491-1533, Email :  satish.kodali@globalfoundries.com

Abstract —   EBAC is a very well understood technique. It is  usually implemented on long traces where physical inspections  are challenging. It is also implemented on short traces where a  suspected resistive path is expected. In the current work, two  very interesting cases where EBAC analysis implemented on  much smaller length traces are presented.

Keywords—EBAC, resistive contacts, small nets, soft fails   I NTRODUCTION

EBAC (Electron Beam Absorbed Current) is a well know  technique implemented in failure analysis laboratories [1-5].  Typically, the technique is used to isolate the fails along a long  scan logic trace. This method would precisely narrow down  the inspection area and layer of interest for defect  identification. Hard failures, like complete metal line opens,  can easily be identified by one probe EBAC technique.  Resistive failures cannot be isolated by this method. Two  probe EBAC is the preferred technique to identify resistive  failures. Introduction of second probe gives the SEM incident  electrons two paths. The electrons always prefer the least  resistance path avoiding resistive failure. EBAC amplifier  tracking one of the probe signals can identify the failure  location precisely.  In most instances top side delaying cannot  provide two probe access to a callout. Backside sample  preparation is key to gaining access to thoroughly inspect any  potential resistive open fail in each driver to receiver paths.   This paper discusses two interesting cases of EBAC  application on short nets where the defects would have been  very difficult to be found otherwise.

The backside sample is prepared by removing the  passivation and the first backend metallization. The sample is  then mounted face down on a thin glass slide with epoxy and  conductive Ni filler followed by curing the epoxy in an oven  for 15 -20 mins. The sample is then grinded to remove most  of the bulk silicon. The remaining thin silicon is chemically   removed using TMAH solution. The subsequent sample is  hand polished to remove the STI oxide and gates to expose the  active and gate contacts. Fig. 5 shows the schematic  representation of the backside sample at this stage. Single  probe EBAC can identify a hard open in the BEOL. Two  probe EBAC is very good at identifying resistive contacts  which are hard to be perceived with single point method.

In this technique, one probe (signal probe) connects the  driver of callout to EBAC amplifier signal, while the other  probe (ground probe) connects EBAC ground to a receiver of  the callout. This gives the SEM incident electrons on the net  two potential routes. One through the signal probe and the  other through the ground probe. The signal probe path can be  perceived through the EBAC amplifier. If the callout trace is  free from defects, there is a direct short between the signal and  ground probes with no output to the EBAC amplifier. But if  there is a resistive open, the path from the defect to the signal  probe will be perceived through the amplifier. The subsequent  EBAC signal provides the exact location of the high resistance  point in the callout. The process is repeated on each driver to  receiver paths.

Upon thorough inspection on  the driver side, a subtle short of the active contact to the Via 0  ground rail was identified as shown in Fig. 8. A subtle active  contact mis alignment towards Via 0 ground rail is also  identified. TEM analysis is performed to further understand  the root cause. Figure 8 also shows the direction of TEM cut  to perceive the defect.

Fig. 9 shows the TEM analysis confirming the active contact  to Via 0 ground rail runner short. The driver output for Net 1  is corrupted by the short to ground rail leaving the receiver  gate in an indeterminate state. This is the root cause of the  PEM signal generated during the fault isolation.

Due to the normal tapered profile of the Via 0 and the  location of the short, it is very unlikely to identify the failure  during regular top-down inspections. Backside sample  preparation and EBAC analysis can narrow down the failing  net to identify the root cause. Retrospectively, frontside single  probe EBAC would have identified a short to the power or  ground rails. But it would still be hard to perceive the short  from the top-down view. Several repeater and non- repeater  units analyzed also identified the same failure mode.

Implementing appropriate OPC (Optical Proximity  Corrections) and correcting the marginal misalignment  identified on the active contacts fixed this systematic edge/  donut yield loss. EBAC and backside sample preparation is  instrumental in identifying this specific fail mode.

## D ISCUSSION AND  C ONCLUSION  

Two interesting cases of EBAC analysis are presented in the  current work. In the first case, the standard layer by layer  inspection technique was proven to not isolate a subtle but  hard fail. EBAC along with backside sample view was  instrumental in identifying the failure. In the second case, even  though the defect is very much localized, it is important to  confirm the presence of the defect and to identify TEM cut  direction precisely without exposing the defect. It is also  important to use low kV imaging to minimize unwanted beam  damage artifacts. Two probe EBAC was successfully able to  isolate the fail to a metal runner to perform subsequent TEM  analysis. The paper emphasizes the importance of leveraging  on EBAC technique when normal process may not provide  the best outcomes to identify fails.
