## Accurate Unknown Identification of Dark Colored  Contamination with Sub-Micron Infrared (O-PTIR)  and Reflection Mode FT-IR Spectroscopies  

Michael K. F. Lo, Eoghan P. Dillon   Photothermal Spectroscopy Corp, 325 Chapala St., Santa Barbara, CA, USA  Phone : (+1) 805 845-6568, Email:  mike@photothermal.com

Abstract —Accurate molecular analysis of rough, darkcolored contamination particles extracted from the crosssectioned underfill region is usually a challenging task with  conventional spectroscopic tools.  Combining conventional  reflection mode Fourier-transform infrared (r-FTIR) and submicron   infrared   spectroscopies   (Optical   PhotoThermal  InfraRed or O-PTIR), the chemistry near the surface and of the  bulk can be effectively evaluated.  The combined analysis yields  a highly specific set of chemical information for pinpointing the  potential   origin   of   contamination   particles   in   the  microelectronic package.

Keywords—sub-micron, infrared, contamination, O-PTIR,  underfill, reflection, FT-IR, Raman

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

Accurate   identification   of   foreign   matters   and  contamination has been the holy grail of microelectronics  failure analysis engineers and scientists working in both the  front-end and back-end processes.  Devices become more  powerful just as they are miniaturized into complex and spacesaving packages, in the likes of system-in-package (SiP),  2.5D/3D integration, glass-based interposers, chip-on-waferon-silicon (COWOS), etc.  As a result, the ROI of  contamination becomes smaller, thus requiring higher  sensitivity and higher spatial resolution than offered by  conventional FT-IR spectroscopy.

Identification of unknown with FT-IR spectroscopy is  further complicated when measurements are conducted in  reflection mode, especially for irregularly shaped surfaces that  scatter IR radiation.  For a perfectly flat organic surface,  reflectance at normal incidence is approximately  $4\%$   at non

absorbing wavenumbers [1].  On rough, non-flat surfaces,  scattering becomes more obvious, and overall reflectance  reduces, thus weakening infrared spectral intensity, as well as  distorting infrared band shapes. Both qualities reduce the  likelihood of accurately identifying the unknown.

At strong infrared absorption bands, such as carbonyl,  reflectance significantly varies at the same infrared band  positions due to its strong coupling to wavenumber-dependent  refractive indices.  Without applying appropriate correction  algorithms (Kramers-Koenig for specular reflection or  Kubelka-Munk for diffuse reflectance), those specific bands  would assume derivative shapes that are shifted relative to the  actual infrared spectrum. Frequently, specimens in FA are  complex and can contain powder-like compositions, thus  similar to diffuse-reflectance FT-IR measurements [1].  While  it is possible to make reasonable corrections to these distorted  spectra, dark-colored material absorbs infrared light very  strongly over a broad mid-infrared range and would lower  reflectivity than most organic matters.  Such a characteristic  further reduces the likelihood of correctly identifying the  unknowns due to low signal-to-noise.  While this technique  could be greatly challenged by sub-  $\cdot20\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$   particles, it still can  provide useful information when particles are sufficiently  large   $(<100\,\upmu\mathrm{m})$  . For this contribution, we will generally refer  to this data collection mode as r-FTIR.

Attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessories could be  employed as a viable alternate operation mode for FT-IR  microscopes as they provide a higher spatial resolution due to  their usage of a high refractive indexed germanium (Ge)  crystal pressed against the ROI surface. Depending on the  angle of incidence, the penetration depth is in the order of half  to   $1\ \upmu\mathrm{m}$   [1]; but in doing so, the Ge probe would permanently  alter and/or cross-contaminate the specimen. Since this  technique requires perfect contact between the specimen and  the ATR crystal, any imperfections would lead to distortion of  infrared absorption bands that standard ATR corrections will  not sufficiently account for.

Reflection mode operated Raman microspectroscopy  could provide non-contact analysis but has been largely  ineffective due to the strong auto-fluorescence (AF) baseline  and low damage threshold of many dark-colored specimens  [2]. AF typically originates from the aromatic components in  the organics, which usually absorb the photons from the  visible laser light and emit a stoke-shifted emission in longer  wavelengths.  Epoxies, phenolic resins, polyimides, solder  masks, and coloring agents represent typical compounds with  strong auto-fluorescence.  The visible excitation laser light  would also induce laser damage on these dark-colored  samples without yielding meaningful chemical information  about the FM.  Such “double-losses” strongly discourage the  use of Raman spectroscopy for many of the contamination

investigations [2].  Still, it does remain a viable alternative for  analyzing weakly auto-fluorescent samples.

Until recently, chemical analysis of foreign matters (FM)  could be enhanced with the availability of sub-micron spatial  resolution   optical   photothermal   infrared   (O-PTIR)  spectroscopic and imaging systems. O-PTIR may also be  referred to as a close variant of the mid-infrared photothermal  (MIP) technique [3].  This novel infrared-based technique has  demonstrated non-destructive spectroscopic extraction of  chemical information from very small (sub-micron) dark ROI,  even when metal layers in close proximity are present in the  cross-section [4,5,6,7].  This technique operates on the  principle of a pump-probe arrangement, where the pump is  referred to as the pulsed infrared laser source for exciting the  specimen, and a separate 532 nm visible constant wave (CW)  laser acting as the probe (Fig. 2).  Both the infrared and visible  lasers are co-linearized before being focused onto the sample  surface at respective diffraction-limited spot sizes.  Where the  material absorbs the infrared light, localized transient  photothermal   effect   (including   mirage   effect   and  photothermal   expansion)   would   commence   over   its  diffraction-limited spot.  When the IR laser is off, the  photothermal effects would also be off.  The CW-operated  probe laser would monitor the laser reflectivity differences  between the on-off states, which would be equivalent to the  intensity of the photothermal effect at a given wavenumber  [3].  For the infrared pump, spot sizes could vary depending  on the infrared wavelengths (\~3.3 to   $12.5\;\upmu\mathrm{m})$  ; but the probe's  visible light would be focused to approximately less than 1   $\upmu\mathrm{m}$  .  This property enables a sub-micron spot size toward the  detection of localized photothermal effects within the focused  visible laser spot.

The experimental conditions employed by the O-PTIR  technique satisfy the requirement of a non-destructive  analysis.  The use of a pulsed infrared laser as an excitation  source and a visible laser as a probe beam, which constitute  the pump-probe arrangement, significantly reduces the power  density requirement in the probe laser intensity necessary for  high signal-to-noise spectroscopic acquisition.  As a result, the  original state of the specimen could be easily preserved for  future secondary analyses.  Since this novel instrument  provides spectral shapes in close agreement with conventional  FT-IR spectroscopy, spectral analysis can be amicably  compared to existing FT-IR spectroscopic databases to  identify the unknown [8].  This latter quality enables rapid  identification using both commercially available and customcompiled infrared databases.  The O-PTIR technique is  typically sensitive to the top few micrometers [9,10] to yield

spectra with “normal” infrared band shapes.  On the contrary  with r-FTIR, the depth of penetration of an absorbing species  in the infrared would be dependent on the infrared absorption  spectral profile, e.g. in the order of  ${\sim}10~\upmu\mathrm{m}$   for the strongest  absorption band.  The probe depth would be even deeper for  the weaker IR bands; therefore, r-FTIR is more naturally  suited for bulk property measurement instead of probing  surface chemistries.  In other words, spectral features of  smaller or thinner components may be overshadowed by the  bulk material and obscure material identification.

Unlike Raman spectroscopy, O-PTIR is not at all  influenced by fluorescence and auto-fluorescence properties  upon laser excitation.  The reflected probe laser encoding the  photothermal signals is quantified in the photodiode detector  only when there is a change in the infrared-induced  photothermal response.  As a result, the signal-to-noise of OPTIR can be very high, even for sub-micron-sized polymeric  absorbers [4]. Further, recent technical advances have enabled  the use of auto-fluorescence imaging on dark-colored  specimens for spatially differentiating the heterogenous  mixtures [6,7]. Such novel capability expedites the  identification of foreign matters and contaminations by  providing a distribution map of the chemical species without  knowing the exact chemical makeup of the unknown.

For many recent FM and contamination analyses, the  available lateral surface area may be as small as one to a few  micrometers [4-7].  In nearly all those cases, FT-IR has not  provided useful chemical information.  On the other end, in  cases where the contamination is sufficiently larger than the  diffraction-limited spot size of mid-infrared waves (e.g.  greater than   $100~\mathrm{~\textmu m}$   on the xy plane direction), the  combination of O-PTIR and r-FTIR spectroscopies may  synergistically offer additional insights into the source of the  contamination, which would have been unavailable  previously.  In this contribution, we will demonstrate a special  case when we could carefully combine the surface sensitivity  of O-PTIR spectroscopy and the bulk analysis offered by rFTIR for identifying a rough-surfaced unknown foreign  material in the underfill. The unique combination of the  components revealed by such combined analysis provides  probable sources of contamination that enhance the credibility  of the said analysis without additional sample preparation.

## II.   E XPERIMENTAL 

The sample has been cross-sectioned to reveal the extent  of the foreign matter (FM).  Using a clean needle, the FM has  been extracted from the region of interest embedded within  the underfill (UF) resulting in a particle that has an end-to-end  dimension of approximately   $100~\upmu\mathrm{m}$  , which is subsequently  transferred onto the copper adhesive tape.  The extracted  sample is then placed directly onto the sampling stage of an     O-PTIR microscope (mIRage™, Santa Barbara, USA)  equipped with a quantum cascade laser (QCL) that tunes from  2990 – 2700 and   $1800-950~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$   with spectral stepping of 2   $\mathrm{cm^{\cdot1}}$  .  The optical view of the sample is focused through the  reflection-based Cassegrain objective with a 0.78 numerical  aperture (NA). An ultra-sensitive photodiode detector for low  levels of visible light has been used for sensing photothermal  signals to avoid sample degradation. The visible probe laser is   $532\,\mathrm{\nm}$   and operated in the constant wave (CW) mode.   Wavenumber accuracy and spectral quality of the O-PTIR  microscope are verified by measuring and comparing the OPTIR spectrum with the conventional FT-IR spectrum of  polystyrene.  For r-FTIR spectral acquisition, the sample is

placed onto the sample stage in a conventional FT-IR  microscope (Thermo Fisher iN10, Waltham, MA, USA), and  spectra are collected in the reflection mode with   $4~\mathrm{{cm}^{-1}}$    spectral resolution through an aperture size of   $\sim50~\upmu\mathrm{{m}}$   and  averaged over 16 scans.  For O-PTIR spectra, spectral analysis  and de-mixing analysis are conducted with KnowItAll 2024  (Wiley Science Solution, Hoboken, NJ, USA); all analyses  have been conducted without baseline correction.

Combining the interpretation of bulk r-FTIR and surfacesensitive O-PTIR spectra provides significant benefits in  analyzing complex unknowns.  Inspecting for minor  spectroscopic details, the weaker infrared absorption bands at  1426 and   $\bar{13}30\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$   are also present in Spectrum B, consistent  with PVC as a tertiary component to PTFE and epoxy.  The  shoulder at   $1244\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$   on the high wavenumber side in  Spectrum B is likely a result of combining epoxy’s aromatic  C-O stretch and PVC’s C-H in-plane deformation [11]. With  further evaluation of the r-FTIR spectrum (Spectrum A, Fig  5), several additional features can be deduced.  The strong IR  absorption of the dark PVC has mostly dominated the spectral  composition; the penetration of IR light should be mostly  extinguished within about   $10~\ensuremath{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$   from the top surface at the  strong absorption bands [1]; the infrared waves away from  these strong absorption bands would travel deeper into the  specimen.  For this reason, the typically small bands would  appear to be larger than when taken as a uniform thin film in  the specular reflection arrangement [1].  Further inspection of  Spectrum A suggests a shoulder near   $1156\ensuremath{\,\mathrm{\cm^{\bar{1}}}}$  may be  consistent with a minor contribution from the   $\mathrm{.CF_{2}}.$  - symmetric  stretch of PTFE.   The minimal overlap of PTFE’s absorption  band at   $1156\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$   against PVC enables the observation of the  former as a shoulder.  Given the very strong signal from PTFE,  the surface-sensitive O-PTIR data portrays the contamination  as a PTFE-dominated fragment with a small contribution from  PVC.  In essence, both the r-FTIR and O-PTIR have  spectroscopic components of both PVC and PTFE.  The  highly sensitive O-PTIR would also suggest the presence of  epoxy that r-FTIR could not reveal. Using this step-by-step  process, the identity of the unknown can be systematically  deduced, starting from the large bands in major components  to minor components.

Taking observations from surface-sensitive O-PTIR and  bulk-oriented   r-FTIR   spectroscopies   for   two   major  components (PTFE and PVC) in the dark fragment, it is now  possible to significantly narrow down the potential sources of  contamination.  We now have a short list of such  contamination most relevant to microelectronic packaging in  the manufacturing process: fragment of a PTFE tube with  PVC sheathing for gas delivery in a chemically aggressive  environment [12], or threaded PVC pipe fittings with PTFE  tape seal.

## C ONCLUSION 

A combined analysis of O-PTIR and reflection mode FTIR has been demonstrated to reveal the complex chemical  composition of a dark-colored unknown fragment extracted  from a microelectronics packaging.  Such analysis becomes  available when the specimen is just large enough for acquiring  an r-FTIR spectrum with sufficient SNR; for this study, the   $100~\upmu\mathrm{m}$   contamination was extracted from the underfill area  presented on the cross-section.  With the bulk-sensitive rFTIR revealing a major component of PVC and surfacesensitive O-PTIR suggesting a PTFE derivative, the combined  analysis provides a highly valuable set of tools to resolve the  most complex contamination.  In this demonstration, we have  also made use of the weaker infrared absorption bands from  respective methods to improve the confidence in the analysis.   In doing so, meaningful deduction of unknown material can  be reasonably exploited to pinpoint potential sources of  contamination.
