## Study on Different Failure Mechanisms of  MOSFET Caused by Different Oxide Defects in  14nm FinFET IC  

Yanfen Wang, Shijun Zheng, Shan Zhang, Junsheng Wang, Guang Lu, Gaojie Wen   Failure Analysis Laboratory, NXP Semiconductors (Tianjin) Ltd, No.15 Xinghua Avenue, Xiqing District, Tianjin, China  Phone: (86)15022607178, Email: yanfen.wang@nxp.com

Abstract —With the application of FinFET technology,  especially as FinFET technology nodes reach 14nm and beyond,  we will see more and more front-end-of-line (FEOL) defects at  the transistor level, rather than back-end-of-line (BEOL)  interconnection defects. These minor defects often have a  significant impact on the yield and reliability of the final  chipsets. This paper presents two different types of FEOL  defects in 14nm FINFET technology. (Finding the approximate  location by EFA, further fault isolation down to MOSFET by  nanoprobe, finding the physical defect by TEM analysis). Both  types of FEOL defects result in leakage of MOSFETs. We  studied the different failure mechanisms and the possible  mechanism of formation. Finally, it was concluded that both of  them were probably caused by the oxide defect in different steps  of the FEOL process. One NMOS leakage case was gate leakage,  and the failure mechanism was dielectric-breakdown-induced  epitaxy (DBIE), which may be caused by an Oxide bottom  interface layer (BIL) defect. Another PMOS leakage case was  mainly Drain and Source short, and the failure mechanism was  the Ge bridge defect, which may be caused by the Oxide Etch  Stop Layer (ESL) defect going through a series of subsequent  process steps. It provided evidence for the fab process, and  yield-quality improvement in 14nm FinFET. Also, it was  instructive and meaningful to the FinFET failure analysis.

Keywords—14nm, FinFET, front-end, oxide, nanoprobe,  leakage, TEM, DBIE, Ge bridge

## I.    INTRODUCTION 

In the FinFET FEOL process, there will be new complex  defect types due to its 3D stereoscopic structure. This 3D  structure presents challenges for manufacturers to improve  quality and reduce defect rates at advanced nodes. Also, these  new complex defect types present challenges for the failure  analysis. For the failure analysis of 14nm FinFET ICs, the  Tester generally screens the failed vector, and after that,  Diagnosis is used for finding the signals of suspicion.  Furthermore, through Photon Emission Microscope (PEM),  Electro-Optical Frequency Mapping (EOFM), Electro-optical  probing (EOP), Dynamic Analysis by laser stimulation  (DALS), Laser Assisted Device Alteration (LADA) to narrow  down the scope of the failure to one cell or several cells, and  then scanning electron microscope (SEM) / passive voltage  contrast (PVC) and nanoprobe are used to gradually narrow  down and eventually find the failed device. Based on the  nanoprobe test results, the Transmission electron microscope  (TEM) / Scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM)  analysis scheme including the Planar view TEM (PV-TEM)  plan and cross-sectional TEM (X-TEM) plan are developed  and the defect is finally found. This paper presents two  different types of  FEOL defects in 14nm FinFET IC which  cause leakage of MOSFETs and then affect the entire  function. After further research on the failure mechanism and

the formation mechanism, they are all caused by the oxide  defect.

## II.   EXAMPLES   OF   FAILURE   CASE  

## Case 1 :  

First, this SCAN Chain failure was replicated by Tester for  failure verification. The diagnosis revealed one signal on the  chain which matched with scan failure.   PEM analysis revealed  3 emission spots on the diagnosis area. After the circuit  analysis, we found that the PEM spots were on the clock path  of this diagnostic scan cell. Then EOFM analysis didn’t find  abnormal spots. EOP analysis of the upstream/downstream of  the net under suspicion revealed that the wrong data first  appeared on the signal ../ buffer4_reg_0_/nm.   And it was  caused by an anomaly in the clock signal. Then nanoprobe IV measurements were performed. In Fig. 1, for location2, we  focused on the inverter of this block and confirmed the  leakage in the NMOS region of the inverter, as shown in Fig.  2 (a). The leakage matched with the electrical failure perfectly.  Therefore, the following analysis focused on  location2.  EBIRCH analysis was performed and an abnormal EBIRCH  spot was caught, as shown in Fig. 2 (b). Then PV-TEM and  EDS analysis didn’t find obvious anomalies in the leakage  region. XTEM was performed along the direction of the gate.  An incomplete Oxide BIL layer between the fin and the highk layer was observed, as shown in Fig. 3.

This failure mechanism was dielectric-breakdowninduced epitaxy (DBIE). Fig. 3 (a) was a high-resolution  TEM (HRTEM) image of the reference fin. The Oxide BIL  was intact, complete, and uniform in thickness. The thickness  of the oxide layer was about 1nm. Fig. 3 (b) was the HRTEM  image of the target defective fin. Fig. 3 (c) and Fig. 3 (d) were  the zoom-in images corresponding to the reference fin and  target defective fin, respectively. The gate dielectric layer  (Oxide BIL) was obviously damaged, and silicon epitaxy  occurred. Si atoms protruded from the fin and touched with  the high-k layer of the metal gate. Fig. 3 (e) and (f) were  STEM images of the defective fin. Fig. 3 (g) was the EDS  mapping image of the oxygen element which contained the  reference fin and the defective fin. The amount of oxygen  element signal acquisition decreased significantly at the  DBIE location. Above all, it caused the leakage from Gate  (…/latch/neck ) to Source (VSS) /Drain (…/net1885463) on  NMOS. Then it caused functional failure.  Eventually, it  caused a complete electrical failure. The formation of DBIE  was probably caused by the low quality of Oxide BIL. For  example, DBIE would probably occur at the location where  the oxide layer is weak, provided that the oxide layer is not  uniform.

## Case 2:  

It was a Pin short case failure analysis. OBIRCH spot was  detected at the pass transistor PMOS which was a bunch of  PMOS connected in series. Nanoprobe I-V measurement  results on the Via0 layer shown short between Drain and  Source at 8 adjacent PMOSFETs, as shown in Fig. 4 (a). The

current was   $19{\sim}87{\upmu}{\mathrm{A}}@0.1\mathrm{V}.$  . Gate leakage was also detected.  See Fig. 4 (b). The current was around   ${\sim}400\mathrm{nA@}0.1\mathrm{V}$  .  Leakage between Source and Drain was the most severe. Then  the PV-TEM, PV-TEM-EDS (Fig. 6), X-TEM and X-TEMEDS (Fig. 7) analysis detected the Ge Bridge between Source  and Drain of the PMOS, as shown in Fig. 6(a)(c).

This failure mechanism was the Ge bridge between the  Source and Drain of PMOS. As we know, to improve PMOS  performance and stress considerations, the wafer requires  undergoing a selective epitaxial growth of silicon-germanium  (SiGe) to form PMOS Source/Drain. The Ge element was  introduced in that process step. That caused the Source and  Drain to short directly and then finally induced the electrical  failure.

But how did this Ge bridge defect occur? After in-depth  analysis and research, it was found that the defect was  probably caused by the low quality of the thermal oxide film  grown on the fins of this region before the deposition of the  dummy gate. The dummy gate was the amorphous silicon  which is deposited using chemical vapor deposition (CVD).  This oxide layer was used as the etch stop layer (ESL) in the  process step of etching amorphous silicon to make a dummy  gate. Now we presume the thickness of the Oxide ESL film in  this region was much thinner than the preset range. Then the  fin over-etch issue would take place in the process of etching  amorphous silicon due to the thin thickness of  Oxide ESL in  this region. The fin was over-etched into the Well location.  The etch stop surface was lower than the top surface of the  shallow trench isolation (STI). It looked like a groove where  the fin would have been raised, as shown in Fig. 8. Next step,  the PMOS fins outside of the nitride spacer are etched away,  as shown in Fig. 9(a). The wafer then undergoes a selective  epitaxial growth of SiGe.

Normally, SiGe is formed only at the Source and Drain fin  locations, as shown in reference locations in Fig. 9, and also  in Fig. 7(d)(e)(f). Because SiGe only nucleates on the exposed  silicon surface which means that it only forms in the PMOS  Source/Drain fins (in this step, all other locations are covered  by nitride, oxide, or SiCN hard mask). However, in the failed  PMOS region, SiGe is formed from the Well region that is  over-etched to the Source and Drain fin region, as shown in  the defect locations in Fig. 9, and also in Fig. 7(a)(b)(c).

Then in the following dummy gate etching process, the  Oxide ESL on the channel fin under the dummy gate is also  used as an etch stop layer. Typically, the etching will stop at  the Oxide ESL on the fin. But at this failed PMOS location,  the Oxide ESL was very thin. The channel fin would also be  over-etched into the Well region after the dummy gate was  removed, as shown in Fig. 10. That was the reason why we  didn’t see the channel fin signature in Fig. 6 (b).

Next, after the dummy gate was etched away, the Oxide  ESL on the channel fin needed to be etched away then a thin  layer(\~1nm) of high-quality oxide was regrown as the gate  oxide layer. We also called it Oxide BIL which underlies the  High-k dielectric and ensures that the interface between the  high-k material and the fin silicon is smooth and electron  mobility is not reduced. There is the possibility that it results  in an uneven surface at the over-etched WELL region in the  course of the etching process and cleaning process. The

uneven surface of the over-etched Well region would probably  result in low-quality BIL, such as an incomplete BIL or an  uneven BIL. After this, high-k dielectric would probably  contact with the Well region somewhere directly. Or it could  cause DBIE somewhere. It caused the leak between the Gate  and the Source/Drain. Typically, the gate leakage caused by  such defects is relatively small. It is the explanation for the  minor Gate leakage defect in Fig. 4(b).

SiGe in the Well underneath the Source/Drain region  could reach the over-etched Well underneath the Gate through  diffusion or penetration. Because after some process steps,  some voids, gaps or rough surfaces at the over-etched Well  region underneath the Gate will probably occur. Then it would  cause a result shown in Fig. 6 and Fig. 7(a)(b)(c). That was Ge  run-through in the Well region from the Source terminal to the  Drain terminal which induced a direct connection between the  Source and Drain of PMOS. Then it caused a large short  between the PMOS Source and Drain shown in Fig. 4(a).

The following is some evidence to support the mechanism  of the failure formation I described above, which is caused by  the over-etching of the fin caused by the Oxide ESL layer  defect. As we know, Ge diffusion probably happens under  certain annealing conditions. It is a relatively common reason  for the formation of the Ge bridge. In this case, how do we  distinguish which one was correct? Here are some points.

The first point is that there is little or even no Si element  in the fin location underneath the Gate in the defect region  shown in Fig. 6(b). No fin signature. However, if it were the  result of Si-Ge interpenetration, then Si should not be  completely absent. The second point is that there is no fin  signature in the target defect region in the XTEM image  shown in Fig. 11(b) compared with the reference shown in  Fig. 11(a). These two points can be used as evidence of the  absence of fins, indicating that the fins were etched away  rather than the Si-Ge interdiffusion.

The third point is the Ti and Al element anomaly as  indicated by the red arrow in Fig. 12 (b)(d). The anomaly  location should be the vertical direction of the fin position. We  can see more Ti and Al elements at the defect location than the  reference in Fig. 12(a)(c). Ge diffusion should not change the  shape and profile of the channel fin and the gate during the  SiGe epitaxial growth annealing process, nor should it result  in a significant increase of Ti and Al elements in the vertical  direction of the fin position. The abnormal rise of Ti and Al at  the vertical direction of the fin location can indirectly prove  the absence of fins. As the fins were etched away, the gate  material was deposited in the place of the original fins. In the  structure of these 14FinFET samples, the main body of the  Gate structure is Ti element, followed by Al element, which is  consistent with the PVTEM EDS results shown in Fig.  12(b)(d). And Ti anomaly looks more obvious than Al  anomaly. It is consistent with the hypothesis that the fin was  etched away.

The fourth point is that Ge diffusion during the annealing  process should not cause defects in the oxide BIL layer and  high-k dielectric layer, and should not cause gate leakage  shown in Fig. 4(b).

Fifth, it is known from previous research results that the  typical diffusion lengths of Si-Ge interdiffusion after hightemperature steps in the current technology are comparable  to the thickness of SiGe thin films in the devices. So based on

this, if the formation mechanism is Ge diffusion, the diffusion  length should be the same because all the fin sizes are the  same. But from the images shown in Fig. 7(a)(b)(c), we can  see a detail that the bottom boundary of the Ge element has a  certain slope rather than a horizontal line. This is also  contrary to the argument that the formation mechanism is Ge  diffusion.

## III.   C ONCLUTION 

This paper mainly studied the different failure  mechanisms for MOSFET leakage. One failure mechanism  was DBIE. The other one was Ge bridge. For these two  different failure mechanisms, we investigated the possible  formation mechanism. Both of them were probably caused by  oxide defects in different steps of the 14nm FINFET  technology process. This provides evidence for fab process  improvement.
