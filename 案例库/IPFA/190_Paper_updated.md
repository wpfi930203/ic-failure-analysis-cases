## Capacitive Coupling Voltage Contrast   Contribution to PVC in Advanced SRAMs  

Greg M. Johnson 1 , Rainer Arnold 2 , Magdalena Eder 2 , Hafner Bernd 2 , Bjoern Gamm 2 , Stefan Meyer 2 , Wolfgang Berger 2   1  Carl Zeiss Microscopy, USA,   2  Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Oberkochen, Germany   Email :  greg.johnson@zeiss.com

Abstract —Advances in SEM imaging voltage contrast of  advanced technology node SRAMs are discussed. The use of  large apertures, careful choice of plane of polish, and secondary  electron detection modes in the SEM column may enable the  triggering of Capacitive Coupling Voltage Contrast. An AI  segmentation model that provides a quantitative quality factor  for the images is presented.

Keywords—CCVC, PVC, SRAM, inspection, SEM, AI

## I.    INTRODUCTION  

Passive voltage contrast (PVC) is an important technique  for defect inspection of semiconductor samples and may  derive from different mechanisms. PVC results are also  frequently touted when evaluating the effectiveness of a  particular column design in an electron microscope [1] [2] [3]  [4]. Some of the most regularly cited mechanisms for PVC in  electronics samples include a capacitive effect for metals, and  the built-in potential of a  $p/n$   junction for semiconductors.  In  the capacitance mechanism, those features with larger  capacitance can emit secondary electrons  for a longer period  of time than smaller ones [5]. It is understood that for this  mechanism, the longer the beam sits on a sample, the lesser of  an effect will be seen, as eventually, all features will lose a  significant amount of electrons, and charge up positively,  making all features dark.

The PVC mechanism associated with semiconductor  samples arises from the built-in potential of a  p/n  junction. The  built-in potential of the diode will impose a differential energy  cost to electrons seeking to escape from the N side versus  those leaving the P side, and thus N features are darker [6] [7].  This effect is pictured in Fig. 1, with respect to typical crosssectional features of an SRAM (Static random-access  memory). Again, it is generally understood that very long  beam times will overwhelm small features and ruin this effect,  as evidenced by the general understanding that PVC is more  difficult in the smaller features of advanced technology nodes.

Another effect that may play a role when a dielectric  material exists above the features of interest is Capacitive  Coupling Voltage Contrast (CCVC). This dielectric layer may  act as a capacitor when buried features are charged negatively  or positively. The electron beam may then interact differently  with the two regions of dielectric, based on the charge of the  buried features. [8] [9] [10] [11] [12]

Any paper that would seek to provide a complete  understanding of the optimal conditions for voltage contrasts  for all semiconductor samples would be taking on a Herculean  task. This paper will attempt to provide some guidance for  optimizing PVC in a ZEISS GeminiSEM. The experiments  were carried in two different waves. The first set helped to  provide a baseline, and the second set provided statistical  rigour to the conclusions.

## II.   PROCEDURE  

## A.   Baseline experiments  

For an early set of experiments, a commercially available   $7\,\mathrm{\nm}$   technology node SRAM sample was delayered to  contact level. The sample was examined in a ZEISS  GeminiSEM 460 scanning electron microscope. Experiments  were undertaken at various beam landing energies and  working distances.  Fig. 2 shows an experiment at working  distance of 5.7 mm and a cycle time of 1.1 minutes, as a  function of landing energy. At very low energies, there is  almost a topological effect, where contacts are visible, but no  contrast.  Across this range, greater voltage contrast between  N and P regions in the structures become more apparent at  around   $1.0\ensuremath{\,\mathrm{kV}}$  .

Next, a series of images were taken at   $0.8\;\mathrm{kV}$  , cycle time  of one minute, and imaged at three different working  distances. These results confirm (unpublished) ray-tracing simulations  of low-energy, secondary electrons, traveling up the the  GeminiSEM column, which suggest an optimal collection  efficiency in this range. Based on the results of these  simulations, and their confirmation in initial imaging, a more  complete set of experiments were undertaken with beam  conditions on a different chip. The conditions of  $1.0\,\mathrm{kV}$   beam  landing energy and 5.0 mm working distance were fixed for  the remainder of the work.

## B.   Rigorous comparisons  

For the next set of samples, a commercially available,  5 nm technology node, SRAM sample was mechanically  delayered to a point where the internal node connector was  buried just underneath the surface. The sample was inserted  into a ZEISS GeminiSEM 560 scanning electron microscope,  and imaged with different working distances, apertures, and  beam landing energies. In all of the SEM images for the  remainder of the paper, care was taken to choose brightness  and contrast values to optimize the full range of the RGB  values in the image. Typical results for 1 kV imaging at 5.0  mm working distance are shown in Fig. 4. At the low  magnifications seen in the image, the column of contacts  associated with VDD become increasingly distinct against  the background as the aperture is widened to allow more  current, reaching a maximum effect with the  $60\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$   aperture.  These effects will be quantified in a later section of the paper.  In a careful consideration of the   $120\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$   aperture images, one  can see the L-shaped, internal node contacts stand out against  the background. However, since the resolution of these  images were worse than the others, the   $120~\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$   aperture  results will be dropped from further consideration in this  paper.

## III.   ARTIFICIAL   INTELLIGENCE  

To assess the feasibility of using artificial intelligence  (AI)-based image segmentation for statistical comparison of  PVC results over many SRAM cells, five SEM images were  recorded from the same area of a 5 nm technology node  sample, covering several dozen SRAM cells. Different pixel  dwell times and averaging schemes (frame averaging and no  averaging) were used for each image, while all other imaging  conditions, notably the acceleration voltage of 1 kV and the  pixel size of 1.213 nm, were kept the same. Therefore, the  resulting images showed the same region of interest, but at  different signal-to noise ratios. Three different types of  substructures (VDD, PG, GND) and the remaining image  area (background) were manually marked in one of the  images (See Fig. 10). A deep learning network model that  uses a U-Net with EfficientNet as encoder and Pixelshuffle  as decoder was trained on this image with its three annotation  classes (ZEISS arivis Cloud [13]). The trained model was  then applied to the remaining four images to assign their  pixels to one of the classes (see Fig. 11). Using the  segmentations thus produced as masks, grey value statistics  were calculated for each image and class separately and  plotted to assess their consistency. The plots (Fig. 12) show

that the distributions of the grey value means calculated from  each VDD, PG and GND feature do not overlap significantly  and are consistent from image to image. This indicates that  the AI segmentation used is robust against signal-to-noise  variations in the images, and that PVC imaging using our  instrument setup allows to clearly separate VDD, PG and  GND features by their grey value distributions. No intent was  made to use an A.I. routine to select defective areas: a  possible   analysis   for   a   future   paper.

## IV.   D ISCUSSION  

A mechanism for these advanced results is proposed  and depicted in Fig. 13. The built-in potential of the diodes is  acquired by the metals connected to the silicon, and these  charges are capacitively coupled to the top surface, where  voltage contrast effects will impact the resulting secondary  electron emission. As the beam is interacting with the  dielectric, it is plausible that there may be less damage to  devices: a proposition to be explored in future work.

![INTERNALNODES   BL; GND   VDD  ](https://venus-trag-1258344701.cos-internal.ap-guangzhou.tencentcos.cn/userUpload/is-62e36cd9/2025-03-29/6b802c43-b9b9-408b-bec8-bf3d5ea3123d/6b802c43-b9b9-408b-bec8-bf3d5ea3123d.jpg?q-sign-algorithm=sha1&q-ak=AKID61g5D5fy1TjiRv84nEOHtFJ8gyzjCPL0&q-sign-time=1743215884%3B1743219484&q-key-time=1743215884%3B1743219484&q-header-list=host&q-url-param-list=&q-signature=2a00a0394608f6b8ee61e4ebbc96266eb085a094)
Fig. 13. Model for Capacitive Coupling Voltage Contrast. A specimen  contains active lines with diodes. These lines are connected by metallic  middle of the line (MOL) contacts which in several cases exist just under  the surface. The built-in potential of the  $p/n$   junction causes a charge to  build up at the MOL contacts. It is capacitively coupled with charges that  also build up on the surface. This effects secondary electron emission from  the surface, and has effects even at beam landing energies that would not  be able to penetrate the sample and/or have secondary electrons with  appropriate escape depth.

An increase in aperture could plausibly provide two  different effects: increased current, or a lower depth of field  (DOF). The current delivered to the sample increases by an  approximate factor of four with each doubling of the aperture  size. DOF is unlikely to play a role here, because with the  higher working distance of 5.0 mm used in these experiments;  the actual angle of attack is hardly changed even between 15  and   $120\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$  . The CCVC effect likewise would be a strong  function of beam current.

The experiments suggest that excellent PVC results can  be found at a plane in which local interconnect contacts (such  as for the internal node and GND) are located below the

exposed surface. This demonstrates the value and necessity  of taking SE2 images during any experimentation with PVC.  One needs to know the sample’s depth of the plane of polish,  especially if comparing results between samples. The work  also highlights the importance of making quantitative  measurements of brightness values between different types of  contacts.

There may be some concern about an application using  1 kV of beam landing energy to 5 nm SRAM contact arrays,  when nanoprobing is typically undertaken at much lower  energies. This effect, too, could be quantified in follow-up  work. However, inline inspections might be performed at  energies higher than that typical for nanoprobing.  Furthermore, this work showed that imaging of 300 contacts  with only 10 seconds of imaging provides useful contrasts  and could be optimized even more at a later date.

Finally, the use of AI techniques helped to quantify and  show the statistical variation between various contact types  within an image. Further refinement of this model could  distinguish between at-level and buried features of the same  type of contact (GND, for example).

## V.   C ONCLUSIONS 

A number of conclusions may be drawn from this work.  Firstly, these results have demonstrated superior voltage  contrast in imaging a SRAM sample with a   $60~\upmu\mathrm{m}$   aperture,  which might not be most analysts first choice for obtaining  high resolution work. A principle is therefore demonstrated  that every analysis should be optimized for its own purpose: it  is often a failed strategy to first obtain “the best image  possible”, then optimize for other factors.

Secondly, a method for quantification of voltage contrast in  SRAM failure analysis is presented. An analysis that uses AI  may help with obtaining a more statistically valid comparison,  but the results are also seen with a simple line profile. The  choice for best conditions might not have been as obvious if  one were relying on the human eye to make value judgements.  It was also possible to demonstrate that every type of contact  had its own unique contrast level, in accordance with  expectations from design.  The results also demonstrate that  the depth of polish should be indicated in these  communications.  This paper recommends adoption of such  quantitative practices in any future technical or commercial  communication in our industry going forward.
