## A Laboratory System For X-Ray Assisted Device  Alteration (XADA)  

S. H. Lau 1 , W. Lo 2 ,  B. Stripe 1 , F. Su 1 , S. Lewis 1 , R. I. Spink 1 , W. Yun 1  1 Application Lab Sigray Inc., 5750 Imhoff Dr., Suite I, Concord, CA 94520, USA  2 Advanced Technology Group, NVIDIA, 2721 San Tomas Expy., Santa Clara 95051, USA  Phone : (+1) 408-505-2139, Email :  shlau@sigray.com

Abstract —Traditional backside optical fault-isolation (FI)  techniques such as laser voltage probing (LVP), photon emission  microscopy (PEM) and laser assisted device alteration (LADA)  are no longer generally useful for 3-D stacked integrated circuits  (IC) or backside power distribution (BPD) ICs due to nearinfrared (NIR) blockage on both sides of the transistors. Recent  work has demonstrated the effectiveness of using focused x-rays  from a laboratory source for altering transistor threshold  voltages   $(\mathbf{V_{T}})$  , changing ring oscillator (RO) frequencies, and  inducing timing shifts in switching inverters. These results show  that x-rays can be used for x-ray assisted device alteration  (XADA) taking the place of LADA for 3D and BPD ICs in many  debug and FI scenarios. Here, we present details of the first ever  system developed and optimized for this emerging and groundbreaking application.

Keywords—XADA, Device Alteration, X-ray, Backside Power  Distribution

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

With 3-D stacking and with BPD ICs, access to the  transistor layer is blocked by NIR opaque layers [1]. This  prevents the use of some of the most common and productive  techniques that have been developed over the past few decades  for flip-chip ICs, including LVP, PEM, and LADA [2-4].

It is well known that x-rays can penetrate metal stacks and  other NIR opaque materials and can alter transistor  characteristics [5-7]. These earlier studies on the impact of xray exposures were primarily concerned about device  reliability. More recent studies have directly explored the  feasibility of using x-rays for targeted and intentional device  alterations. Flip-chip ICs were used in one of the studies due  to the lack of access to BPD ICs [1]. Transistor   $\mathrm{V_{T}}$   shifts, RO  frequency shifts, and inverter timing shifts have all been  demonstrated [8].

## II.      E XPERIMENTAL  S ETUP 

These preliminary XADA experiments were performed on  a modified micro x-ray fluorescence microscopy   $(\upmu\mathrm{XRF})$    system (Sigray Inc, Concord, CA, AttoMap-200). A Cu x-ray  source (characteristic x-ray energy of 8keV) and monocapillary x-ray optics were used. NIR optics and camera were  incorporated to allow backside navigation on flip-chip ICs. A  shutter was added to precisely control exposure doses.

While this system was adequate for the initial XADA  feasibility studies, it lacks many important features needed for  reliable and routine XADA isolations. This includes support  for CAD alignment and navigation, tester docking capability,  the required stage accuracy and precision, and overall system  stability. As a result, a new system optimized for XADA has  been developed.

In the remainder of this paper, we first present XADA  induced timing shift data and compare to LADA results to  illustrate some of the important differences between the two  techniques. We then present details of the newly developed  XADA tool, including system design choices necessitated by  the properties of x-rays and a real-world example using this  tool to localized defect in a SRAM chip. We finish with a brief  discussion on the prospects for reducing x-ray spot size before  offering conclusions.

## IV.    D ISCUSSION 

## A.   Persistence  

One obvious difference between LADA and XADA is the  transient nature of LADA compared to the persistent nature of  XADA. As shown in Fig. 5, the timing shift induced by  XADA does not recover, even  ${\sim}800$  s after the beam has been  turned off, while Fig. 4 shows that the LADA effect  effectively disappears as soon as the beam is removed from  the node. This finding is consistent with our previously  reported results on XADA- induced transistor   $\mathrm{V_{T}}$   shifts [1].  Based on these findings, we consider the XADA effect to be  permanent for all practical purposes. Methods to deal with this  persistence will need to be developed. One has been recently  reported [8]. Another method may be to monitor pass/fail  counts at each pixel and then display changes in the count rate.

## B.   Depth of Field.  

For the diffraction limited SIL LADA optics, the focused  xy spot size is given by the Airy disc diameter:   $\bar{\mathsf{d}}=0.\bar{6}1^{*}\!\!\lambda\!\!\sqrt{\mathrm{NA}}=197\mathrm{nm}$  , where   $\uplambda$   is the wavelength of light,  1064 nm, and NA is the numerical aperture of the SIL, 3.3.  The spot size in the z direction (i.e., the depth-of-field, DOF)  is given by:  $\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{z}}=\lambda^{\ast}\mathfrak{n}/(\mathrm{NA})^{2}=342~\mathrm{nm}$  , where n is the index of  refraction of the immersion medium, 3.5 for Si in this case.  (see  microscopyu.com/dof  for more information). The half  angular aperture of the SIL can be calculated using the  definition of numerical aperture:  $\mathbf{NA}=\mathbf{n}^{*}\mathrm{sin}\boldsymbol{\alpha}$  , where α is the  half angular aperture. Solving for α, we obtain 70.5 degrees.  (See  microscopyu.com/NA  for more information.)

Compared to the rather ‘blunt’ α of LADA, the beam for  XADA can be considered a pencil beam with low divergence  angle of   ${\sim}10$   mrad, and, hence, a large DOF of  ${\sim}50~\upmu\mathrm{m}$  . There  is no way to isolate layers within this range for 3-D stacked  ICs during x-ray exposures.  Rather, the user will need to use  circuit layout and diagnostic callout information to help  isolate any sensitive nodes identified. One possible alternative  is to mimic a technique developed for therapeutic radiography  where the incident x-ray angle is varied about the target  location during exposure. This will cause the ‘pencil’ beam to  be incident in different directions during exposure,  concentrating dose on the target location but reducing dose in  the surrounding regions.

## C.   X-ray Penetration Depth  

The penetration depth of x-rays in any material is  dependent on the energy of the x-rays from the source anode.  In the case of the Cu anode used for these experiments, the xray photon energy is   $8.04\,\mathrm{keV}$   with   $50\%$   transmission through   ${\sim}2~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$   of Au. The DUT used for these experiments were flipchip packaged ICs with the backside Si thinned down to   ${\sim}50\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$  remaining silicon thickness (RST). The question  naturally arises: how will results compare when using an  actual backside power device with metal on both sides of the  transistor? Some absorption numbers to compare:   $50~\upmu\mathrm{m}$   Si  has similar absorption as   $15\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$   of Cu. When approaching a  backside power device from the frontside, it’s also possible to  completely remove the Si carrier to eliminate losses as much  as possible.

## D.   Spot Size  

With the large spot sizes currently in use, another natural  question is how successful XADA isolations can be. Even  with the expected development of   ${\sim}1~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$   level optics, such a  large spot size can still cover many nodes in the IC. If the  nodes are all part of the same ‘critical’ path, then it may be  difficult to isolate which node is the limiting one. With  sufficient SNR and centroiding of the P/F hits, it may be  possible to localize the most sensitive node. However, since  sensitivity may depend on many parameters, including node  size, drive strength, scan parameters, caution will be  demanded when interpreting result, especially for early  adopters of XADA. It should be noted, however, that low  magnification LADA, e.g., 20x with NA of 0.42, has a spot  size of   ${\sim}1.5~\upmu\mathrm{m}$  . It is still considered very useful for preisolating over large ROI.

## E.   Observed Timing Shifts  

The data presented in Figs. 4 and 5 all show a negative timing  shift, i.e., a speedup of the clock transitions. The data is  presented as-is and no real characterization or interpretation  of the results was attempted. The main goal of the choice of  experimental parameters was to find a set of conditions such  that the timing data was as stable as possible (high-V DD ) while,  simultaneously, as sensitive to perturbations as possible (lowV DD ) in order to have sufficient SNR to be able to detect the  expected small timing shifts. It will require a lot more  characterization to try to understand the LADA and XADA  effects: Varying V DD , node side/drive strength etc. The  physical effects of the laser versus x-rays should be very  different, as evidenced by the persistence effects observed for  XADA, so different explanations will be needed. Each  absorbed LADA photon generates one electron-hole pair  (EHP) while each absorbed 8keV x-ray photon can generate

over two thousand EHP. However, there are far fewer photons  in the x-ray beam than there are for typical LADA laser  powers used so instantaneous x-ray induced current is far  weaker. This leaves oxide structure charging as the primary  mechanism through which the x-rays interact. Another factor  to take into consideration when comparing LADA to XADA  results: The XADA beam is large enough to encompass both  pFET and nFET in the inverters probed, making it difficult/not  possible to separate pFET and nFET effects. The LADA beam  is still small enough to give some isolation between nFET and  pFET during exposure.

## V.    N EW  S YSTEM  D ESIGN  

As mentioned, the above experiments described in the paper  were performed using a modified version of Sigray’s  AttoMap-200, which is a system designed for x-ray  fluorescence. To investigate the practical use of x-rays for  LADA-like applications, Intel collaborated with Sigray, Inc.  to develop a first-of-a-kind x-ray-assisted device alteration  (XADA) tool, shown in Figure 6. The system is designed to  accommodate large tester apparatuses and allows testers to be  mounted and removed without disconnecting cables to the  tester. Testers can be connected to a data acquisition unit that  is included in the XADA tool, which is synched with x-ray  exposure to pinpoint physical defects in the DUT. The novel  XADA tool also includes a built-in metrology area that is  used to calibrate alignment of the x-ray optics to the x-ray  source. The metrology area is used to verify alignment  between the x-ray and optical components for navigating on  the DUT.

## VI.    S POT SIZE   

X-ray spot size of   $<5~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$   leverages two groundbreaking  patented technologies: (1) a high brightness x-ray source with  discrete target materials (e.g. Si, Cr, Co, Cu, Au, Mo, W, and  Rh), which allow users to change between x-ray excitation  energies and (2) high efficiency, Pt-coated x-ray optics that  demagnify the x-ray source spot (shown in Figure 7).  Continued development of these core technologies with  brighter X-ray source and smaller X-ray probe size is ongoing  and is part of the product’s roadmap.

## C ONCLUSION 

The upcoming transition to BPD architecture obsoletes  many of the NIR tools and techniques that play key roles in  flip-chip FI, including LVx, PEM, and LADA. The FA  community will need to develop alternatives that can fill some  of the gaps. X-rays are a form of ionizing radiation capable of  penetrating metal stacks, making them interesting to explore  as a possible replacement for LADA. Preliminary studies have  shown that x-ray induced transistor   $\mathrm{V_{T}}$  , RO frequency, and  inverter timing shifts are all possible. A debug showing a  triangulation technique to deal with the x-ray persistence  effect was also demonstrated [8]. In this paper, an example of  the x-ray induced timing shift of an inverter fabricated in a  5 nm process flip-chip was presented. Details of a system  designed and optimized for XADA were also presented. We  expect XADA to play a key role in the era of BPD ICs.
