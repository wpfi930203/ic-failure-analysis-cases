## ESD Characteristics Improving of LVTSCR by  Adding RC Triggering Circuit in 0.18 μm BCD  Technology  

Yang Wang 1, 2\* , Shuoxin Ji 1, 2 , Shuang  $\mathrm{Li^{l,2}}$  , Hongjiao Yang 1,2  Xijun Chen 1,2  1 Department of Physics and Optoelectronics, Xiangtan University, Xiangtan, Hunan, 411105, China  2 Laboratory for Microelectronics, Optoelectronics and System on a Chip, Xiangtan, Hunan, 411105, China  Phone : +86 731 58292195, Email : wangyang@xtu.edu.cn

Abstract -  In this paper, Low Voltage Triggering SCR  (LVTSCR) and RC-triggered LVTSCR are fabricated in a 0.18  μm Bipolar-CMOS-DMOS (BCD) process. TCAD simulation is  introduced to study the ESD protection devices’ working  mechanism, while transmission line pulse (TLP) measuring  system being used to characterize the devices’ Electrostatic  Discharge (ESD) characteristics. It is verified that the additional  RC triggering circuit can reduce the devices’ trigger voltage,  and better ESD characteristics with higher holding voltage can  be obtained through altering certain critical size.

Keywords – ESD, LVTSCR, BCD, TLP, TCAD simulation.

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

Electrostatic discharge is one of the key factors affecting  the reliability of electronic devices and electronic systems.  Being capable of instantaneous discharge of large current,  which is high enough to damage the internal circuit, ESD  events can lead to chip failure. As semiconductor technology  advances, chip integration has shown a concurrent increase [1- 3]. Electrostatic discharge poses a progressively critical  challenge due to the gradual thinning of the gate oxide layer,  particularly within the demanding operational environments  of the vehicle Controller Area Network (CAN) Bus [4-6].  Amid the array of ESD protection solutions developed, the  Silicon-Controlled Rectifier (SCR) stands out for its superior  area efficiency and robustness [7-13]. Notably, the enhanced  LVTSCR featuring an integrated MOSFET, pioneered by  Chatterjee et al., has demonstrated a reduced trigger voltage  [14-18]. The reduction of trigger voltage would ensure the  device’s turning on before the damage of internal circuit, and  oftentimes indicates a rapid response when ESD pulse arrives.  This study presents the fabrication of an original LVTSCR  architecture alongside an RC-triggered LVTSCR variant  within a  $0.18\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$   BCD process. Though adding RC triggering  circuit, RC_LVTSCR is designed to reach a lower trigger  voltage and prevent mis-trigger with the least severe layout  penalty. Utilizing Technology Computer Aided Design  (TCAD) simulation, we elucidate the operational principles  underlying these devices. Through TLP measurement  techniques, our investigation confirms the proposed structures  exhibit lower trigger voltages and higher holding voltages,  affirming their efficacy in ESD protection.

## II.   D ESIGN AND  D ISCUSSION 

## A.   Device Structure and Working Mechanism  

The cross-section of traditional LVTSCR structure is  depicted in Fig. 1(a). This traditional structure comprises a  standard SCR structure augmented with an embedded n

channel metal-oxide semiconductor (NMOS). The inclusion  of the NMOS introduces an additional surface path compared  to the original SCR structure. Moreover, the drain of the  NMOS serves as an  $\mathrm{N}+$   bridge positioned between the SDNW  and SDPW doped regions, facilitating the active switching of  the reversed PN junction from SDNW/SDPW to N+/SDPW,  which effectively reduces the breakdown voltage of the  junction. Within the structure, R1 and R2 represent two  parasitic resistors, while the lateral NPN1, PNP, and vertical  NPN2 denote three parasitic bipolar junction transistors  (BJTs). Consequently, two ESD discharge paths are formed:  the first path comprising the parasitic PNP and NPN1, and the  second path comprising PNP and NPN2. Fig. 1(b) illustrates  the proposed structure, RC_LVTSCR, which incorporates an  additional RC triggering circuit alongside the ESD clamp  device. The RC triggering circuit primarily consists of a  resistor R and an NMOS gate oxide capacitor C. Meanwhile,  the LVTSCR functions as the ESD clamp device. The  corresponding equivalent circuit diagram of the proposed  device is presented in Fig. 1(c).

When an ESD pulse arrives at the Anode of the traditional  LVTSCR, a drain-source breakdown would occur within the  embedded NMOS, resulting in a lower trigger voltage  compared to a simple SCR structure. Within this specific  clamp device, the conventional LVTSCR encompasses two  low-resistance pathways, comprising two SCR paths  considering the inclusion of an additional N-type buried layer  (NBL). In contrast, while remaining the same clamp device,  the proposed configuration integrates an RC triggering circuit  connected to the Anode. This trigger circuit facilitates the  activation of the ESD clamp device by supplying a positive  voltage to Node A during the turning-on process. By altering  the resistor and capacitor parameters of the triggering circuit,  the RC time constant can be adjusted, consequently modifying  the charging and discharging duration of the capacitor and  thereby influencing the voltage fluctuation of the trigger node,  Node A. In the illustrated configuration depicted in Fig. 1(b),  a   $100~\mathrm{k}\Omega$   resistor and a 0.45 pF NMOS gate oxide capacitor  are employed within the proposed RC triggering circuit.

## B.   Experimental Results and Discussions  

To elucidate the operational dynamics of the ESD  protection device, a comprehensive two-dimensional (2D)  device simulation employing mixed-mode TCAD simulation  methodologies was undertaken. To simulate the electrical  behavior during an ESD event, Phumob model (Philips  Unified Mobility model), Auger recombination model, SRH  recombination model (Shockley Read Hall recombination  model), Impact Selb model (impact ionization model), bgn

model (band gap narrowing model) and lattice temperature  model were introduced. The aforementioned models are  initially calibrated using a standard procedure. Their  calculated results are temperature and electric field dependent.  Specifically, a square-like 0.3 A current pulse characterized  by a rising time and falling time each set at 10 ns, with a total  duration of 100 ns mirroring typical TLP current pulse  parameters, was applied separately to the Anodes of the  devices, while maintaining the Cathode potential at 0 V. The  resulting current density distribution of the conventional  LVTSCR and the RC_LVTSCR are respectively depicted in  Fig. 2 and Fig. 3. Notably, the augmented protection device  incorporating an RC triggering circuit employs the LVTSCR  structure as its primary ESD clamp mechanism, thereby  sharing analogous current pathways with its traditional  LVTSCR. Consequently, their respective current distributions  exhibit a striking resemblance. When the device is initially  triggered, the drain source breakdown takes place, forming the  surface current path, followed by the deepened current path as  the device being fully triggered. Upon scrutinizing the current  distribution profiles of these protective devices, it becomes  evident that both configurations feature three current paths:  the surface current path that connects the drain-source area of  embedded NMOS, the main SCR path and the SCR path  formed by NBL.

The RC triggering circuit in RC_LVTSCR serves two  primary functions: coupling a positive voltage to the gate and  preventing mis-trigger during a normal power-on situation.  Naming the node between resistor and capacitor as Node A,  its voltage during the aforementioned simulated ESD event is  shown in Fig. 4. It’s noticeable that the additional trigger  circuit facilitates a gradual reduction in positive potential  applied to the gate throughout the ESD event. As shown in Fig.  5, comparing to the LVTSCR in which the gate constantly  being placed at ground potential, the RC_LVTSCR has a  stronger electric field beneath gate area, assisting the  triggering process of RC_LVTSCR.

The RC triggering circuit integrated into the RC_LVTSCR  comprises a   $1\bar{0}\bar{0}\,\mathrm{k}\Omega$   resistor and a 0.45 pF NMOS gate oxide  capacitor. The specific selection of resistor and capacitor  parameters within the triggering circuit yields a time constant  of 45 ns, a value notably smaller than conventional choices.  This deliberate adjustment is motivated by the placement of  the triggering circuit at Node A, situated on the gate of the  embedded NMOS. Consequently, the supplementary RC  triggering circuit functions primarily to facilitate the  activation process of the LVTSCR, rather than directly  influencing its operational duration. Therefore, the RC  constant is set to a relatively diminutive value to minimize the

required layout area while maintaining its functional efficacy.  With these specified parameters of the external triggering  circuit, the RC_LVTSCR is also rendered impervious to nonESD signals, thereby mitigating the risk of false triggering  during normal power-on or power-off sequences. The  simulation of the RC triggering circuit during a normal poweron and power-off situation was executed within the Cadence  environment, wherein a   $5\mathrm{~V~}$   pulse characterized by rise and  fall times of   $1\,\upmu\mathrm{s}$   was applied to the Anode. As depicted in Fig.  6, the maximum voltage attained by the triggering node, Node  A, during the power-on event is merely  $0.2\,\mathrm{V}$  , and is obviously  insufficient to initiate activation of the ESD clamp device.  Consequently, it is established that even under the influence  of an exceedingly rapid power-on signal with a   $1\;\upmu\mathrm{s}$   rise time,  the clamp device remains immune to false triggering, thereby  affirming the robustness of the proposed structure against  false   triggering   during   normal   power-on   scenarios.  Furthermore, with the potential of Node A stabilizing after 2  μs, the pulse duration is designated as   $4~\upmu\mathrm{s}$  . Similarly, during  the power-off scenario, the maximum negative voltage of 0.2  V precludes the false triggering of the clamp device.

Both the conventional LVTSCR structure and the  enhanced configuration featuring an additional RC triggering  circuit were fabricated utilizing a   $0.18~\upmu\mathrm{{m}}$   BCD process. The  layout dimensions for each structure entail a finger length of   $25~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$  . Fig. 7 showcases the microscope images of the 6- finger LVTSCR and the 6-finger RC triggering LVTSCR.   Notably, the RC_LVTSCR incorporates the same primary  clamp device as the LVTSCR, augmented by an appended   RC  triggering circuit. Through connecting the drain, emitter, and  substrate of a MOSFET, the gate oxide would play the role of  the capacitor in the RC triggering circuit. To assess their ESD  performance, the TLP testing methodology was employed.  The devices’ current-voltage (I-V) characteristics were  evaluated utilizing the Thermo Scientific TLP system.  Sequential current pulses characterized by a rise time of 10 ns  and a pulse width of 100 ns were applied to the Anodes of the  devices. Subsequently, with each pulse, the current level  incrementally increased. Following each pulse, a DC voltage  of 5.5 V was applied to the pin to measure its leakage current.  The devices were deemed to have reached their respective  failure points once the leakage current reached   $1~\upmu\mathrm{A}$  . The  current corresponding to this failing point was documented as  the devices’ failing current.

The TLP IV curves of both mentioned devices are depicted  in Fig. 8, while their corresponding parameters including  trigger voltage   $(\mathrm{V_{t1}})$  , holding voltage   $\mathrm{(V_{h})}$  , and failure current  (I t2 ) are presented in Table Ⅰ. Evidently, the incorporation of  the additional RC triggering circuit in RC_LVTSCR notably  diminishes the trigger voltage. The holding voltage of these  two structures remains practically identical, owing to their  shared clamp device. To further investigate the ESD  characteristics of the proposed RC-triggered LVTSCR,  variations in the width of the field oxide (FOX) situated  between the straddled ND bridge and PD in SDNW, denoted  as D1 and highlighted with a red arrow in Fig. 1(b), are  examined. The enlarged D1 would lead to a larger base region  of the parasitic PNP transistor, thereby diminishing its current  gain,  $\beta_{P N P}$  , and consequently resulting in a larger holding  voltage. As shown in Fig. 9, the BV characteristics remains  consistent owing to the uniformity of the breakdown surface  across all discussed devices. Specifically, D1 is set to   $4~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$  (RC_LVTSCR2) and   $8~\mathrm{~\upmum}$   (RC_LVTSCR3), while the  previously mentioned devices are maintained at   $2\ \ \upmu\mathrm{m}$

(traditional LVTSCR and RC_LVTSCR). The corresponding  TLP testing results are depicted in Fig. 10, while their  associated parameters are tabulated in Table Ⅱ. It is evident  that as the size D1 increases, there is a corresponding rise in  the holding voltage.

To assess the performance of the ESD protection device, a  figure of merit (FOM) is introduced, as delineated in (1). This  metric is computed based on the device's failure current (I t2 ),  holding voltage   $\mathrm{(V_{h})}$  , area factor (A), and trigger voltage   $(\mathrm{V_{t1}})$  .

$$F O M=\left(I_{t2}\,\,X\,V_{h}\,\right)/\left(A\,\,X\,V_{t l}\,\right)$$
The area factor A is integrated into the formula to account  for the differing layout area of the devices, resulting from the  presence of the additional RC triggering circuit and variations  in parameter D1. As illustrated in Table Ⅲ, the  RC_LVTSCR3 exhibits the highest area factor due to its  inclusion of the RC triggering circuit and the largest D1 value.  Upon comparing the characterization results of the four  devices, RC_LVTSCR3 emerges as possessing superior ESD  characteristics, indicated by its highest FOM value, elevated  holding voltage, and highest failure current.

## III.   C ONCLUSIONS 

In this study, four distinct ESD protection devices were  meticulously designed and fabricated utilizing a  $0.18\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$   BCD  process, with the Low Voltage Triggered Silicon Controlled  Rectifier (LVTSCR) serving as the primary clamp device  across all configurations. Leveraging the TLP testing  technique, the efficacy of an additional RC triggering circuit  in enhancing ESD performance was systematically evaluated.  Notably, the RC triggering circuit exhibited a pronounced  effect on reducing the trigger voltage while concomitantly  elevating the holding voltage. To provide a comprehensive  assessment and facilitate comparison among these devices, a  figure of merit, FOM, was introduced, wherein the area factor  was judiciously integrated to account for variations in device  layout area. Through this quantitative evaluation, it was  demonstrated that a remarkable FOM value of 1434.72 A /   $\mathrm{mm}^{2}$   could be achieved by strategically manipulating certain  critical parameters of the device.
