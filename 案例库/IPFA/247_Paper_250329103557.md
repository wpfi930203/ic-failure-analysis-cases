## PCB Surface Component Detection with Computer Vision Assisted Label Generation 

Deruo Cheng, Jingyang Dai, Yee-Yang Tee, Yiqiong Shi, and Bah-Hwee Gwee School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering Nanyang Technological University, Singapore { deruo.cheng, daij0011, yeeyang.tee, yqshi, ebhgwee } @ntu.edu.sg

Abstract —Surface component detection from optical images of Printed Circuit Boards (PCB) has been an effective approach to quickly identify and verify the electronic parts for ensuring PCB integrity in nowadays largely outsourced supply chain. Automated detection of PCB surface components based on emerging machine learning techniques is however hampered by the high variety among different component classes and the associated challenges in manual data labeling for training component detectors. In this paper, we first present a Computer Vision (CV) assisted approach to generate the label for PCB components, i.e., bounding boxes, from the design files, which is more efficient and reliable than data labeling based on human experience. With our collected PCB image dataset where labels are generated by our proposed CV-assisted approach, further evaluations have been performed to investigate the component detection performance using You-Only-Look-Once (YOLO) object detection models and to study the correlations between detection performance and class-specific dataset statistics.

Index Terms —Printed Circuit Boards, Object Detection, Computer Vision, Hardware Security.

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

The market of Printed Circuit Boards (PCB) has been growing continuously and large-scale outsourcing of manufacturing has also become a prevalent practice in the industry. Considering the potential threat of malicious manufacturer, it is essential to verify the integrity of PCBs to ensure the trustworthiness of supply chain and proper functioning of devices or systems, especially for security-demanding sectors. Detecting surface components from PCB images and subsequently crossreferencing against design files are one of the major tasks for PCB assurance. In recent years, computer vision and machine learning techniques have been actively sought after for the purpose of PCB surface component detection based on optical PCB images.

Stobbe  et al.  [1] presented a pattern and structure-based recognition of PCB components with a graphics module for selective de-soldering. In [2], Mar  et al.  presented the work on segmenting solder joints from PCB images through iterative thresholding and region filling, while Zeng  et al.  proposed to localize PCB components by identifying solder joints through color distribution [3]. In [4], Herchenbach  et al.  presented the segmentation and classification of through-hole components, where optical images are captured for PCBs together with depth information using kinect sensor. In [5], Li  et al.  presented the segmentation of PCB surface-mount devices with image pixel information by identifying the printed bounding

border of small devices such as resistors and capacitors, and by identifying homogeneous large areas for ICs. Along with the increasing popularity of adopting machine learning techniques for PCB surface component detection (or formulated as segmentation), several research works have focused on PCB image dataset, which is another pillar of machine learning model development and benchmarking. In [6], Pramerdorfe et al.  proposed the PCB-DSLR dataset with 748 images for 165 PCBs collected from a recycling facility. PCB-DSLR dataset provides the pixel-wise mask for the image area enclosing the PCB board, and the bounding box for a total of 9313 ICs. Kuo  et al.  proposed a dataset which consists of 47 PCB images for 30 componnet classes, 2 text classes, and 1 unknown class [7]. Lu  et al.  [8] proposed FICS-PCB dataset and subsequently FPIC dataset [9] was proposed by Jessurun et al.  for optical inspection of PCBs which consists of 271 images for 97 different PCBs with bounding box annotations being updated sequentially.

The existing public datasets with various semantic labels have greatly benefited the development of machine learning techniques for PCB surface component detection. However, the criteria of labelling PCB components varies from dataset to dataset. The labelling process is mainly human-dependent and subject to the human labeller’s experience in terms of localizing the components and recognizing component classes. These pose challenges for the case of using different datasets for model development. Furthermore, the purely humandependent labelling process is deemed less reliable as it is quite difficult for human labeller to correctly identify the correct classes for all components which are getting smaller and more ambiguous to identify. In this paper, we propose a more reliable Computer Vision (CV)-assisted approach to label the PCB components that is mainly dependent on design files with human refinement instead of purely depending on human labeller. We adopt CV algorithms to extract component information from PCB design files and generate PCB Component labels for object detection based on the extracted information. We further evaluated You-Only-Look-Once (YOLO) models [10] on a dataset of 21 PCBs collected through our proposed approach of CV-assisted label generation.

## II. P ROPOSED  M ETHOD 

Fig. 1 presents our proposed approach of CV-assisted label generation for collecting labelled dataset that is required to train detectors for PCB component detection. There are several key steps in our proposed CV-assisted label generation, including image alignment, shape detection, text parsing, followed by manual refinement and final detector training. We now delineate these key steps in following subsections.

## A. Alignment of Optical Image and Assembly Layout 

In our proposed CV-assisted label generation, the component labels, including the location and class, are generated from design files such as assembly layout and Bill of Materials (BoM). The generated component labels are then assigned to optical PCB images, which are initially unaligned with the PCB design files. The step of image alignment serves the purpose of aligning two modality of information, i.e., the optical PCB images and corresponding assembly layouts, so

that the labels generated from assembly layout can be directly assigned to the optical image. For image alignment, we adopt projective transformation as in [11] to transform the optical image so that it has the same dimension as the assembly layout and with aligned positional information.

## B. Shape Detection and Text Parsing for Label Generation 

With aligned optical image and assembly layout, many component locations can be automatically extracted from assembly layout through computer vision algorithms. In the assembly layout, the component locations are usually marked with regular shapes such as rectangles and circles. In this case, automated shape detection algorithms can be used for extracting the location, i.e., coordinates, of the surface components. Since not all components are marked with regular shapes in the assembly layout, the extracted component locations are thus partial as shown in Fig. 1. The shape detection algorithms will greatly speed up the labelling process as it locates all components that are marked with regular shapes in assembly layout. With component locations extracted from assembly layout, we further utilize BoM to extract class labels

## C. Manual Refinement for Final Detector Training 

The above labelling procedure should work for most cases. However, location extracted from assembly design for very tall components may not match with their actual location captured in the optical images due to imaging view-angle problem. In this case, the component location extracted from the assembly design needs to be adjusted according to the actual component location, and to obtain the final error-free labelling. With the labels for PCB component locations and class labels, existing object detectors such as YOLO [12] can then be used for final training of PCB surface component detector.

## III. E XPERIMENT  R ESULTS 

In this section, we first present our dataset collected through our proposed CV-assisted label generation, followed by further performance evaluation of YOLO on our collected dataset.

## A. Data Collection & Pre-processing 

Our dataset was collected from 21 PCBs, where 20 PCBs are double-sided and 1 PCB is one-sided (with metal surface on the other side). 41 optical images have been captured and matched with corresponding assembly layout images. YOLOformat labels including bounding box for component location,

i.e., normalized    $(x_{c e n t e r},y_{c e n t e r},w,h)$   and class labels have been generated for 32 out of 41 images that contain interested surface components. There are 31 classes of components labelled in our dataset and the component classes are determined based on BoM instead of manual recognition as presented earlier. There is a total of 3363 component instances and the number of components under each class is shown in Fig. 2. The width/height of the 32 PCB images in our dataset and the width/height of components relative to images are presented in Fig. 3 (a) and (b), respectively.

To train and evaluate YOLO object detector, we split our labelled dataset into 3 subsets for training, validation and test. Considering the small number of instances for certain classes as shown in Fig. 2, we divide 3 subsets to ensure that the training data must contain all component classes, and validation and test data must contain at least 10 component instances for each class. Classes not meeting training/validation/test requirements were discarded for evaluation. The remaining classes unclude: Capacitors, Resistors, Diodes, Chips, Inductors, which are very commonly seen components in various PCBs.

## C. Results & Discussions 

Fig. 4 presents the detection performance of YOLOv5m on 5 component classes of our collected dataset. It can be seen that the detection performance on test set of Capacitor,

Resistor and Chip are around 0.8 in terms of mAP50, where these 3 classes of components have over 100 component instances in our dataset. On the other hand, the detection performance on the remaining two classes of Diode and Inductor, which have less than 100 component instances, are much lower in terms of mAP50. This indicates a strong classspecific correlation between detection performance and the number of component instances under the class. The rather high detection performance of Chip which has only around 200 instances could be partially benefiting from the relatively more consistent image features of Chip components on PCB. Based on the evaluations, the number of component instances

for each class in the dataset is a key factor that affects the detection performance, implying the necessity of more labelled data for PCB surface component detection and more efficient labelling strategy such as our proposed CV-assisted approach.

## IV. C ONCLUSIONS 

In this paper, we have proposed an approach of CV-assisted label generation to generate labels based on PCB design files for PCB surface component detection, which is more reliable and efficient compared to human-dependent labelling strategy. Evaluations on our collected dataset using YOLO object detection models have shown a strong correlation between detection performance and number of component instances under each class, demonstrating the necessity of more labelled data and efficient labelling strategy for PCB surface component detection such as our proposed approach of CV-assisted label generation.
