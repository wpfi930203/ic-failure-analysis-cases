## Using Microprobe to enhance Die Level Static Fault  Isolation in Complex IC  

D. Nagalingam\*, A.C.T. Quah, S.J. Moon, S.M. Parab, P.T. Ng, S.L. Ting, H.H. Ma, C.Q. Chen    Electrical Failure Analysis group, QRA Department, GLOBALFOUNDRIES Singapore  60 Woodlands Industrial Park D, Street 2, Singapore 738406.  \* Phone: +65 6670 1508   Email:  dayanand.nagalingam@globalfoundries.com

Abstract-   Modern SoC design incorporates power management  circuits to minimize standby leakages of digital logic circuitry.  This isolates the logic blocks from being directly powered by the  IC’s pad level power supply and ground. As a result, die level  static fault isolation became ineffective even for gross defects that  are shorting the power lines in the logic blocks due to gated  supply.  From the foundry standpoint, fault isolation on such  devices are even more challenging without the product knowhow,  device specific hardware and test program to properly condition  the power management circuits. An alternative method is to  bypass the power control circuits through microprobing on the  top metal traces to directly supply power to the logic blocks.  However, the key challenge to this method is the means to identify  these power gated devices and verify the relevant power traces to  microprobe, sometimes even in situations without layout  information. In this paper, 3 different approaches have been  illustrated using 3 case studies to overcome this challenge and  achieve static fault isolation success on power gated devices.

Keywords  –  Static Fault Isolation, Power Management Circuits,  Microprobing, Layout, Defect Induced Emissions, Light Emision  due to Off State Leakage Current.

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

With more sophisticated SoC design, many functional  blocks like digital, analog, memories and standard IP blocks  such as I2C, SPMI are integrated into a single IC chip. In such  complex designs, minimizing standby leakage is one of the key  objectives. Some of the circuit level standby leakage reduction  techniques are transistor stacking, multiple threshold voltage  (Vth) using MTCMOS, dynamic Vth, supply voltage scaling,  power gating etc. [1] [2]. In particular, power gating is used to  selectively power down certain blocks in the chip while keeping  other blocks powered up. It uses internal   $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   and/or V SS   switches to control the power to certain blocks to minimize  standby leakages. This impedes fault isolation as failure that  resides in such power gated blocks are inaccessible through  conventional pad level biasing of the power supply and ground.  This challenge may not be relevant in IDMs but it would be  greatly experienced by foundry due to limited access and  knowledge of product, circuit design and layout information. In  this paper, the aforementioned issue is addressed via an  alternative approach where the power management circuits are  bypassed and power and ground nodes of the functional blocks  are directly accessed through microprobing on their respective  far Backend of line metal traces. Microprobing is a  well-established and adopted FA approach to access the internal  nodes of an IC by directly probing on the metal traces or circuit

edit pads to measure and characterize the electrical signature of  the nodes within the circuitry for design debug or failure debug  [3-5]. In most cases, microprobe electrical analysis is  performed with relevant circuit understanding and layout  information at hand. However, this might not always be the  case.

Fig.1 showed the simplified view of a SoC that uses internal  power gating [6] design. It contains two functional blocks  where “Always ON functional block” is directly powered by  V DD , while “Power Gated functional block” is connected to V DD   via a power switching network consisting of power gating  controller and power switching fabric. To access a failure in the  power gated block, internal power switching fabric has to be  programmed to channel the power to the block. This is usually  not achievable without device specific test hardware and test  program. To bypass the power switching fabric, power has to be  supplied directly to the internal power lines labeled as V DDX  in  Fig 1.

The key challenge involved in implementing this method is  the identification of the relevant backend metal traces that  correspond to   $\mathrm{V_{D D X}}$  .  In this paper, three approaches are  described to address this challenge, namely by:

I.    using layout tracing if layout is available,    II.    validating with visibly gross defect hotspot signal in  the absence of layout information,    III.    using Light Emission from Off-State Leakage Current  (LEOSLC) [7] to identify the internal   $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   of specific  logic blocks.

Each approach will be demonstrated with relevant examples in  the case studies section.

Another challenge is the availability of sufficiently  large metal dimension for microprobing to eliminate the need  for additional sample preparation or circuit edit laid pad.  Tedious sample preparation will limit the throughput and cycle  time as well as the freedom of die selection during fault  isolation. Although it’s a design related challenge, conventional  VLSI physical design inherently helps us in overcoming this  hurdle. Typically, backend-of-line consists of multiple  interconnect layers that serves different purposes. For an 8  metal layer process, the lowest layers such as M1 and M2 are  used as local interconnects to provide routing to the standard  logic cells. The intermediate layers from M3 to M6 are used for  signal routing and the top most layers, M7 and M8, are used for  the global interconnects such as  $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   and   $\mathrm{V_{SS}}$  . Since supply nets  have high current loads, they are often much wider than  standard signal routes [8]. Thus, having V DD  and V SS  traces at  the top layer with wider dimension makes it convenient for  microprobing without the need for additional sample  preparation.

## II.   CASE   STUDIES  

Following section describes in detail the three different  approaches that can be used to identify the suitable microprobe  location. The effectiveness of the microprobe approach has  been demonstrated through the successful debug of the low  yield issues on different products.

## Case Study I : Using Layout Tracing  

A 90nm product suffered from low fault isolation success  rate on several yield issues relating to logic failures.  Conventional method of biasing  $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   and  $\mathrm{V_{SS}}$   pads seemed to be  ineffective. Strangely, the pad level   $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   that supplies power to  the digital logic was defined as 3V which is   ${\sim}2.5$   times higher  than the typical operating voltage of a standard 90nm logic cell.  Hence, it was suspected that power management circuits (PMC)  were in place between the pad level   $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   and the logic cell V DD .  Layout analysis proved the suspect to be true, as pad V DD  and  logic cell  $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   did not form a single net. Layout trace results are  displayed in fig.4a(i) and 4b(i). Black net corresponds to the  pad   $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   net traced from top metal downwards to Metal1. Red  net represents logic cell   $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   trace from Metal 1 upwards to top  metal.  Blue dotted box is the power management circuit which  receives input from pad  $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   and the output drives the internal  V DD  node, V DDX  that powers the logic cells as highlighted by  green arrows. Fig.4a(i) and 4a(ii) showed the V DD  and V SS  pad  probe locations and the corresponding photon emission results  respectively. Strong emission seen from the power management  circuit compared to the light emission (LEOSLC) from digital  logic block implied that power is gated at the PMC from  entering the logic block. Fig.4b(i) and 4b(ii) on the other hand  showed microprobe location on the internal power node, V DDX ,  and the corresponding photon emission obtained. It is clearly  evident that PMC is not emitting and LEOSLC from logic block  is more prominent compared to pad level biasing condition.  This emission signature indicated that microprobing on V DDX

had bypassed the PMC and directed the power to the logic  circuitry.

## Case Study II : Using Defect Induced Emission in the absence  of layout information  

This case study described a different approach to identify the  mircroprobe location in the absence of layout information. A  new product encountered low yield issue due to power short  and leakage failures. I-V curve tracing between   $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   and   $\mathrm{V_{SS}}$  detected short or high leakage on failed dies as compared to  good die as shown in fig 6. Further electrical fault isolation  localized exclusive and identical TIVA and photon emission  hotspot on several bad units as shown in fig 7(a) and 8(a)  respectively. Progressive FIB at the spot consistently revealed  fused signature from active layer until metal 2, as shown in fig  7(b), which appeared to be due to electrical over stress rather  than process related. Fault isolation was revisited to verify  whether the fused signature was the true root cause of the short  failure. Interestingly, NIR imaging on one of the failed die  detected a large anomaly in the logic circuitry yet the hotspot  was still similar to other dies. Fig 8(a) showed the anomaly  circled in red and the identical spot indicated by an arrow. NIR  imaging on more dies revealed similar anomaly randomly  located in logic circuitry. Clearly, conventional fault isolation  approach was ineffective to localize the defects even if they  were gross enough to cause leakage or short between supply  and ground. It was hypothesized that a power management  circuit was blocking the power from channeling to the logic  circuitry and thus gating the defect induced photon emissions.  The identical TIVA spot location was suspected to be part of the  power management circuit that was electrically overstressed  during testing due to a supply bridge in the logic block. This  further shunt the detection of the laser induced signal from the  failure logic circuitry. To achieve static fault isolation success  on this device, an alternative approach was needed to bias the  failing logic block and to visualize a hotspot at the large  anomaly in Fig 8(a). Since conventional approach turned out to  be unsuccessful, microprobing was attempted to identify the  internal node that could give a hotspot at the anomaly. The  sample with large anomaly was subjected to passivation  removal using RIE to expose the top metal and facilitate  microprobing. After several rounds of trial and error,  microprobing on one of the top metal trace, V DDX , (as shown in  fig 8(c)) successfully detected gross signals at the anomaly and  no signal at the suspected power management circuit as shown  in fig.8(b). High magnification image in fig.8(d) clearly depicts  that emission signal overlaid exactly with the anomaly. Top  metal microprobe location of V DDX  is shown in fig.8(c).  Subsequently, microprobing on   $\mathrm{V_{D D X}}$   versus   $\mathrm{V_{SS}}$   pad showed  high leakage compared to a good unit as illustrated in fig.9. This  case study emphasized the effectiveness of using defect induced  emission in the absence of layout information to successfully  identify the microprobe location. Fig.10 illustrated another  successful example on the same product where microprobe  approach managed to localize a weak emission resulting in  functional logic failure and subsequent physical inspection  revealed a minor metal void. This approach was successful on  several low yield issues to isolate BEOL and FEOL defects in  core logic as well as SRAM.

## Case study III : Using Light Emission due to Off-State  Leakage Current to identify internal V DD  of specific logic  blocks.  

Static fault isolation on another   $40\ \mathrm{~nm}$   product also  encountered similar roadblock. Fault isolation success rate was  very low and even for those successful cases; only defects  residing within 1/3 digital blocks (block B) could be localized  through given digital  $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   pad. This raised a suspicion whether  the other 2 logic blocks (A and C) were power gated. Thorough  device characterization was carried out to validate the biasing  condition. Fig 11(a) showed the PEM overlay image of  LEOSLC of a good die upon biasing  $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   and  $\mathrm{V_{SS}}$   pads. As seen  from the image, LEOSLC was detected only from digital block  B whereas no LEOSLC from blocks A and C. This implied,  power to blocks A and C could be gated by power management  circuits. Microprobing approach was thus applied to identify  the appropriate top metal trace to supply power and obtain  LEOSLC from the power gated blocks. After several rounds of  experimentation, two independent metal traces   $\mathrm{V_{D D X}}$   and V DDY   were identified that powered block A and C respectively. Fig  11(b) and 11(c) showed the LEOSLC of the respective blocks A  and C. Combined biasing of  $\mathrm{V_{DD}\left(p a d\right)+V_{D D X}\left(\upmu p r o b e\right)+V_{D D Y}}$  (µprobe) versus V SS  (pad) successfully powered up the entire  digital logic as shown in fig 11(d). Optical image in fig 11(e)  showed the probing of  $\mathrm{V_{DD}}$   pad, and microprobing of V DDX  and  V DDY . The alternative 4 pin biasing condition was validated  using a recently solved issue where defect was observable from  backside NIR imaging. Fig 12(a) showed the PEM overlay of a  failed die obtained by conventional biasing of V DD  and V SS  pads.  Physical anomaly highlighted by dotted box was clearly evident,  however no emission spots were observed. This proved that

conventional biasing was insufficient to access the failure. Then  fault isolation was attempted using 4 pin microprobe setup and  gross signals were successfully localized in block C as shown in  fig 12(b). Applying this 4 pin setup certainly improved the fault  isolation success in future cases.

## III. C ONCLUSION 

In summary, the prevailing complexity in static fault isolation  due to power management circuits in integrated circuits was  discussed. Microprobing approach was demonstrated as an  alternative approach and the practical challenges involved in  the implementation were also discussed in 3 different case  studies. First case study illustrated a straightforward approach,  where layout was used to identify the microprobe locations.  Next two case studies were more complex due to  non-availability of layout information. In the second case study,  defect induced photo emission was used as an indicator to  identify the right metal trace for microprobe, whereas in the  third case study, LEOSLC was used as a gauge to identify the  power gated blocks and the suitable microprobe locations to  power them up. These approaches also illustrated the  significance of device characterization to validate the biasing  condition and thereby improve the static fault isolation success  rate.
