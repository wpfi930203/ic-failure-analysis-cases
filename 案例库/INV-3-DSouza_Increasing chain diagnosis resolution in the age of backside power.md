## Increasing chain diagnosis resolution in the age of  backside power  

Jayant D’Souza   Siemens EDA, 8005 SW Boeckman Road, Wislonville, OR, USA Phone : +1 (503) 685 7023, Email :  jayant.dsouza@siemens.com

Abstract —Scan chain diagnosis has long been used to  identify manufacturing defects and yield issues in early ramp.  Identifying and eliminating these yield issues quickly and  efficiently are required to ensure business success of a product.  Root causing defect mechanisms that produce yield loss require  a combination of fault isolation and failure analysis. The advent  of new technologies, like backside power, in advanced process  nodes have made fault isolation extremely challenging. In this  paper, we present results from three new software-based  technologies that provide accurate localization to enable  efficient failure analysis of both front-end of line and back-end  of line defects. Additionally, these technologies can alleviate the  pressure on fault isolation for front-end of line defects and  provide reduced area for back-end of line defects for Physical  Failure Analysis (PFA).

Keywords—chain diagnosis, scan diagnosis, yield, yield ramp,  fault isolation, defect localization, failure analysis, silicon defects,  FEOL, BEOL

## I.    B ACKGROUND 

Manufacturing defects can occur throughout the product  lifecycle. During the early yield ramp phase, identifying these  defects and eliminating them quickly are critical for product  profitability. For digital ICs, scan chains, inserted during the  design-for-test (DFT) stage of the design flow, are heavily  leveraged to test the logic in the design. These scan chains  form a major part of the digital logic in the design. When  defects occur in the scan chains of the design, it prevents  testing of the logic of that design. However, defects on the  scan chain also provide extremely valuable data to improve  defectivity on a process node. The defects on scan chains are  heavily leveraged during the early ramp of a process and  during new product introduction (NPI).

## II.   I NTRODUCTION 

Scan patterns generated by an automatic test pattern  generator (ATPG) are used to create test stimuli that  structurally test the logic of the design. These stimuli are also  known as logic test patterns or test vectors. As part of the test  pattern generation process, chain tests that validate the  integrity of the structure of the scan chains are also generated.  These test patterns comprising both logic and chain tests are  then applied to the design using automatic test equipment  (ATE) during the manufacturing test phase in the flow shown  below in Fig. 1.

Since scan chains are the backbone of the digital test  infrastructure, it follows that diagnosing defects on these  chains is the first order of business since they can also indicate  systematic defect mechanisms that a process or product are  prone to. Identifying and addressing these systematic defects  has been heavily utilized during yield ramp. The faster these  defects are identified and addressed the quicker the yield  ramp.

During early yield and product ramp, defects found in the  scan chains form the largest proportion of the defect  population. The proportion of scan chain defects have been  reported to be as high as   $70\%$  . As systematic defect  mechanisms are identified during early ramp and addressed,  the yield increases. For more mature process nodes (7nm and  above), as systematic defect mechanisms found in scan chains  were addressed, the process matured. At that point the scan  chain failures tended to settle at around   $30\%$   of the defect  population as the number of logic defects occupied a large  percentage of the population. One possible explanation for this  phenomenon is that defects tend to be either systematic or  random. As systematic defects in the chain are addressed, the  entire process yield improves and random defects and other  systematic defects in the logic become more prevalent in the  defect population. The result of scan chain diagnosis is a set of logical and  physical locations (called suspects) with possible defect types  that localize the possible defect area on the failing die. These  suspect locations are used to guide the failure analysis process  in the determination of the defect mechanism causing the  observed defect phenomenon.

When more than a single suspect location exists in the  diagnosis reports, fault isolation is performed on each of the  suspects to further localize the defect prior to the destructive  physical failure analysis process. The complexity of advanced  process nodes such as 5nm, 4nm, 3nm and 2nm as well as the  feature sizes on those nodes have made PFA extremely time  consuming and complex. Small feature sizes and features like  backside power delivery have made fault isolation extremely  challenging or downright impossible to use in some cases.  Backside power delivery technology is a technique where the  power to the device is delivered from the back of wafer, not  through the interconnects (front side of the wafer). Traditional  optical fault isolation techniques use the backside of the wafer  to expose transistors and localize defects. The presence of  metal layers due to backside power delivery technology

presents a major challenge to these traditional optical  techniques. The requirement for chain diagnosis has been to  provide higher resolution diagnosis (fewer suspects) to enable  the faster yield ramp for these advanced nodes.

In this paper, the results of three new technologies are  presented to diagnose defects that impact chain defects at  these advanced nodes. These new technologies not only  produce accurate diagnosis results but also dramatically  reduce the suspect area to be inspected by PFA.

## III.   C HAIN DIAGNOSIS RESOLUTION 

Chain diagnosis has proven to be accurate across multiple  nodes and is a trusted tool for PFA guidance and yield  learning. For static defects, the current state-of-the-art chain  diagnosis results have been reported to have over   $80\%$    accuracy with around   $25\%$   of these cases comprising single  suspect diagnoses. Additionally chain diagnosis results  containing up to 3 suspects on average account for  approximately   $65\%$   of chain diagnosis results. These  diagnosis cases (including up to 3 suspects) do not require  much (if any) fault isolation and can result in the part being  taken to PFA directly. This empirical and consistent  observation across different process nodes and design styles  provide a proof point that most chain defects can accurately  be diagnosed. Analyzing the chain defect population for more  advanced nodes, indicates that some newer defects tend to  have a larger and more consistent effect on the defect  population. New technologies to address defects in the backend of line (BEOL) and front-end of line (FEOL) are  presented with some results in the subsequent sections.

## IV. BACK-END OF LINE CHAIN DEFECTS

Analysis of chain failure data for modern process nodes,  indicate that defects continue to impact the back-end of line  (BEOL) layers. These defects are both in the shift path or in  a global control signal like clock, scan enable, set or reset.  The following sub-sections indicate that could be due to  defects in the scan shift path between two scan cells or in  global control signals like clock, scan enable, set, or reset. In  this section, we show how newly developed techniques can  be applied to further reduce the search area thereby  improving the PFA effectiveness. For each of the cases  below, simulation-based injection was used on a smaller  open-source design to re-create an actual silicon failure on a  real device with PFA confirmation.

## C.   Defects in global control signals  

Global control signals like clock, scan enable, set and  reset can also contain defects. The population of such defects  has grown in more advanced nodes. Previous diagnosis  technologies typically ignored these kinds of defects because  their occurrences are somewhat rare and were assumed to  result in catastrophic failures. However, as indicated in [1]  defects in clock trees can also result in scan shift failures. This  kind of defect typically manifests as multiple failing chains.

A recent study of volume silicon data presented in [1]  shows that   ${>}23\%$   of defect population were attributed to  global control signal defects i.e. defects in either clock, scan  enable, set or reset.

New advances in chain diagnosis technology allow for the  accurate diagnosis and a more highly resolved result of these  defects as well.

## V.  FRONT END OF LINE DEFECTS

Defects in the front-end of line (FEOL) have started to  become more prevalent at advanced nodes. Another  interesting change in the design is the increased number of  multibit flip flops (MBFFs). Multibit flip flops (MBFFs) are  the result of a design technique where discrete flip flops that  are adjacent to each other in layout and function as a shift  register, can be combined together in a single standard cell.  Since these cells offer reduced area, lower power, and better  timing, they are preferred in physical implementation flows.  Up to   $80\%$   of the scan chains of modern designs are  comprised of MBFFs. Typical scan diagnosis analysis (for  chain and logic) ends at the standard cell boundary. By  default, defects inside the cell are not specifically called out.  The sheer number of MBFFs and the increasing number of  defects in the front-end layers require a new technique to  analyze and ensure that diagnosis resolution and accuracy are  met for these modern designs.

New technology comprised of enhanced switch-level  simulation is used to determine different functional modes in  these complex cells.  As shown in the Fig.8 below, this  simulation can help distinguish between transistors and nets  inside the cell (highlighted in red and blue).

## VII.   C ONCLUSIONS 

The latest advances in scan chain diagnosis reduce  the number of suspects in the diagnosis report dramatically.  This reduced number has a direct impact on the area of the  die to be analyzed during failure analysis. In some cases, fault  isolation can be skipped entirely, and the part taken directly  to PFA. This can lead to huge savings in time, reducing the  overall time to root cause a defect significantly. As evidenced  by this paper, scan chain diagnosis will likely play a more  pivotal role in failure analysis and yield analysis for advanced  process nodes.
