## Effects of the Interfacial Layer on the Leakage Current  and Hysteresis Behaviour of Ferroelectric Devices

Abstract —Data collected from electrical characterization on  Metal – Ferroelectric – Insulator - Semiconductor (MFIS) and  Metal – Ferroelectric – Metal (MIM) Ferroelectric (FE) devices  are compared. Although numerous studies exist on both device  structures, a direct comparison of the differences in the  signatures of degradation during electrical characterization is  lacking. In this work, analysis of Interrupted CVS and transient  current measurements using the PUND waveform alongside  pulsed cycling stress indicate that the interfacial layer (IL) in the  MFIS structure is the origin of the discrepancies and that the IL  influences the collected data through increasing charge trapping  and defect generation, as well as modifying the band energy  diagram of the device stack. The qualitative studies of electrical  characterization data provide a starting point for quantitative  analysis and identification of the physical mechanisms underlying  the observed degradation.

Keywords- Ferroelectric, Constant Voltage Stress, MFIS, MIM,   Electrical Characterization, Defect Distribution, Trap Assisted  Tunneling.

## I.      I NTRODUCTION  

Hafnia-based ferroelectric (FE) devices are one of the  primary candidates for an emerging non-volatile memory  (eNVM) due to their CMOS compatibility [1], scalability,  simple 1T memory cell architecture and low write energy [2].

While the Metal – Ferroelectric – Metal (MIM) device  structure is highly suited for studies on the intrinsic FE  properties   of   hafnia-based   materials,   many   practical  applications require the use of a transistor structure, where a  dielectric Interfacial Layer (IL) is deposited between the FE  layer and the Si substrate to produce a Metal – Ferroelectric –  Insulator - Semiconductor (MFIS) device. However, the IL is  a prominent source of reliability issues [3-9]. Physical  phenomena such as charge trapping [3, 7-8], wake-up and  fatigue [7, 9] operate differently in  different device structures,  due to the presence or absence of the IL. While many studies  have been conducted solely on hafnia-based FE devices  separately using MFIS [6-9] and MIM [1, 10, 11] structures, to  our knowledge there has not been a direct comparison between  the two device structures and an analysis on what the  differences in structure entails. Therefore, this work aims to  make qualitative comparisons between the MFIS and MIM  device structures by examining the differences in the  signatures   of   degradation   measured   using   electrical  characterization. The goal is to aid in the analysis of electrical  characterization and pave the way for quantitative analysis and  pinpointing of the underlying mechanisms behind the observed  degradation resulting from electrical stressing.

## III.    D ATA /R ESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

A.   Comparison of J-t characteristics between MFIS and  MIM capacitor devices

Figure 4 shows the difference in  $J_{-t}$   characteristics between  the two device structures when stressed with interrupted CVS.  As shown in Figure 4(a), the leakage current density across the  MFIS devices increases with time both within and between the  CVS cycles. An initial rapid increase in current density in each  cycle can be observed, followed by a subsequent slowing of  this increase. Such  $J_{-t}$    characteristics suggest that the defects  are generated within the device stack over the course of the  stressing and that this increase in current is limited by the  number of traps available for Trap-Assisted Tunnelling [13],  or enhancement of the tunnelling barrier by trapped charges  [14-15]. An abrupt decrease in leakage current can also be  observed between each CVS stressing cycle, after each  interruption, which is indicative of charge detrapping [13]  occurring within the  ${\sim}50\upmu\mathrm{s}$   interval between the application of  CVS. Conversely, from Figure 4(b), the   $J_{-t}$   in MIM devices  decreases with time between each cycle of CVS stressing. The  inset of Figure 4(b) also indicates that the  $J_{-t}$    decreases during  each cycle of stressing as well, with an abrupt increase in  current between cycles as a result of the interruption of CVS.  This behaviour is also indicative of charge trapping [16-18],  which similar to the MFIS case enhances the tunnelling barrier  and reduces the magnitude of leakage current over the course  of each cycle of CVS. The abrupt increases in current between  CVS cycles is also indicative of detrapping occurring between  cycles [16-18]. It should also be noted that the leakage currents  across the MFIS structures is roughly 5 orders of magnitude.  The primary difference between the two device structures is  the presence of observable defect generation, as evidenced by  the increasing current in the MFIS devices. In the case of the  MFIS structure, during the application of the positive CVS the  overall polarization state of the device is switched to point  downwards. A large electric field across the   $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$  (IL) is  present and this field is further enhanced by the presence of the  downwards polarization of the FE-HZO layer [19-20], as  illustrated in Figure 5. Given that the SiO 2  IL is often known  to be sub-stoichiometric and contain large amounts of oxygen  vacancies due to interactions with the deposited HZO layer

[21-22], and that charge transport across the device stack is  primarily assisted by traps (defects) located in the IL [23-24],  it is likely that the difference in  $J_{-t}$   behaviour between the two  device structures can be explained by the presence of the   $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$  IL and its effects on charge trapping and defect generation.

## IV.    C ONCLUSIONS 

Interrupted CVS and  P-E  loop electrical characterizations  were conducted on MFIS and MIM devices. The differences in  the signatures of degradation measured using electrical  characterization and the possible mechanisms and causes  behind the observations were discussed. The IL was identified  in multiple different comparisons to be the origin of the  discrepancies between MFIS and MIM devices.   From the  Interrupted CVS experiments, it was found that the MFIS  devices experience larger leakage currents and observable  defect generation over the course of the application of voltage  stress, compared to the MIM structure, due to the physical  nature of the IL. Charge trapping and detrapping processes  were also identified as an integral part of the analysis of  J-t  data. From the comparisons of cycling stress and P-E loop  data, it can be deduced that the IL is the cause of the  discrepancies between the two device structures as well, due to  the asymmetry of the device stack. The presence of qualitative  differences in results from electrical characterization between  MFIS and MIM structures highlights the differences in  physical mechanisms affecting each device. Understanding  these differences is of paramount importance in electrical  characterization and allows for quantitative analysis of the  underlying mechanisms behind the observed degradation  resulting from electrical stressing.

This study assumes that the differences in capacitor sizes  and shapes do not affect the electrical characterizations  performed on the devices. Furthermore, the MFIS and MIM  samples were provided by different collaborators, which could  result in differences caused by variations in fabrication  conditions. However, this study focuses primarily on the  qualitative aspects of the measurement data, which should be  accounted for by the differences in device structure rather than  factors pertaining to fabrication conditions or the shape of the  capacitor pads. Due to the complexity of calculating the  electric fields across each layer and substrate of the MFIS  structure, more involved calculations accounting for the  nonidealities of the SiO 2  layer and Si substrate have been  conducted using the Ginestra Simulation Platform to properly  determine the field drops. A relative permittivity of 6.6 for the

IL (more typical of the thicknesses considered) and of 21 for  the HZO layer have been used. While this calculated field  value of  $2.53\mathrm{~MV/cm}$   differs from the 2.87 MV/cm from the  calculations outlined in Section II, this overestimation of the  applied electric field means that the difference in  $J_{-t}$   magnitude  between MFIS and MIM devices described in Section III  should be even more significant.

Potential extensions of this work could include  comparisons of simulations of the defect drift and generation  rates in each device structure, which will provide quantitative  measures of the differences in electrical characterization data  between the two device structures.
