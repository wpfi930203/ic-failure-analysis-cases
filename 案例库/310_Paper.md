## Study of Silicon thickness for electron  

transparency

Tan HK\*, Liu BH, Chooi ML, Hua YN, and Li XM  Physical Failure Analysis Department, Wintech Nano-Technology Services Pte. Ltd, 10 Science Park Road #03-26  \*   Phone number +65 67777354, Fax Number +65 67772462 Email:  Hankeat@wintech-nano.com

Abstract-   As technology scales, transmission electron microscope  (TEM) has become one of the most widely used analysis tool in  characterization and failure analysis. This paper introduces  techniques on ensuring electron transparency for TEM analysis  using secondary electron emission, backscatter electron emission  and X-ray emission, all of which could be found within a  scanning electron microscope (SEM) system.

Keywords  –  TEM sample preperation, FIB, SEM, EDX,  Silicon thickness .

## I.   I NTRODUCTION 

Technology scaling on products has increased the  demand for nanoscale analysis which utilizes TEM for failure  analysis and material characterization. Electron transparency  on the analytical sample is required for analysis techniques  such as TEM and transmission electron backscatter diffraction  (t-EBSD). Unique specimens that only allow one sample  preparation attempt pushes demand for a qualifying method  for electron transparency during sample preparation.

The quality of TEM analysis depends on the sample’s  electron transparency. TEM imaging involves the scattering of  electron as electrons transmit through the sample. A thicker  sample that is not electron transparent would cause electrons  to undergo multiple scatterings which results in poor  analytical data. An ideal thickness of a TEM sample would  dependent on electron mean free path in the material which is  typically  ${<}100\mathrm{nm}$   for high resolution TEM analysis. There is  however no developed methods to measure electron  transparency while preparing TEM samples. As such there  would be occurrence of TEM samples being too thick for  analysis after the sample has been loaded into the TEM.

When analytical samples are rejected by TEM, the increased  exposure to atmosphere reduces the success rate of TEM  analysis. Use of materials such as copper and silver in many  devices places a requirement to reduce the time between  sample preparation and analysis to prevent diffusion.

Focused ion beam systems (FIB) equipped with a SEM is the  tool of choice for sample preparation. FIB allows precise  thinning of sample for electron transparency. The sample  thickness after FIB preparation is however not easily  interpreted.

In this paper, a method of using SEM to estimate silicon  thickness is suggested. Secondary electrons, backscattered  electrons, and X-ray signals are collected and correlated with  silicon thickness. Monte Carlo simulations are used to explain  the   observations   and   verified   using   cross   section  measurements.

A 5keV SEM secondary electron image is shown in Fig.  4(a). It is observed that there is high secondary electron  intensity at thin silicon thickness. This trend is observed using  various acceleration voltages and more pronounced in higher  acceleration voltages. In Fig. 4(b), the different intensities of  the electron yield   using different accelerating voltages are  plotted.

The intensity values can be influenced by brightness and  contrast adjustments; however the inflection points where the  intensity increases are based on the penetration depth of the  primary electron. We attribute this increment in intensity from  additional secondary electron yield when there are secondary  electrons emitted from the both sides of the lamella as primary  electron penetrate the sample. In a bulk sample, only surface  secondary electrons are collected due to the low escape depth  of secondary electrons.

Fig. 4(b), we observe constant intensity before increasing  gradually when silicon is at 200nm thickness. The secondary  electron intensity increases at a faster rate when under   $100\mathrm{nm}$  thick. A change in  $5\mathrm{keV}$   secondary electron intensity can then  be used to indicate that the sample is under   $200\mathrm{nm}$   thick.

These inflection points in the secondary electron intensity are  based on electron – sample interactions and could be used to  determine sample thickness .

In Fig.4(c), we observe the isocontours indicating the energy  remaining in the primary electron beam as it strikes the silicon  surface. About   $5{\sim}10\%$   of the primary electron energy loss  happens at the   $150\mathrm{nm}\cdot200\mathrm{nm}$   thickness. Part of this energy  loss is converted to secondary electron production which is  detected by our secondary electron detector seen from the  increase in secondary electron intensity in Fig. 4(b).

From Fig.4(c), Electrons with   $10\%$   of energy left can reach  200nm; however from Fig.4 (b), there are no obvious increase  in secondary electron intensity beyond  $200\mathrm{nm}$   and any data is  likely obscured lack of detector efficiency or by detector noise.

Fig. 4(d) shows different acceleration voltages and its  corresponding electron intensity curves. Different electron  energy on different material substrates can be investigated  further to aid in TEM lamella preparations.

Figure 6 shows the X-ray emission when the sample  thickness changes from thick to thin. X-ray count reduces with  the sample thickness. The interaction volume of the electron  that produces X-rays is reduced in thinned samples, resulting  in lower X ray counts as sample thickness is reduced.

X-ray counts consist of two types of X-rays. Bremsstrahlung  process where the X-ray yield is based on beam current, atomic  number and acceleration voltage, and characteristic X ray  production which is based on fluorescence yield [3]. These  parameters are kept constant in this pure silicon sample and  therefore the only variable would be the interaction volume.

The percentage of X ray counts relative to bulk silicon can  then be referenced to a calibration curve such as fig.(6) to tell  the silicon thickness.

## IV.   C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduced methods for non-destructive  quantification of lamella thickness using different techniques  to ensure the success rate of TEM inspection during failure  analysis and material characterization.
