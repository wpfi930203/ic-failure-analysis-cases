## Advances in high-resolution non-destructive defect  detection and localization enhanced by intelligent  signal processing  

Sebastian Brand 1 , Michael Kögel 2 , Christian Grosse 1 , Pascal Gounet 2 , Christian Hollerith 3 , Frank Altmann 1 1 Electronic Materials and Components, Fraunhofer IMWS, Halle,Germany  2 Product Quality and Reliability, ST-Microelectronics, Grenoble, France  3 Infineon Technologies AG, München, Germany  Phone : (+49)345 5589 193, Email :  Sebastian.Brand@imws.Fraunhofer.de

Abstract —   In   development   and   production   of  microelectronics products the assessment of the condition of  either the full component or only specific parts is of high  relevance. To allow for screening and monitoring and to leave  the part unaltered the inspection techniques are required to  operate non-destructively. While this allows for full-cover final  inspection it also enables repetitive monitoring beneficial for the  exploration of material interactions, potential subsequent defect  formations and consequently for failure isolation. With  increasing complexity inherent in advancing microelectronic  technologies, high reliability, robustness and thus, successful  failure analysis is essential. The correspondingly shrinking  feature sizes and the involvement of heterogeneous materials  highly challenge existing techniques. Furthermore, the  interpretation of the acquired data becomes increasingly  difficult requiring specifically skilled operating personal. In  previous studies machine learning (ML) approaches have been  developed and evaluated for their ability to analyze signals  acquired by scanning acoustic microscopy (SAM) with the goal  of automated defect detection, characterization, and failure  isolation. The present paper investigates different ML  architectures to analyze the time signals after transformation  into the spectral- and wavelet domains. Results showed that 2D  CNNs analyzing the acquired acoustic signals in the wavelet  domain representation performed best, however at the expense  of additional computational effort. Furthermore, ML-based  analysis was explored for its potential to locate and isolate  electrically active defects in the depth-dimension based on  thermal emissions using lock-in thermography (LIT). Obtained  LIT-related results are promising, however require further  research to fully enfold its potential. It was further found that  transfer properties of the inspection tools interfere with the  defect specific signal features and thus so far tie the trained  models to the specific equipment used. Future work should  therefore focus on removing the specific tool related transfer  characteristics of the equipment from the measurement data to  allow for intra-tool compatibility and thus a more generalized  application. ( Abstract )

Keywords—Acoustic Microscopy, non-destructive defect  localization, lock-in thermography, Machine Learning, AI  Introduction

In the failure analysis chain, but also for investigating the  physics behind failures and in the assessment of the product  quality are non-destructively operating inspection- and  analysis techniques of superior relevance. Resulting from their  non-destructive character inspected parts and components  remain unaltered allowing for repetitive application and thus  for screening and monitoring to investigate specific features  and their condition. Repetitive monitoring is highly valuable  for investigating the formation of anomalies and their  propagation into a defect, thus enabling the exploration of

material interaction under operation and stress conditions. The  ongoing increase of assistance and automation across the  industrial, automotive and consumer sector lead to elevated  reliability expectations that meet with increasingly complex  power- and microelectronic components and systems. To  ensure performance and the safety of electronic products,  defect affinity, -formation, and -propagation need to be  investigated and understood in detail. Besides repetitive  analyses for research and exploration purposes, broad range  inspection abilities with accurate and reliable recognition  performance are mandatory for quality inspection and  monitoring, inevitable for high-reliability and premium  products. Consequently, the high complexity of the electronic  components, the potential need for a high-throughput  inspection, the requirement of non-destructivity and the need  for high-precision detections must be combined, resulting in a  large amount of data that must be analyzed on-the-fly with  high level of performance. Due to its non-destructivity  microscopic acoustic imaging is a widely employed technique  in quality assessment, technology development and failure  analysis. Depending on the specimen’s composition it allows  for spatial resolutions on the order of hundreds of microns  down to the single digit micron-range. The contrast arises  from the interaction of the incident elastic wave with the  sample’s internal objects and their mechanical properties,  respectively. Caused by the decreasing feature sizes combined  with an increasing material variety in microelectronic devices  the incident wave gets reflected and scattered resulting in echo  signals commonly containing trains of interfering pulses.  Interpretation of the reflection signals, received by the  acoustic probe becomes increasingly difficult, requiring  profound expertise of the failure analysis engineer. Previous  studies have positively evaluated the potential of machine  learning for analyzing and interpretation of acoustic data for  increasing inspection reliability and the level of automation  for defect identification and isolation [1, 2]. These studies  have employed 1D convolutional neural network architectures  for analyzing the shape of the acoustic signals in time domain  [3, 4] and to identify defects based on the acoustic signature.  Other work focused on ML-based image analysis to recognize  shapes in acoustic micrographs that potentially correspond to  a defect site [5] or to improve image resolution using machine  learning based algorithms [6].

The present work extends the analysis of individual  acoustic signals from the pure time domain to the spectral and  wavelet domains and correspondingly the model architectures  from 1D to 2D. While the information content in the time  signals is identical, representation in spectral – and wavelet  domain is expected to provide additional degrees of freedom  which have the potential to contribute towards an increased

## I.    M ATERIALS AND  M ETHODS 

## A.   Analysis of Acoustic Microscopy Data  

Scanning acoustic microscopy was conducted on three  sample sets and signals were acquired in time domain and  stored for evaluating the ML-based analyses approach.

## 1)   Description of Samples and Equipment  

Acoustic inspection was performed on two sets of nonmolded flip-chips and a set of glued power devices (thin-die  setup). “Set-I” contained flip-chips with an edge length of the

Si-die of   $6\,\mathrm{\textrm{x}}7\,\mathrm{\textrm{mm}}$   and a thickness of   $350~\ensuremath{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$  . Flip-chip  interconnects of these samples were  $50\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$   in lateral diameter  at a pitch of   $135~\upmu\mathrm{m}$  . “Set-II” contained flip-chips with edge  lengths of the die of 7 x 7 mm and a thickness of   $85~\upmu\mathrm{m}$  . The  interconnects of these samples were   $80~\ensuremath{\,\upmu\mathrm{m}}$   and   $120~\ensuremath{\,\upmu\mathrm{m}};$  respectively at varying pitch. Both sample sets were purposely  taken out from the manufacturing process prior to applying  underfill material. Extensive stressing was performed on both  sets for accelerated aging and initiation of defect formation in  response to thermo-mechanical stresses. Samples of “Set-II”  were placed alternately in boiling water and liquid nitrogen to  generate sufficient thermo-mechanical stress to initiate bumpfractures. Samples of “Set-I” were extracted from reliability  testing where they underwent multiple flip-chip attach reflow  cycles in which samples are exposed to temperature gradients  between room temperature and   $240^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  . “Set-III” contained  power devices with a thin Si-die glued onto a metal backplate.  Voids in the die-attach result in a decreased electrical and  thermal conductivity and are to be avoided. Due to the thin Sithicknesses, a   $2.4\,\mathrm{\mm\200\,\mathrm{\MHz}}$   acoustic transducer was  employed for data acquisition for the sample Sets “I” and  “III”, and a 3.2 mm focal length 200 MHz transducer was used  for inspecting the samples of “Set-II”. A conventional acoustic  microscope containing a scanner, a pulser/receiver and a  digitizer was employed. Received signals were digitized at a  rate of 1GS/s and a resolution of 8bit and stored on the  microscope internal hard drive.

## 2)   Signal pre-processing and ML-based analysis  

Acoustic signals can be considered as a time series in  which amplitude values are represented as a function of their  temporal occurrence. When transformed into the spectral  domain the exact same information is represented as a

function of frequency. The conversion of a time series of  measurement values is computed through the Fourier  transformation and results in a complex valued vector  representing amplitude and phase at the various frequencies.  While the information in both domains remains constant, the  representation differs, providing an additional degree of  freedom for information extraction as the signal is expressed  in terms of periodicities and thus may allow for a more  accurate class assignment in the ML-based analysis.

The left column of Fig. 1. shows the acoustic signal as a  function of time of three distinct objects in a flip-chip device  of “Set-I”. These are Top: a fractured bump, Vertical Center:  an intact bump, Bottom row: an underfill delamination. The  center column contains the power spectra of the three signals.  The temporal information is contained when the real part and  the imaginary part of the spectra is summed. This signal  condition will be referred to as “real+imag” in the remainder  of this paper. A more intuitive way for signal representation  in the spectral domain would be in terms of power distribution  as a function of frequency. The spectral power distribution can  be computed from the absolute values of the complex valued  spectrum. The power spectrum however does not contain  temporal information, which is why it was not considered for  application here.

The experimentally obtained acoustic data were separated  into four classes and used for training two different types of  1D CNN architectures. These classes where: “defective  Bumps”, intact Bumps”, “delamination of underfill” and  “background/ undefined”. It was observed in previous work  that the number of misclassifications is substantially reduced  when providing a separate class for signals that do not belong  to one of the other classes and was considered here for  increasing classification performance. The 1D architectures  contained one-dimensional input layers. For processing such twodimensional matrices, a machine learning architecture  requires a two-dimensional input layer. The architecture  employed here was a modified version of the GoogleNet  model [8] containing a 2D-input layer. The model is pretrained designed for recognition of structures and shapes in  two-dimensional images. In the present work it is employed  for characterization and classification of the wavelet matrices  obtained from the acoustic signals through CWT. ## B.   Lock-In thermography data acquisition and ML-analysis

Its sensitivity reaches down to several   $\upmu\mathrm{W}$   of  dissipated power. While, due to the application of highly  magnifying infrared optics a high lateral resolution down to  the range below   $10\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$   can be achieved a precise localization  in the axial dimension can still be difficult to obtain. ## II.   R ESULTS AND  D ISCUSSION 

The purpose of the presented work is the development and  evaluation of machine learning based solutions for the  analysis of transient measurement data with respect to their  application for interpretation and classification in the context  of non-destructive defect identification and localization in  complex microelectronic packages. Furthermore, the study  aims at investigating the performances of different model  architectures for the analysis of signals obtained from  differing samples and inspection methods. The methods  addressed here are acoustic microscopy and lock-in  thermography. The following section presents and discusses  the results obtained with the materials and methods described  above.

## B.   Lock-In Thermography  

Lock-in thermography is a highly sensitive technique for  the detection and localization of even minor electrical  disfunction when accompanied by thermal emissions. As  three-dimensional architectures become increasingly relevant  in microelectronics the defect localization needs to also extend  to the third dimension. Previous approaches employed the  temporal delay between electrical excitation and the reception  of the thermal emissions in terms of phase to pinpoint the  defect as the thermal source in depth [7, 9]. While this  approach is generally valid, the practical application requires  complex calibration and detailed knowledge about the  samples structure to obtain a precise depth estimate. The  present research investigated the general applicability of  machine learning based approaches to pinpoint defect related  sources of thermal emission in the depth dimension inside a  specific sample. This is in contrasts to previous work which  has investigated unsupervised machine learning for the  separation of thermal sources but has not addressed direct  axial defect localization [10]. Evaluated for this purpose were  the three ML models (2x 1D, 1x 2D) as described above.  Analogous to the acoustic data analysis time-resolved thermal  responses were pre-processed by Fast Fourier transformation  and applied to the 1D model architecture. TRTRs were also  CWT-transformed into the time-frequency domain and the 2D  modified GoogleNet model has been applied. In extension to

the previously described ML-based analyses the models have  additionally been varied by implementation of a regression  output layer (vs. classification output layer) for directly  deriving quantitative depth estimates. ## III.   S UMMARY AND  C ONCLUSIONS 

The paper describes the development, application and  evaluation of machine learning based signal analysis in 1D  and 2D for non-destructive defect detection, identification and  localization in failure analysis of microelectronic components.  The methods addressed data that were acquired by either  acoustic microscopy or lock-in thermography. The potential  of the approach is demonstrated by four case studies including  automated bump inspection in flip-chip devices and  delamination recognition in a power device containing a thin  Si-die by high resolution acoustic analysis. Furthermore, the  quantitative localization of the depth of a thermally active  defect using lock-in thermography through both, classification  and regression was investigated. While for the acousticsrelated analysis the potential is quite evident, a reliable  application of machine learning for the application in lock-in  thermography although promising, still requires further  research. The observations made further lead to the conclusion  that transfer properties of the measurement equipment largely  influence the signals shape both in the time- and the frequency  domain. In the case of the acoustic analysis, the data recorded  are affected by the acoustic transducers spectral characteristics  that influence the length and overall shape of the acoustic  pulses that get reflected from the samples internal structure.  As the ML-algorithms employed target recognition of shapes  in either the time- spectral- or time-frequency domain, the  biasing owed to the equipment transfer characteristics largely  ties the applicability of a trained ML-network to the  specifically used equipment and thus prevents a general  applicability and acceptance. Future research should therefore  focus on the removal of the equipment related data  falsification.
