## New Approaches in ESD Risk Measurement of PCB  Assembling Machines and ESD Countermeasures  

Peter Jacob 1, \*

1 Empa Duebendorf, Electronics & Reliability Center, Ueberlandstr. 129, CH-8600 Duebendorf, Switzerland  \*Phone: +41 58 765 4288 Fax: +41 58 765 6954 Email:  peter.jacob@empa.ch

Abstract-   The ESD risk evaluation of automated PCB (printed  circuit boards) assembly machines became extremely difficult in  the last years: Accelerated process speeds and limited access to  critical positions made direct measurements impossible. A new  test board with a contactless voltage probe attached is presented,  providing precise in-situ measurement of electrostatic charging of  the PCB. Surprising results were achieved, opening a new view on  different ESD charging and discharging scenarios during the  component   placement   process.   In   conclusion   of   these  measurements, also new ESD protection approaches have to be  considered, which avoid friction electricity between the nozzle and  components to be placed.

Keywords – ESD in PCB assembly, assembly tool ESD risks, ESD  risk evaluation in assembly machines, charging risks in pick&place

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

The assembly of printed circuit boards (PCB) is known to be  one of the most risky process steps with respect to ESD [1].  However, due to the extreme speed of modern assembly  machines and their parallel operation using multi-nozzle heads  (to pick 40 and more devices at the same step for assembling  them), ESD risk evaluation on these tools became very difficult  or even impossible. At the same time, such tool-integrated ESD  protection becomes more and more important, since the  device-integrated ESD protection becomes more and more  limited with respect to extremely high degree of integration  (which also reduces space for ESD protection circuitry),  ultra-high frequency devices and multi-stack-packages.

## II.   PRESENT   METHODS   OF   ESD   RISK   MEASUREMENTS  

At present, ESD debugging of assembly machines is done by a  series of singularized measurements. At first, the grounding of  metal parts is checked, followed by the control of the electric  dissipation of the picker nozzles. In addition, the materials of  reels, tapes, cover tapes etc. are characterized on electric  dissipation by measuring their surface resistivity. Finally, it is  tried to measure the voltage of devices hanging on the picker  nozzle (by stopping the nozzle movement and measuring the  electrostatic potential by a contactless voltage probe) and when  landed onto the PCB. However, due to various limitations, all of  these measurements suffer severe deviations from reality: the  contactless voltage probe’s region of measurement is (with an  averaging measurement area of about 15mm) by far bigger than  the size of most components. Fast movements, which generate  electrostatic potentials are interrupted and dissipation  measurements on small geometries are difficult or even

impossible and frequently do not allow to provide useful  conclusions on charge/ discharge mechanisms and on  countermeasures preventing the materials from charging by  friction.

Also experiments to touch down the nozzles without loaded  devices onto a test PCB, as proposed in the first version of the  ESD Forum’s guideline 1013 [2], were not useful since the  procedure didn’t reproduce what really happens at the moment  of landing a device onto the PCB. Therefore at first a small  chapter will introduce what happens in the assembling  procedure considering charging and discharging.

## III. CHARGING AND DISCHARGING IN PCB ASSEMBLY  

At first we need to consider, where electrostatic risks come  from in assembly processes. The basic rule behind is, that  charge separation takes place, wherever friction or an intensive  mechanical   contacting/   decontacting   sequence happens  between two isolators  or  between an isolator and a dissipative  or conductive material. Such events happen when unwinding a  tape from its reel or when removing a cover tape from the  carrier tape and when picking components and placing them  onto the PCB (at the very moment when the nozzle loses the  contact to the device). The latter applies even if the nozzle is  made from electrostatic dissipative or conductive material and  the housing of the device in operation is made from isolating  plastics. When the device has been landed onto the PCB, the  nozzle releases this device and departs to pick up the next  device. At the very moment of releasing, the charge separation  happens. Since the device is already in an electric contact to the  PCB (via the solder paste), this charge gain is distributed into  the metal PCB tracks connected to the landed device (fig. 1). The same happens  when the next devices are landed, so that the charge of the PCB  increases as more, as more devices are placed onto it. Only the  continuous air stream of an air ionizer can avoid this continuous  charging of the PCB. The ESD risk now is, that all new devices  brought by the nozzle for placement onto the PCB, are well  grounded: the reels are made from electrostatic dissipative  materials, the picker nozzle is made from dissipative rubber – in  short, everything which needs to be obeyed to keep a good  electric grounding, has been done…but now, a perfectly  discharged new device is landed onto a  charged  PCB! At this  very moment, where the device is landed onto the PCB, it  suffers an inverse CDM discharge (fig. 2). In addition, when  being released from the nozzle, again new charge is generated  to increase the overall charge of the PCB again.

However, in some cases, even worse scenarios can happen. For  instance, if we have a multi-layer-PCB with front side and  backside PCB tracks one of the tracks participating in the  discharge scenario might be grounded by the PCB clamp (fig.  3). In such case, the charge stored may be much higher due to  the plate capacitor formed between the (grounded) backside  track and the (charged) front side track. A discharge of such  unintended PCB-capacitor into a grounded neighbor track via  the device to be landed, will kill it due to the extremely high  current flow and discharge power.

Of course, it is important to make the reels, nozzles etc. from  electrostatic dissipative materials. Otherwise, an inverse  scenario would add-on: all charges collected in the conductive  body of the device to be placed (chip, leadframe) would suffer a  hard discharge at the moment of landing onto the PCB,  especially as long as related PCB tracks are at a big difference  in electrostatic potential.

In worst case, all scenarios described can end up in a four-digit  voltage, becoming dangerous for ESD-sensitive devices at the  moment when they are landed onto the PCB. Therefore air  ionizers in the assembly channel of any PCB assembly machine  should become mandatory.

## IV. NEW MEASUREMENT METHOD FOR ESD RISK  EVALUATION  

Since the placement process in modern machines is running at  extremely high speed and by use of multi-nozzle units, which  can place up to about 40 devices at the same time onto a PCB,  the new measurement method had to be automatized and  integrated into the process flow. This has been achieved by  means of a test board with a small and a big metal area for  placements. These areas are also via-connected to identical  backside areas of this PCB. At that backside, an adjustable  contactless voltage probe of a contactless electrostatic  voltmeter is mounted at a distance of 3mm to the area of which  the voltage shall be measured (fig. 4). The voltage probe is  connected to the electrostatic voltmeter, of which the measured  voltage is in-situ recorded by a measurement laptop (lab view  program).

## VI. DISCUSSION OF MEASUREMENT RESULTS  

The measurements have shown that the voltage of the PCB  increases by the increasing number of devices, which have been  landed to it. Smaller devices gave a smaller voltage increase  than big devices. This becomes comprehensive, since for small  devices also very small picker nozzler are used, so that the  electrostatic friction effect becomes smaller.  It has also be shown that an air ionizer blowing into the  assembly tunnel of the machine, can avoid the continuous  increasing of charging the PCB but cannot avoid small charge  peaks at the moment where the nozzle releases the device.  However, although precise voltage levels have been measured  on the test-PCB, these results are only of qualitative nature: in  the test PCB, we have a defined copper-coated placement area.  This area corresponds to a rather big conductive capacitance  (the conductive capacitance is proportional to the surface of a  conductive area)  compared to the conductive capacitance of the  device to be landed onto the PCB. However, in reality, the  individual metal tracks of a usual PCB provide a much smaller  capacitance than our big test area of the test PCB. This,  however, means, that the voltage to which in reality the PCB  can be charged, can reach much higher values than those  measured on the test PCB. To make this better comprehensive,  let us make the following thought experiment (fig. 13): We  have two isolated metal spheres, both the same size. One sphere

If we touch the two spheres  together, the charge of the 10kV sphere will be distributed to  the surface area of both spheres together, so that after touching,  both spheres are at 5kV, following the equation  $\mathrm{Q=C\cdotU}$  , where  Q is the charge, U is the voltage and C is the conductive  capacitance. Since the charge Q doesn’t change, the voltage  must drop to the half, if the capacitance becomes twice by  touching. Continuing our experiment, we now use a much  bigger 0-V-sphere than the one at   $10\mathrm{kV}$  . In this case, the voltage  after touching will end up at significantly less than 5kV. If we  now replace the small sphere by the device to be landed and the  big sphere by the surface area of the PCB track (or test area on  the test PCB), it becomes comprehensive, that the voltage  becomes as bigger as smaller the PCB track area is. However,  there are further influences, which can both increase or decrease  the final voltage on the PCB: A not neglectable influence is the  humidity, which strongly influences electrostatic effects. At  less than   $40\%$   r.h., electrostatic effects appear very strong, at  more than   $60\%$   r.h. they are significantly suppressed. Also the  shape of the PCB tracks give an influence: if the design of the  line tracks show sharp corners or peaks, a corona discharge may  rapidly decrease the charging, too. And, finally, the wiring  achieved by already landed components, will of course again  change the relevant conductive capacitance, too

## VII. CONSEQUENCES FOR THE PCB ASSEMBLING  

Up to now, no measurement method did show the real  situation of charging of PCBs when being assembled. This  series of measurements has shown that the major concern is a  continuous accumulation of charge onto the PCB metal lines,  resulting in a significant gain of voltage. Since this charge is  accumulated on – compared to the device conductive  capacitance - large surfaces (PCB lines plus component  surfaces already assembled), severe discharge may happen, if  ESD-sensitive devices are landed onto a charged PCB. The  worst case would be if some PCB lines were bound to ground  potential by the metal bars, which fix the PCB within the  machine. In such case, it could happen that at first, some  floating PCB lines are charged at the beginning of the assembly  and then, a device is landed with one pin to a grounded line and  another pin to a high-voltage-charged line as already shown in  fig. 3. This device would suffer a hard CBE (charged board  event) - discharge with direct, undamped voltage entry into one  pin and the exit to a hard ground through another pin.

## VIII. USEFUL COUTERMEASURES  

## “Conventional” approaches  

In order to suppress the voltage increase on the PCB, the use of  air ionizers that continuously suppress the charging of the PCB  under assembly must be mandatory in all cases where ESD  sensitive components are assembled. Also the sequence of  assembling should be considered. If at least one of the PCB  metal lines is connected to ground via the metal fixing bars, the  assembly could start from there, assembling at first all non-ESD  sensitive components as for instance resistors, inductances etc.  Then, a network of passive components already provides  discharge paths all over the PCB circuitry. If thereafter, the  active components are landed, at least a growth of voltage on  the PCB can be suppressed. In this regard, a specific problem  arises, if many small PCBs are integrated in one big “master”  PCB (called panel). In such case, those small PCBs arranged in  the middle of the panel usually are completely isolated and  don’t have any electrical connection to ground.  Then it  becomes impossible to suppress systematic charging without  using an air ionizer. Therefore, in panel design, it should be  considered to design ground connections through the PCB  depanelization bridges, which would provide electrical contact  at least to grounded frames or would ground bus lines of the  small PCBs to the rail holder of the panel during the assembly  process. If done this way, the assembling sequence (passives  first) must be regarded in order to prevent from scenarios as  described before in fig. 3.

An alternative method to air ionizers for avoiding charging  the PCB is the use of soft, electrostatic dissipative brushes,  touching the PCB from the backside. However, this method can  be applied only for double-sided and multi-layer prints with  PCB-internal vias. The advantage of a brush solution is its  nearly maintenance-free approach compared to air ionizers,  which need a periodic cleaning of the corona needles.

Beyond these methods to avoid charging the PCB, also the  charge import can be reduced or suppressed at its source. Here,  the generally known countermeasures as the use of antistatic  reels, tapes, cover tapes and dissipative placement nozzles  should be considered [4].

## New approaches  

Using conventional ESD suppression measures, already a  good success can be achieved. However, looking at modern  ultra-high speed assembly machines with 40 and more picker  heads operating at the same time, the neutralization effect of air  ionizers cannot cope in all cases with such high-speed  assembling. Furthermore, in many modern ICs, chip-internal  ESD protection circuitry is more and more limited due to  integration density, high frequency, signal sensitivity and other  reasons. In such scenarios, new ESD protection methods in  production processes are necessary. When looking at the  measurement results in V., it becomes obvious that most of the  charging applies at the very moment, when the nozzle releases  the device after landing it onto the PCB. In this moment,

friction electricity generates when the (dissipative) nozzle lifts  itself from the (isolating) housing of the component. Friction  electricity is always generated, when either two touching  isolating materials are separated or when an isolating material  and a conductive or dissipative material is separated. Only if  both materials to be separated are either electric conductive or  at least dissipative, no friction electricity will be generated (fig.  8, right half). In consequence, this means, that if the  component’s surface is dissipative, no friction electricity shall  be imported onto the PCB anymore. To achieve this, either all  components   (including   non-ESD   sensitive   passive  components) need to be lacquered at the housing surface by a  dissipative transparent paint, or, alternatively, the nozzle will be  humidized by a dissipative liquid (e.g. water), before it picks-up  the next component – thus providing a temporary dissipative  layer onto the surface of the device to be assembled. In the  subsequent heating for soldering, this layer will remove itself  by vaporization. This “futuristic” sounding proposal might soon  become necessary when future integrated circuits become  extremely ESD-sensitive due to the abovementioned reasons.

## IX. BENEFITS FOR FAILURE ANALYSIS  

Frequently, failure analysis faces to customer complaints  reporting about time-zero-failures on system level while the

chip provider’s final test results on packaged device level show  perfect yields and functionality. In these cases, it becomes  difficult for the analyst to identify root causes which are in  client factories and usually are out of his control. This paper  provides a deep-understanding access to one of the most  frequent failure root causes between packaged device and  system level processing. A technical review by an ESD expert  should easily highlight the problems described in this paper, if  no useful countermeasures are installed there and many useless  8D’s   and   failure   signature   verifications   limited   to  semiconductor device level can be economized.

## X. CONCLUSION  

A new measurement method has been presented for in-situ  evaluation of ESD risks in PCB assembly machines. It has  proven that electrostatic friction between the plastic device  housing and the picker nozzle is generated at the very moment,  when the nozzle lands the device onto the PCB and releases the  device. By this, a continuous charging of related PCB tracks  can be observed, which, however, is influenced from further  factors as PCB track layout, humidity etc. The systematic  charging of PCB has been characterized and different  ESD-scenarios from practice were described as well as useful  countermeasures including new approaches for ESD prevention  of very sensitive devices.

These results of this paper open a deeper understanding of the  electrostatic charge flow during PCB assembly and will allow a  significant reducing of both ESD-root-caused yield- and  reliability risks in the assembling of PCBs with ESD-sensitive  electronic devices.
