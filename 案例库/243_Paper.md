## Study of cross contamination between InP substrate  and Silicon substrate during Phosphorus depth profile  

measurement

Wu Mengxue\*, Zhu Lei, Liu Jiahui, Hua Younan, and Li Xiaomin   Surface & Chemical Analysis Department, Wintech Nano-Technology Services Pte Ltd  10 Science Park Road #03-26  \*   Phone: +65-67777354   Fax: +65-67772462 Email:  mengxue@wintech-nano.com

Abstract-   The dopant detection limit of SIMS instrument is a  critical factor in semiconductor implant process monitoring and  control. However, after various substrates are tested in the  chamber, possible cross-contamination may lead to change of the  detection limit, resulting in the inaccurate measurements and  conclusions. In this paper, the cross-contamination effect is  simulated with Phosphorus contaminant sputtered over the SIMS  chamber. The degree of the cross-contamination is quantified and  correlated with the detection limits. One of the failure cases is  schematically illustrated and studied.

Keywords  –   Cross-contamination, Failure Analysis, Ion  Implantation, Channeling Effect, Detection Limit .

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

Dynamic second ion mass spectrometry (DSIMS) is one of  the most precise tools used to quantify the elemental  composition of a sample [1]. Moreover, it is especially well  known for the excellent detection limits (DL) for trace  elements. Therefore, this technology is widely used in  semiconductor industries for both failures analysis and routine  check-up for production line. Due to the fact that DSIMS  machine is bulky and expensive, one machine may need to  handle several substrates and different dopants in various  circumstances. In some cases, cross contamination happens  when the dopant element in a test is the same as the substrate of  another test. For example, III-V group elements, such as  Phosphorus and Arsenic, served as dopant in silicon substrate,  but they are constituting the film composition of the substrate  materials in LED industry. When the analyses are performed on  different substrates, cross contamination will happen. This  would lead to an increase of the DL of DSIMS up to 2 to 3  orders of magnitude, which greatly impedes the performance of  the tool. Therefore, knowing the amount of contamination  during the analysis is crucial in getting the quantified results for  DSIMS depth profiles.

In this work, we investigated the introduction of Phosphorus  contamination by Indium-Phosphorus (InP) substrate and its  effect on the detection of Phosphorus as dopant in Silicon  substrate. We used the Phosphorus standard sample with a dose  of 1E14 atoms  $/\mathrm{cm}^{2}$   and an Rp of 9 nm from National Institute of  Standards and Technology (NIST) to characterize the change in  DL. By acknowledging the quantified amount of contamination  with respect to sputtering time on InP substrate, we can have a  clear picture of the effect of cross contamination.

## II.   E XPERIMENTAL CONDITION 

The Phosphorus profiles were measured in this work using  DSIMS with the model of CAMECA 7f-Auto. Cesium source  with impact energy of 6 Kev and primary ion current of 65 nA  was employed throughout the measurements. The status of the  test condition was stable. The samples were placed into three  different windows of the back-mount holder and loaded into the  machine. The raster area was set as  $200\;\upmu\mathrm{m}\times200\;\upmu\mathrm{m},$  , analysis  area of   $33\;\upmu\mathrm{m}\times33\;\upmu\mathrm{m}$  , and mass resolution is 4800. The sputter  rate in Silicon substrate was   $42.271~\mathrm{nm/min}$  . Vacuum value of  the Cesium source chamber was 4.8E-8 mbar, and 7.4E-10  mbar in the main test chamber. Liquid nitrogen was added half  an hour before the loading of the samples in order to reduce  environmental hydrogen contamination and optimize the DL of  Phosphorus.

To quantify the contamination induced by InP substrate, the  experiment is conducted in the following procedure: firstly,  depth profile of Phosphorus in both NIST sample and pure  silicon sample are tested. Next, the ion beam is moved onto the  InP sample. By increasing the time duration primary ion landed  on the InP sample, the amount of Phosphorus ions  contaminated onto the blocking plate is controlled. After each  time duration, the Phosphorus DL is tested on both NIST and  pure silicon samples. The time duration is chosen around 1 to 2  mins at the beginning, and increase to 5 to 10 mins to observe  the trend of DL more efficiently.

As shown in Fig. 2, Phosphorus depth profiles measured in  NIST standard sample at different sputter times are overlaid.   The time is controlled from 0 min to 29 mins, with interval of 1  to 10 mins. By increasing the sputtering time on the InP  substrate, more Phosphorus particles are redeposited onto the  blocking plate in front of the secondary ion collection column,  where the charged ions go through and reach the detector. With  more Phosphorus particles accumulated on the plate, the  background noise is higher and leads to inaccurate analysis  result for the sample.

Before introducing the contamination manually into the  chamber, the DL for Phosphorus is measured as   $8.245\mathrm{E}+15$  atoms  $\mathrm{{/cm^{3}}}$   for the NIST sample, and   $5.005\mathrm{E}{+}15$   atoms  $\mathrm{{'cm}^{3}}$   for  the pure Silicon sample. The DL calculated data range is  averaged from 200 nm to   $210\;\mathrm{\nm}$   for initial Phosphorus  analysis and from 225 nm to 250 nm for the others. As shown in  Fig. 2(a), the implantation depth profiles of the Phosphorus are  repeatable at high concentration, where the influence of the  redeposited Phosphorus is low. When the concentration drops  lower than   $1\mathrm{E}{+18} $  atoms/cm 3 , background noise of the  Phosphorus becomes obvious. With increase of sputtering time,  the DL of the depth profiles are increased, which can be clearly  observed in Fig. 2(b).

The increase of DL values in NIST sample and Silicon  sample are shown in Fig. 3. The linear increment trend agrees  well with theoretical understanding [2]. Longer sputtering time  of the InP substrate leads to higher Phosphorus background,  and thus increases the DL of Phosphorus. The data can be found  in Table 1, where the DL values of both NIST and pure Silicon  samples are averaged at the level of   $6.007\mathrm{E}{+}15$  atoms/cm 3 .  With high Phosphorus contamination, a DL with value of   $^{4\mathrm{E}+17}$   atoms/cm 3  is observed in both samples, which  corresponding to an increment of 65 times. When a failure  analysis or routine check is done in this situation, the dose

calculated will be higher than the actual value, which leads to  wrong diagnose of the sample. Two examples are given below  to illustrate the disadvantage of high DL.

The first example is a real case where the FA sample is  implanted with low dose dopant. In Fig. 4, we present the tested  depth profile of a Phosphorus implanted Silicon sample with  dose of 7.38E12 atoms  $\dot{/\mathrm{cm}}^{2}$   and Rp at 1509.32 nm. This dose is  considered common in current silicon wafer industry and IC  chip production line. Implantation dose matching between  machines would require a high accuracy to maintain the  performance of the wafer products. The peak concentration for  this depth profile is at Rp, which read as   $1.433\mathrm{E}{+}17$   atoms/cm 3 .  Thus, when the contamination of Phosphorus on the blocking  plate  reaches around 1E+17 atoms  $\mathrm{{'cm^{3}}}$  , the peak of the profile  would be hard to distinguish. Also the variance in detected ion  intensity of the dopant will be only in one order of magnitude,  leading to an inaccurate calculation of the dose of the sample.

Another example is that DL is highly important in  characterization of channeling effect [3]. The channeling effect  usually   happens   when   the   implanted   substrate   is  monocrystalline, and the stopping power of the ion particle is  low in certain orientation [4]. For example the 0 degree incident  of ion implanted into a   $<\!110\!>$   axis silicon substrate would lead  to the longest tail of channeling effect, which is well known and  described with dual Pearson distribution [5].

Here we illustrate this phenomenon schematically in Fig. 5,  where the blue line represents the real Phosphorus depth profile  in a FA sample, and the brown line is the Phosphorus depth  profile we obtained experimentally at high DL. In the situation  when the implantation angles of the machines are set wrongly,  the distribution of Phosphorus would go into a deeper depth.  This would further lead to the change in junction depth of the  device and impede its performance electrically. At high DL, it  is impossible to distinguish whether the channeling effect  occurred in the FA sample. This is the reason we need to  eliminate the contamination of dopant.

The above mentioned two examples are the typical  measurement issues faced during high DL. In this paper, we  reduced the DL back to machine usual performance by milling  the clean Silicon substrate with high current Oxygen ion source.  The current is around   $1.5\;\upmu\mathrm{A}$  , and large raster of   $500~\upmu\mathrm{m}^{*}\;500$

$\upmu\mathrm{m}$  . We sputtered the clean Silicon for 2 hours. After which, the  Phosphorus is measured again on the NIST sample. It can be  seen clearly in Fig.6, after cleaning Phosphorus on the blocking  plate, the DL is back to  $1\mathrm{E}{+}16$   atoms/cm 3 , which is close to the  machine’s usual performance.

## IV. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we quantified the effect of cross contamination  of Phosphorus between InP and Silicon substrates. The DL of  Phosphorus in the NIST sample is two orders of magnitudes  higher with the presence of Phosphorus contamination.  This  offers a clear idea of how the DL changes with chamber  contamination. Two FA cases in which the detection limits  impede the measurement accuracy are presented.
