## Case study of on-board optical module failure induced  by PDMS  

Yang Yu, and Yuanxin Lee  Failure Analysis Team, H3C Technologies Co., Limited, Hangzhou, 310000, China.   \* Phone: +86-571-86761860   Fax: +86-571-86761860   Email:  yu.yangA@h3c.com

Abstract-   On-board   optical   module,   which   implements  photoelectric transformation, has been widely applied in  communications industry. Any contamination blocking optical  transmission will decrease its power distinctly, therefore, the  cleanliness of interface between the OBT(On-board Transceiver)  and MT ferrule is in need of great attention. In this paper, we will  show a case about on-board optical module failure due to  pollution during the production. Through a systematic failure  analytical method and using material analysis techniques, we  identified the unknown matter and found out the roots of the  problem.

Keywords  –  On-board optical module, FTIR, PDMS, Failure  analysis .

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

On-board optical module, which is the combination of  optical-electrical converter and circuit controller of the fiber  optic communication transceiver, has been widely used in  communications industry. As shown in Figure 1, it consists of  two parts: OBT(Onboard Transceiver) and MT ferrule. The  interface between them ever exposing to the environment has  the possibility of being contaminated. Therefore, contamination  control for on-board optical module is very essential throughout  the product life cycle.

II.

A NA LYSIS

The board was found failing the functional test during the  production. It was claimed that contamination resided on the  surface of MT ferrule. Failure analysis began on the on-board  optical module by verifying that liquid still existed. After  removing the failed MT ferrule from its corresponding OBT,  contamination with specific shape was in sight (Fig.2). The next  step was to identify the chemical composition of the unknown  matter. ATR-FTIR spectrum of the contamination was shown  in Figure 3, revealing it was mainly composed of PDMS. To be  specific,   absorption   peaks   around    $1089.50\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$     and   $1012.81\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$   corresponded to the Si-O group. The sharp peak at   $791.63\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$   and   $1258.29\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$   were attributed to the Si-C group

and   $\mathrm{Si}.\mathrm{CH}_{3}$   group, respectively [1]. During the analysis,  contamination disappeared over time, indicating PDMS with  lower molecular weight. A more interesting phenomenon was  that it reappeared after heating the optical module at   $80~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   for  hours.

The key for this failure analysis was to find the source of the  contamination. Considering the components of the board and  chemical composition of the liquid, three hypotheses for the  source were put forth namely the board, OBT and MT ferrule.  The following describes each hypothesis and the analysis  performed to prove or disprove it.

Si system heat conductive material was intensively applied in  the electronic industry, and of course including this board.  However, there must have a path for the matter to migrate if it  was the source of PDMS. An all-around inspection with optical  microscopy was performed on the board. The surface of board  and exterior face of optical module were spotless, indicating  this hypothesis was wrong.

To further investigate the inner structure of the on-board  optical module, a failed sample was encapsulated in epoxy and  polished manually. Looking at the images from cross section in  Figure 4, the O-ring and MT ferrule were claimed to be Si

system materials according to the EDS mapping result.  Furthermore, the corresponding ATR-FTIR spectra for them  were shown in Figure 5, revealing that the main material for the  O-ring was PDMS while the MT ferrule’s packaging material  was just filled with   $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$   particles.

With the result of the experiments, contamination didn’t  appear in (1) and (2) while liquid was visible on the surface of  MT ferrule in (3) and (4), indicating the pollution had nothing  to do with the O-ring of OBT. To further support this  conclusion, we chose an OBT from another vendor. Attaching  the new MT ferrule to this OBT, there was no foreign matter  existed through heating at   $80\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  for 2 days. However, when  inserting the old MT ferrule and storing it in oven for hours,

contamination reappeared on the surface. Combining all the  results, the source of PDMS was led to the old MT ferrule.

It is noteworthy the difference between the new and old MT  ferrule was that the old sample was ever enclosed by dust cap  and stored for a few months. Hence, we conducted the  following experiments,

(1)The new MT ferrule enclosed by the dust cap was stored in  oven at  $80\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   for 24h.  (2) The MT ferrule after experiment (1) attached to OBT was  stored in oven at   $80\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   for 24h.

After the first step, there was no abnormity on the surface of  MT ferrule. Then the MT ferrule was attached to the OBT and  stored for another 24h, reproducing the contamination. As  evident from the EDS and ATR-FTIR analysis in Figure 6 &  Figure 7, tiny-particle residue containing Si was detected on the  inside wall of the dust cap, following being verified the same  composition as the contamination on the surface of MT ferrule.

Through further inquiries, we confirmed the inside wall of  dust cap without being cleaned thoroughly and required our  vendor to select and use a more effective cleaning agent.

## III. CONCLUSION  

This paper reviewed the systematic steps and challenges  involved in analyzing contamination of on-board optical  module and also ascertained the source of PDMS and root cause  for the failure. The contamination on the surface was migrated  from the inside wall of MT ferrule’s dust cap. Finally, the  measure to solve the problem was to change the MT ferrule’s  cleaning agent which can remove the residues of PDMS  effectively.
