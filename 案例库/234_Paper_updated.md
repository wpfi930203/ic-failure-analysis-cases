## XPS Characterization of Ag-Sn Backside  Metallization  

Yen Min Chew 1 , Tatt Wai Wan 1 , Huat Chye Lim 1 , Kuan Yew Cheong 2   1 Unit Process Development, Infineon Technologies Kulim, Kulim Hi-Tech Park, 09000 Kulim, Kedah, Malaysia    2 Electronic Materials Research Group, School of Materials & Mineral Resources Engineering, Engineering Campus, Universiti Sains  Malaysia, 14300 Nibong Tebal, Penang, Malaysia  Phone : (+60)44279216, Email : YenMin.Chew@infineon.com

Abstract — X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) was  used to characterize Ag-Sn bilayer structure crucial for  backside metallization (BSM) in power device.  1000 nm Sn  layer followed by a 140 nm Ag layer was deposited using sputter  deposition at   $\bf{200^{\circ}C}$  . The XPS depth profile confirmed a 5 nm  oxide layer at the surface and showcased Ag diffusion into Sn.  Core-level binding energies and oxidation states of O 1s, C 1s,  Sn 3d5/2, and Ag 3d species were analyzed through narrow scan  XPS spectra, including depth profiling at the surface (cycle 0), 5  nm depth (cycle 1), and   $\bf{40\ n m}$   depth (cycle 8). At cycle 0, a  combination of pure Ag, pure Ag3Sn, pure Sn, and other species  was observed, with atomic concentrations of   $\mathbf{1.29\%}$  ,   $\mathbf{4.21\%}$  ,   $\pmb{5.56\%}$  , and   $\mathbf{88.94\%}$  , respectively. The atomic concentrations of  pure Ag, pure Ag3Sn, and pure Sn were   $\mathbf{28.69\%}$  ,   $19.64\%$  , and   $\mathbf{51.68\%}$   respectively at cycle 1, and   $\mathbf{38.02\%}$  ,   $\mathbf{13.16\%}$  , and   $\mathbf{48.82\%}$   respectively at cycle 8. The lower concentration of AgO   $\mathbf{(17.86\%)}$  ) compared to SnO   $(\mathbf{31.84\%})$   at cycle 0 and the  consistent lower concentration of Ag than Sn at cycles 0, 1, and  8 suggested that complete Ag diffusion into Sn, concurrent with  Ag3Sn intermetallic compound formation. The study highlights  the role of XPS in understanding Ag-Sn bilayer thin films and  illustrate the contribution of interdiffusion mechanism to  $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}3\mathbf{S}\mathbf{n}$    intermetallic compound formation.

Keywords— BSM, sputter deposition, XPS, Ag-Sn, Ag3Sn

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

Backside metallization (BSM) stands as a critical facet in  the intricate process of power devices fabrication,  contributing significantly to the overall performance by  fostering optimal adhesion, enhancing electrical properties,  and establishing enduring stability as a vital conductive  pathway within circuits [1]. The technique of choice for  depositing thin films onto the backside of wafers is the  physical vapor deposition (PVD) process, specifically  through sputter deposition. This method is favored for its  versatility, allowing precise control over the composition and  thickness of thin films, thus enhancing the properties of  multilayer deposition films [2].

The conventional BSM has been configured with a  soldering   layer   of   Sn,   facilitating   die   bonding  interconnection, and a protective layer of Ag to counteract the  oxidation of Sn, particularly in specific semiconductor device  applications. This unique Ag-Sn bilayer gives rise to the  formation of intermetallic compounds, with   $\mathrm{Ag}_{3}\mathrm{Sn}$   being  particularly notable at the bonding interfaces. The role of  Ag 3 Sn in enhancing mechanical properties and thermal  fatigue resistance of solder joints is well-recognized [3].  However, challenges in reliability have emerged, attributed  to the occurrence of void formation within the soldering  process.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), a widely  acknowledged method for surface analysis, is employed to

scrutinize surface chemistry, bonding structure, and the  composition of the surface and interphase across the depth of  analysis. XPS operates by examining the peak shift in binding  energies of core-level electrons, a process involving the  emission of a single photoelectron per photon during  excitation [4, 5].

Focusing on electrons in core levels allows elemental  analysis, as each element has unique electronic binding  energies. Furthermore, XPS demonstrates high surface  sensitivity, capturing electrons within the escape depth zone   $(0.5-2.5\ \mathrm{nm})$  ) in the XPS spectrum, with depth of analysis  confined to a range of a few monolayers [6]. Generally, XPS  can detect all elements in the periodic table except hydrogen  and helium due to their low cross-section of interaction.  Numerous studies have validated XPS as a valuable  technique for studying the characteristics and behaviors of  various intermetallic compounds, such as Cu 3 Sn [7], NiZr [8,  9], intermetallic Ce compounds [10, 11], ZnPd [12], and  more.

The effectiveness of TEM-EDX combined with X-ray  diffraction (XRD) in unravelling the formation of  intermetallic compounds, specifically Ag 3 Sn and Ag 4 Sn, in  thermally evaporated Ag-Sn bilayer thin films has been  underscored by previous research [13]. However, it's  noteworthy that limited research has utilized XPS to  investigate intermetallic compounds in Ag-Sn thin films,  possibly due to XPS being primarily a surface technique with  limitations in analyzing films thicker than 10 nm. Addressing  this research gap, our study employs XPS sputter depth  profiling to comprehensively explore the composition and  structure of the Ag-Sn backside metal interlayer, facilitating  the elucidation of the diffusion mechanism between Ag and  Sn.

## II.    E XPERIMENTAL 

BSM process was executed by PVD sputtering system  using Evatec Clusterline 200 to deposit a 1000 nm Sn layer  followed by a 140 nm Ag layer on the wafer backside at   $200^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  . Fig. 1 illustrates the schematic diagram of the BSM  stack on the Si substrate with the expected stacking layers,  providing a visual representation for better understanding.

A transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2 F20  X-Twin) was utilized to provide high-resolution imaging of  the Ag-Sn bilayer. Prior to the cross-sectional cut, a 10 to 20  nm AuPd capping layer was deposited for a sharp interface  and layer protection. Fig. 2 displays the TEM image of the  cross-section of the BSM on the Si wafer. The XPS depth  profiling was performed by Mimos Semiconductor Sdn. Bhd.  using monochromatic Al-Kα x-ray excitation at 24.3 W  power,  $100\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$   analysis spot size, and   $112\,\mathrm{eV}$   pass energy for

electron analysis. Fig. 3 presents a total of 33 conducted  cycles, each with a 5 nm thickness.

## III.    R ESULTS AND  D ISCUSSION   

Fig. 4 shows the overall XPS depth profile of the metal  stack. A   $5\,\mathrm{\nm}$   oxide layer is observed on the uppermost

surface at cycle 0, associated with the presence of   $40\%$  of  oxygen and   $14\%$   of carbon. The sharp reduction in oxygen to   $0\%$   from cycle 0 to cycle 1 provides evidence that the oxide  layer is predominantly observed at the uppermost layer.  Progressing from cycle 1 to cycle 31 (corresponding to 5 nm  to   $155~\mathrm{nm})$  ), a consistent observation emerges: the atomic  concentration of Sn surpasses that of Ag, with a widening gap  along the cycles. Sn content rises from   $54\%$  to   $77\%$  , while  Ag content declines from   $46\%$   to   $23\%$   from cycle 2 to cycle  31. This observed pattern strongly implies the diffusion of Ag  to Sn layers within the Ag-Sn bilayer structure.

Analysis conducted by [14] indicates that   $\mathrm{Ag}$   exhibits a  preference for interstitial diffusion in Sn, while Sn tends to  favor vacancy diffusion, as illustrated in Fig. 9. The slower  vacancy diffusion of Sn into Ag, compared to the interstitial  diffusion of  $\mathrm{Ag}$   in Sn, can be attributed to the relatively small  diffusion coefficient of Sn in Ag [13]. This observation aligns  with the substantial capacity of Ag to dissolve Sn, with a  solubility limit of approximately   $10\%$   [15], while Sn  demonstrates negligible solubility for Ag [16]. Moreover, the  observed penetration of Ag into Sn can be attributed to the  lower activation energy of Ag lattice diffusion in bulk Sn  single crystals (around   $51.5{\cdot}7\bar{7}.0~\mathrm{kJ/mol})$  , in contrast to the  diffusion of Sn in bulk Ag (165-171 kJ/mol) [17].

The growth of Ag 3 Sn intermetallic compounds relies on  the diffusion of Ag into Sn, with an activation energy of 70.3

kJ/mol, comparable to Ag diffusion into Sn [17].  Additionally, the interdiffusion coefficient for Ag 3 Sn  intermetallic compounds is significant, approximately   $\mathbf{\bar{1}}0^{\cdot13}$     $\mathrm{cm}^{2}/\mathrm{s}$   at room temperature [18]. This value is similar to the  diffusion coefficient for interstitial Ag diffusion in Sn and  surpasses the diffusion coefficient for substitutional Sn  diffusion in Ag [13]. This suggests that the formation of  Ag 3 Sn intermetallic compounds can occur at room  temperature through interdiffusion processes, consistent with  prior research [19-21]. On top of that, research finds a  correlation between the intensity of   $\mathrm{Ag}_{3}\mathrm{Sn}$   and the initial  concentrations of Ag and Sn [22]. The formation of Ag 3 Sn  reaches completion upon depletion of either Ag or Sn, with  higher Sn concentrations accelerating the process, as  evidenced in our study (Sn 1000 nm and   $\mathrm{Ag\,140\,nm}$  , resulting  in a high Sn concentration of   $87.7\%$  ). ## IV.    C ONCLUSION 

In conclusion, this study utilized XPS to investigate the  composition and characteristics of Ag-Sn bilayer thin films.  The overall XPS depth profile confirmed the presence of a 5  nm oxide layer at the uppermost surface. In addition, a  consistent observation of a widening gap of Ag and Sn in  atomic concentration along the cycles was noted, indicating  Ag diffusion into Sn. The narrow scan XPS spectra detailed  the distribution of O 1s, C 1s, Sn 3d 5/2 , and Ag 3d, offering a  profound insight into the chemical states and interactions of  these species. Combining these results leads to the conclusion  that the formation of the Ag 3 Sn intermetallic compound  occurred concurrently with the diffusion of Ag into Sn. This  diffusion phenomenon was attributed to the substantial  solubility of Sn in   $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}$   at room temperature, supported by the  lower activation energy of   $\mathrm{Ag}$   lattice diffusion in bulk Sn  single crystals. The observed interdiffusion processes, driven  by   significant   intermetallic   compound   interdiffusion  coefficients, strongly indicate that Ag 3 Sn can indeed form  even at room temperature.
