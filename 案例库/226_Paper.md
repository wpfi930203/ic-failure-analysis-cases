## Signal Processing Method for  Scanning-Acoustic-Tomography Defect Detection  based on Correlation between Ultrasound Waveforms  

Masayuki Kobayashi 1\* , Kaoru Sakai 1 , Kenta Sumikawa 2 , and Osamu Kikuchi 2   1 Research & Development Group, Hitachi, Ltd., 292, Yoshida-cho, Totsuka-ku, Yokohama-shi, Kanagawa-ken, 244-0817, Japan.  2 Ultrasound Inspection System Department, Hitachi Power Solutions Co., Ltd. Horiguchi 832-2, Hitachinaka-shi, Ibaraki-ken,  312-8507, Japan.   \* Phone: +81-45-860-3093   Fax: +81-45-443-9447   Email: masayuki.kobayashi.dg@hitachi.com

Abstract-   It is common to apply scanning acoustic tomography  (SAT) for microelectronic packages inspection because it enables  nondestructive imaging of defects. SAT phase inversion imaging is  useful tool for detecting defect areas accurately and quickly. Phase  inversion imaging is based on the phenomenon of the phase  inversion of an ultrasound wave at interfaces in a sample. We  propose a signal processing method for phase inversion imaging.  Specifically, it is a robust method of finding the phase inversion.  With this method, the phase inversion is found by estimating the  correlation between a reflected wave signal of a sample and  reference wave signal. A negative correlation between these  signals indicates the existence of defects in the sample. Defect  detection tests with the proposed method were carried out using  integrated circuit packages. The tests results indicate that the  proposed method improves robustness for detecting defects  compared with a conventional   method. The tests   also  demonstrated that small defects on the order of 100 micrometers  could be detected using an ultrasound probe with a nominal  frequency of 75 MHz.

Keywords  –  Scanning acoustic tomography, Defect detection,  Delamination, phase inversion .

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

Scanning acoustic tomography (SAT) has been widely used  in inspection of microelectronic packages because it enables  nondestructive imaging of defects, such as delamination, cracks,  and voids, to ensure the reliability of such products. Its  applications have diversified in response to the evolution of  microelectronic   packaging   technology   [1]–[5].   Recent  commercially available SAT systems enable automatic phase  analysis of reflected wave signals, which assists in recognizing  delaminated areas. Images of delaminated areas detected  through phase analysis are generally combined with a C-scan  image [2] for ease of visual identification. This combined  image is often called a phase inversion image. Figure 1 shows  an example of a C-scan image and phase inversion image of an  integrated circuit (IC) package. In the C-scan image, the  brightness of delaminated areas is comparable to that of good  adhesion areas. Therefore, it is difficult to distinguish these  areas depending solely on the C-scan image. In the phase  inversion image, colored areas signify where delamination is  detected through phase analysis. Consequently, SAT system  operators can detect delaminated areas accurately and quickly.  Phase inversion imaging is based on the phenomenon of the

phase inversion of an ultrasound wave at interfaces in a sample.  In this paper, we propose a signal processing method for phase  inversion imaging. Specifically, it is a robust method of finding  the phase inversion.

## II.   P RINCIPLE OF DETECTING DEFECTS 

Figure 2 represents a schematic diagram of the principle of  phase inversion imaging. A reflection mode that analyzes  reflected wave signals from a sample is applied for phase  inversion imaging. In this mode, as ultrasound waves generated  from an ultrasound probe travels through a sample, reflections  occur at interfaces due to differences in acoustic impedance.  Reflected waves are received and converted to electronic  signals by the ultrasound probe. The amplitude of a reflected  wave  $R$   is determined on the basis of the following equation.

$$R=\frac{Z_{2}-Z_{1}}{Z_{2}+Z_{1}}I$$
Where,   $I\,=$  amplitude of an incident wave,   $Z_{1}\;=\;$  acoustic  impedance of the first material, and   $Z_{2}=$  acoustic impedance of  the second material. If   $Z_{1}$   is smaller than   $Z_{2}$  , as shown in Figure  2,  R  becomes positive. A positive   $R$   implies that the reflected  wave will be in phase with the incident wave. In  microelectronic packaging inspection, for example, the phase

inversion will not occur at interfaces between molding resin and  a chip because the acoustic impedance of resin is generally  smaller than that of a chip. However, if delamination exists  between molding resin and a chip,  R  becomes negative because  the acoustic impedance of vacancies (gas phase) forming the  delamination is extremely small compared with that of resin. In  this case, the phase of the reflected wave will be inversed   $180^{\circ}$    with respect to the incident wave. A phase inversion image is  formed by scanning the ultrasound probe    two-dimensionally  and finding the phase inversion at each measurement point of a  reflected wave signal.

Several methods for finding the phase inversion have been  proposed and implemented in commercially available SAT  systems. In most cases, these methods rely on information of  the positive/negative sign of reflected wave signals. Figure 3  represents the principle of a conventional method, described in  reference [6].  An incident wave signal contains several peaks,  as represented in Figure 3 (a), depending on the characteristics  of the ultrasound probe. In this signal, the highest peak,  indicated with an arrow in Figure 3 (a), has a positive polarity.  This conventional method finds the highest peak from the  reflected wave signal and examines its polarity. If the polarities  of the peaks for the reflected and the incident wave signals are  the same, as in Figure 3 (b), the phase inversion is determined  as being absent. Conversely, if each polarity is opposite as in  Figure 3 (c), the phase inversion is determined as being present.

According to the principle of conventional methods such as  that mentioned above [6], it was necessary for phase inversion  imaging to use ultrasound probes generating asymmetric  waveforms in the vertical direction. Figure 4 represents  examples of asymmetric and symmetric waveforms. For the  asymmetric waveform, the highest peak is uniquely determined.  For the symmetric waveform, however, unexpected noise can  easily change the results of identifying the highest peak,  resulting in missing defects.

It is also important for C-scan imaging to select an ultrasound  probe that is compatible with the structure of a sample because  SAT performance characteristics, such as lateral and axial  resolution, depend on the characteristics of an ultrasound probe.  To accommodate various sample structures, many types of  ultrasound probes have been commercialized. In addition,

defects to be inspected are currently becoming more  complicated and have been miniaturized in response to  diversifying structures of microelectronic packages. Therefore,  opportunities to apply various types of ultrasound probes with  different characteristics to obtain fine C-scan images are  increasing. However, ultrasound probes that are suitable for  obtaining fine C-scan images do not necessarily generate  asymmetric waveforms. As explained above, using ultrasound  probes which generate symmetric waveforms likely results in  missing defects using conventional methods. To solve these  problems, we developed a robust method for finding the phase  inversion.

## III.   D EFECT DETECTION BASED ON CORRELATION BETWEEN  WAVEFORMS   

The proposed method uses a reference wave signal that is in  phase with a signal of an incident wave. The correlation  between a reflected wave signal from a sample and the  reference wave signal is estimated to determine the presence of  the phase inversion. Figure 5 represents the principle of finding  the phase inversion using a reference wave signal. The dotted  and solid lines represent a reference wave signal and reflected  wave signal from a delaminated area, respectively. (Step 1) The  highest peaks in the reflected wave signal on the positive and  the negative sides are detected (  $\mathrm{{P_{pos}}}$   and  $\mathrm{{P_{neg}}}$  ). (Step 2) Then the  reference wave signal containing the highest peak  $\mathrm{{P_{r}}}$   is scanned  in the time direction. (Step 3) The reference wave signal is  aligned so that  $\mathrm{{P_{r}}}$   and  $\mathrm{{P_{pos}}}$   overlap, and a correlation coefficient   $C_{\mathrm{pos}}\,(>0)$   is calculated in the time range of   $\mathrm{T_{pos}}$  . The waveforms  of the reference and the reflected waves are not similar in the  time range of   $\mathrm{T_{pos}}$   so that   $C_{\mathrm{pos}}$   is calculated as a small value.  (Step 3) The reference wave signal is also aligned so that   $\mathrm{{P_{r}}}$   and   $\mathrm{{P_{neg}}}$   overlap, and a correlation coefficient  C neg  (< 0) is  calculated in the time range of   $\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{neg}}$  . In this case, the waveform  of the reflected wave appears to be almost inverted with respect  to that of the reference wave in the time range of  $\mathrm{T_{neg}}$   so that the  absolute value of   $C_{\mathrm{{neg}}}$   will become larger than   $C_{\mathrm{pos}}$  . Large   $|C_{\mathrm{neg}}|$    indicates that the phase of the reflected wave is inversed   $180^{\circ}$    with respect to the reference wave.

Large   $|C_{\mathrm{neg}}|$   also indicates the existence of the phase inversion  because the reference wave is in phase with the incident wave,  as mentioned above. If   $|C_{\mathrm{neg}}|$   is larger than   $C_{\mathrm{pos}}$  , threshold  processing will be carried out on   $C_{\mathrm{{neg}}}$    and a pixel value to  minimize over detection. The pixel value is the same as the  brightness of a C-scan image.

With the above-mentioned conventional method, the phase  inversion is found on the basis of local information such as the  polarity of a specific peak. As a result, unexpected noise tends  to affect the results of finding the phase inversion. With the  proposed method,   $C_{\mathrm{{neg}}}$   and   $C_{\mathrm{pos}}$   do not change significantly due  to unexpected noise because these values are calculated from all  the information of a reflected signal. As a result, robustness for  detecting defects will improve compared with that of this  conventional method.

## IV.   E XPERIMENTS 

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, defect  detection tests for IC packages were conducted using several  types of ultrasound probes with different characteristics. Table I  shows the characteristics of these ultrasound probes (supplied  by Hitachi Power Solutions Co., Ltd). All data were acquired  with Hitachi Power Solutions Co., Ltd.’s FineSAT III.

A flat quartz plate was used for obtaining a reference wave  signal. A reflected wave signal from the surface of the quartz  plate was applied as the reference wave signal. This reflected  wave is in phase with an incident wave because the acoustic  impedance of water is larger than that of quartz. Reference  wave signals were acquired using all the ultrasound probes.  Hence, each ultrasound probe has a unique reference wave  signal. Commercially available IC packages were used as test  samples. Artificial defects were introduced in these IC  packages by heat treatment.

Figure 6 shows a C-scan image, correlation coefficient  image, and phase inversion image of sample-A using probe P1. ## VI.   C ONCLUSION 

Recent SAT systems enable phase inversion imaging and is  useful for inspection of microelectronic packages. Phase  inversion imaging enables accurate and quick detection of

delaminated areas by phase analysis of reflected wave signals.  We proposed a signal processing method for phase inversion  imaging. Specifically, it is a robust method of finding phase  inversion by estimating the correlation between a reflected  wave signal from a sample and the reference wave signal. A  negative correlation between these signals indicates the  existence of defects. With the proposed method, various types  of ultrasound probes can be used because the reference wave  signals are unique to the type of ultrasound probe.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method,  defect-detection tests for IC packages were conducted using  several types of ultrasound probes with different characteristics. The tests results indicate that the proposed method improves  robustness for detecting defects compared with a conventional  method. The tests also demonstrated that small defects on the  order of 100 micrometers could be detected using an ultrasound  probe with a relatively high nominal frequency of   $75\ \mathrm{MHz}$  .  Evaluations will be conducted on whether more complicated  and minute defects can be detected with various ultrasound  probes.
