## Combined Application of AFM-XRD-SIMS to Characterize Crystal Properties in  Electroless Nickel Metal Induced by Trace Contaminants   

Wan Tatt Wai\* a , Lee Wei Cheat b , Lim Saw Sing b  and Zakaria Nurhanani b

a  Unit Process, Infineon Technologies Kulim, 09000 Malaysia.  b  Failure Analysis, Infineon Technologies Kulim, 09000 Malaysia  Phone: +604 4278079 Fax: +604 4278442 \*Email: tattwai.wan@infineon.com

## Abstract 

The crystal properties of electroless nickel (EN) film plated on copper (Cu) bond pad (on wafer) can be differentiated by AFM  (Atomic Force Microscopy) for its surface roughness and XRD (X-ray Diffraction) for its crystal orientation.  The EN film  deposited during the clean bath (CB) shows higher roughness under AFM and more oriented crystals under XRD, when comparing  to the one from contaminated bath (COB).  COB was CB pre-treated with small amount of  $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$   and OH - . Applying Secondary Ions  Mass Spectroscopy (SIMS), Cu and hydrogen (H) contaminants were found to be higher in COB processed EN film compared to  the one from CB. The H contaminant in COB EN film is due to its high plating rate (with more OH - ) resulting in more hydrogen gas  by-product accumulation from the EN catalytic reaction. The presence of these 2 contaminants have potentially caused the change  of crystal properties of the EN layer as detected by AFM and XRD.

## Introduction 

Nickel (Ni) is applied in semiconductor as corrosion  protection encasement for copper (Cu) bond pad. Nickel is  deposited onto Cu bond pad through EN plating, a non-current  dependent auto-catalytic plating process. Fig. 1 shows the  chemical reaction of EN deposition. The nickel ion accept  electron from hypophosphite ion so that the Ni metal is  deposited. The reaction produces hydrogen gas, hydrogen ion  and orthophosphite. The deposited Ni contains some  percentage of phosphorous (P). The properties of the EN is  dependent of the ratio of Ni/P. Fig. 2 shows the typical metal  stacks for Cu bond pad encased by EN layer (on top of the EN  layer is palladium and gold) as wire-bonding interface.

Literatures [1-3] have reported that different chemical  composition of the EN bath can contributed to the different  Ni/P ratio, such as higher EN bath’s pH would lead to higher  Ni/P ratio, the condition where the EN layer is more crystalline.  When Ni/P ratio is low, the film tends to be amorphous. The  properties of EN were characterized using mechanical methods  to measure its hardness, tensile strength, ductility, modulus of

elasticity and so on. Nano-indenter is a common method for  these purposes but the results might not be accurate if the  surface roughness of the film is too high. Physical properties of  the films such as density, porosity, adhesion, temperature  resistance, thermal conductivity, solderability and weldability  are also critical. Both the physical and mechanical properties of  EN films are always correlated with the atomic structure and  microstructure of the films. SEM, FIB, TEM and XRD are  some of the common methods for the determination of these  microstructures [1]. While there were reports on the correlation  of EN film and its alloys on its microstructure and physical or  mechanical properties, there were not enough data to correlate  the crystal properties of the EN film to the trace chemical  contaminants of its plating bath.

In this paper, we demonstrated the use of combined material  analysis techniques (AFM, XRD and SIMS) to characterize the  crystal properties of the EN film and correlate them to the trace    contaminants which increase over the time during wafer  processing in EN plating bath. The EN deposit has structural  features at atomic level which can be seen from diffraction of  X-ray using XRD. The key parameters are type of primary cell,  lattice constant, crystallite size, texture and average lattice  distortion. All studies of electrolessly deposited NiP have  confirmed that these consist of extremely fine crystallites or  else they are amorphous in terms of X-ray determined  structures. During the X rotation on the angles of incident  X-ray beam to the detector, the 2θ scan could be done to  calculate the epitaxial crystallite size and the crystal phase of  the films (Fig. 3). The  $\upvarphi$   of an in-plane sample rotation could at  the same time determine the symmetry of the crystal plane.

The roughness of the films as determined by AFM can  provide some information on the crystal’s size and the crystals  distribution pattern on the films. There were very few reports  on application of AFM on EN films. AFM could be a  complementary technique to EBSD (electron back scatter  diffraction) for the characterization of surface microstructure  on the EN films. This is due to EBSD is not able to analyze  microstructure with high surface roughness, which is a typical  phenomenon for most of the EN films.

On the other hand, hydrogen embrittlement is a common  defect for all kinds of metal films. This is especially true for EN  films because the reaction itself (Fig. 1) produces  $\mathrm{H}_{2}$   gas and  $\mathrm{H^{+}}$  ion as by-product. Accumulation of   $\mathrm{H}_{2}$   gas on NiP surface  during the film growth could possibly induce H into the film  structure and as a result the film becomes brittle. The most  challenging part is that H cannot be detected by common X-ray  or energy dispersive method. Foreign metal ions such as Cu  which was dissolved into the plating bath and incorporated into  the films are also a major concern for the film properties which  were not widely discussed. These trace contaminants can be  effectively differentiated by SIMS depth profiling. This work  shows that by combining the application of different  characterization methods, the change of crystal properties of  metal films due to trace contaminants can be differentiated.

## Experimental  

A new EN plating bath was prepared and was labelled as CB.  2 test wafers were processed at this period and were labelled as  W1 & W2. COB was prepared by dipping Cu pellets in solution  until the  $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$   concentration reach 1ppm. At the same time, pH  was adjusted to 1 magnitude higher than CB by adding more  NaOH solution. Another 2 test wafers were then processed at  this bath and were labelled as sample W3 & W4. Field emission  scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Hitachi S4800) was  used to analyze the surface morphologies of the EN film on the  wafers at an accelerating voltage of   $5\;\mathrm{kV}$  . Then, AFM (Bruker  Dimension ICON) was carried out to measure the surface  roughness of the EN films. The roughness measurements were  done using tapping mode at a scan rate of   $0.2\,\textrm{Hz}$   with a  scanning area of   $5~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}^{2}$  . As for XRD analysis (Jordan Valley  JV7300L), θ to 2θ measurement was performed to analyze the  phases contained in the sample in standard and grazing incident  configuration. Scans were measured from   $20–125^{\circ}$   with step  size of  $0.1^{\circ}$  , integration time per step 0.1s. Additionally scans at  different  $\upvarphi$   angles   $(30^{\circ},\!45^{\circ},\!60^{\circ},\!90^{\circ})$   were also carried out .  Ni  peaks of Ni(111) and Ni(200) diffraction peaks were analyzed  and the  $\upvarphi$   dependency for these Ni peaks were plotted. SIMS

depth profiling analysis was performed using CAMECA ims-6f.  The depth is calibrated by measuring the SIMS crater using  Bruker Dektak XT.

## Results & Discussions 

Fig. 4 illustrates the SEM-AFM image of CB and COB  samples. CB sample (Fig. 3 left) shows bigger crystal grain  structure from SEM image. Its roughness as defined by root  mean square (RMS) is 35.8 nm based on AFM analysis. COB  sample (Fig. 3 right) shows homogenously smaller crystal grain  structure while its roughness is   $\mathrm{RMS}\,=\,21.8~\mathrm{nm}$  . This also  indicates that the COB sample has relatively smoother surface  structure. The application of AFM in this case made the  observation of surface morphology change under SEM  quantifiable. It served as a good complimentary method for  surface microstructure analysis for routine SEM scanning.

The effect of crystalline structure of metal film on its  mechanical properties were widely reported. It was well known  that a crystalline EN film has higher corrosion resistivity and  wear resistance compared to amorphous EN film, where the  Ni/P ratio is relatively lower [1]. The difference in the  mechanical strength of both remain the same even though  similar alloys were being incorporated into the both films.

As significant crystal properties differences were observed  for both CB and COB samples without the change of Ni/P ratio  for both, effort was extended by using SIMS to investigate the  trace contaminants that presence in the structure of both  samples especially H.  It is well understood that any impurities  in a crystalline film could lead to crystal defects where the  grain’s structure and size might be changed. One of the  well-known mechanism is hydrogen embrittlement [5,6].  Hydrogen embrittlement is a metal loss of ductility and  reduction of load bearing capability due to adsorption or  diffusion of hydrogen atoms and molecules by the metal. The  results of hydrogen embrittlement is that component crack and  fracture at stresses less than the yield strength of the metal. This  phenomenon is common in hydride forming metal such as  titanium, zirconium, tantalum, niobium and so on, and EN film  is not exceptional due to the release of hydrogen (Fig. 1) as  by-product during the chemical reaction. Fig. 7 illustrates the      mechanisms of the hydrogen embrittlement and Fig. 8 shows an  example of intra-granular cracks of crystalline film affected by  hydrogen embrittlement.

Based on the results of SIMS (Fig. 9), Cu and H were the 2  main trace contaminants detected in CB and COB samples  other than Ni and P. The presence of Cu in the CB film could be  attributed to Cu ion that dissolved into the EN bath from Cu  pad. On the other hand, the presence of H could be due to the  hydrogen gas by-product during the EN catalytic reaction as  explained earlier, and the higher H in COB EN film might be

attributed to the increase of plating rate at COB phase due to its  higher pH. This would lead to faster accumulation of hydrogen  gas on the wafer surface during plating. P content for both  samples were equivalent. As both Cu and H were observed  higher in COB compared to CB samples, it is not confirmed  which contaminants actually contributed to the crystal  microstructure change of the COB samples. It was also not  evaluated in this paper the correlation of crystal microstructure  in CB and COB samples to their mechanical properties.

## Conclusion 

Using SEM-AFM analysis, the CB EN film was found to  contain bigger granular structure size and higher roughness as  compared to COB EN film. RMS for SOBL and EOBL  samples are 35.8 nm and 21.8 nm respectively. Based on XRD  θ to 2θ measurement, Ni (111) and Ni (200) oriented crystal  phases were detected at   $44^{\circ}$   and   $51.7^{\circ}$   respectively. XRD   $\upvarphi$  angles measurements indicated that CB samples showed a  maximum   $\upvarphi=30^{\circ}$   for Ni (111) and Ni (200). COB samples  showed no   $\upvarphi$   dependency, a signature that it has less or  minimum Ni(111) and Ni(200) compared to the earlier. SIMS  depth profiling proved that COB sample contain higher level of  Cu and H trace contaminants when compared to CB sample.  The Cu in CB samples were originated from the Cu bond pad  that dissolved in the EN bath during plating. Higher H in COB  samples could be attributed to more accumulated hydrogen  when reaction rate is increased in COB due to higher pH. Both  of these trace contaminants can be correlated to the change of  crystal properties of EN films from both CB and COB, but it is  not sure that which one plays a dominant role. The correlation  of the crystal microstructure of both samples to their  mechanical properties will be a subject for future studies.
