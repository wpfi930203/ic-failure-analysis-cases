## ESD and Latch-up Design Verification Challenges  in Packaged Parts and Modules  

Michael Khazhinsky 1 , Mohammed Harb 1 , Kuo-Hsuan Meng 1  1 Silicon Laboratories, Inc., 400 West Cesar Chavez Ave, Austin, Texas 78701, USA

Phone :  $+1$   (512) 565-9547, Email :michael.khazhinsky@silabs.com

Abstract — ESD and latch-up stress testing to meet industry  standards is performed on packaged parts and modules.  However, ESD and latch-up design verification has been  historically done on die / silicon database. In case of inadequate  protection, ESD and latch-up damage occurs on silicon.  However, package and module configurations play a significant  role. Packages and modules based on the same silicon die can  have very different ESD and latch-up protection levels. In this  paper we review package and module properties impacting ESD  and latch-up protection. These properties include package and  module size, capacitance, package wire connectivity and  inductance. We discuss ESD and latch-up EDA challenges in  packaged parts and modules. We propose a new verification  flow to access ESD and latch-up on package and module levels  as well as outline the implementation steps.

Keywords — ESD, latch-up, EDA, package, system-inpackage, module, verification flow.

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

There is an increasing interest in addressing Electrostatic  Discharge (ESD) and latch-up risks in heterogenous  integration and advanced packaging products [1]. ESD and  latch-up design verification of 2.5D/3D packaging and  module-level is one of the challenges. Conventional  Electronic Design Automation (EDA) tools are IC-focused,  and each die-technology-tool needs a complex setup. There  is no common format or syntax to establish seamless  communication and setup of the various ESD and latch-up  EDA verification tools using common netlists and pin/pad  definitions at silicon die, package and module levels.

In this paper we will first review some of the package and  module factors impacting product ESD and latch-up  performance. We will review a typical ESD and latch-up  EDA verification flow for System-on-Chip (SoC) and silicon  die level following [2,3]. We will propose a new flow which  extends ESD and latch-up design verification to package and  module levels.

## II.   P ACKAGE  C ONFIGURATION  I MPACT ON  P RODUCT  ESD   AND  L ATCH - UP  P ERFORMANCE   

ESD and latch-up phenomena in CMOS technologies  have been well understood and described, for example, in  [4,5]. ESD and latch-up testing is performed on packaged  parts following industry standards [6-8]. While typically  physical damage occurs on silicon die, package configuration  can play a key role in failure mechanism, severity, and  location of the damage. As a result, packaged parts using the  same silicon die can have very different ESD and latch-up  protection levels. Some of the package factors that impact  ESD and latch-up performance are package size and die-to

package connection properties (inductance, connectivity,  etc.).

Package and module size determine system capacitance,  and, therefore, the amount of induced ESD CDM charge and  peak stress current (I P ). As a result, same die in different  packages will have different levels of ESD discharge current  I P  and will require different ESD protection to meet the same  CDM target levels. For example, a Silicon Labs Series 2 die  can be manufactured in a smaller 4 mm by 4 mm QFN32  package or a larger 13 mm by 15 mm MGM210 module (Fig  1). During 500V CDM event, the smaller QFN32 package  results in peak CDM currents up to 2.4A while the MGM210  module will see currents up to 10.6A (Fig 2). If ESD  protection is designed with 2.4A peak current as a  requirement, the smaller part will pass 500V CDM test while  the larger part will inevitably fail the same test level. Peak  CDM current will also vary widely across package pins as  seen in Fig 3. Therefore, it’s important to have accurate  package-level information when designing ESD protection  network.

Package-level connectivity information is necessary for  analyzing ESD and latch-up. Die and package pins may not  be directly connected in the package. Sometimes, even the  purpose of die pins (power, ground, I/O) may change from  package to package. As an example, consider a die with  multiple voltage domains. The die goes into two different  packages: one package fully utilizes die functionality and  uses all supply domains, while the second one disables some  of the die features by powering one of the domains down (Fig  4). Physically, on die, power domain 1 (VDD1/VSS) is next  to power domain 2 (VDD2/VSS). Power domain 2 is active  in Package A, while power domain 2 is disabled in Package  B by shorting supply VDD2 to ground (VSS) on package  (Fig. 4). This creates grounded n-wells with a nearby active   $\mathsf{p}^{+}$   diffusions in an unrelated powered n-well (Fig. 5). As a  result, vertical PNP and horizontal NPN parasitic bipolars  may turn on leading to latch-up. Package A is immune from  latch-up risk since VDD2 is fully powered and horizontal  NPN bipolar is unlikely to turn on. Since latch-up design  verification is done mostly on die, the difference between  packages A and B will not be comprehended and the  increased risk of latch-up in Package 2 will not be flagged  without package-related connectivity information.

Die-to-package connectivity impact is not limited to  latch-up. Wirebond packages (Fig. 1) commonly have  multiple on-die ground pads down-bonded to common  package ground paddle. This, along with the inductance of  bondwires connecting die pins to package pins, affects on-die  ESD stress. The inductances of die-to-package connections  also play an important role in defining ESD robustness. In  domain-crossing circuits (Fig. 6), the most vulnerable device  under ESD CDM stress condition is typically receiver NMOS   $\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{N}}$   where damaging voltage stress is across the gate   $(\mathrm{V_{gs})}$  .  Significant contribution to voltage difference between  ground nets leading to large   $\mathrm{V_{gs}}$   can come from induced  bondwire inductances   $(L)$  :

III.   ESD  AND  L ATCH - UP  D ESIGN  V ERIFICATION  F LOW

## A.    Conventional Silicon Die-Based ESD and Latch-up  Verification Flow  

IC design for robust ESD and latch-up protection heavily  relies on Electronic Design Automation optimization and  verification tools [2,3,10,11]. Fig 7. shows an example of  conventional ESD and latch-up verification flow. In this flow  commercially available Calibre® PERC [12] engine is used  to enforce ESD and latch-up rule compliance. Typical  verification includes schematic or netlist based topological  checks and layout-based checks, such as back-end point-topoint resistance and current density checks as well as latchup geometrical checks. The rules for these checks are stored  in catalog and dynamically called during verification.  Schematic and layout information is sourced from a design  database. Technology and project information required for  verification run is stored in special files and databases. Data  flow is controlled by script wrapper feeding required  information to Calibre  $^\mathrm{\textregistered}$   PERC engine and generating  Standard Verification Database (SVDB), Design for  Manufacturing Database (DFMDB), results files and required  reports for design verification sign off.

One of the limitations of this ESD and latch-up design  verification flow is that it uses data up to die level only.  Package-level information, while very important, as  described in section II, is not included. We propose an  improved verification flow which includes package-level  information and addresses the above limitation.

## B.    Topological ESD and Latch-up Verification Flow in  Packaged Parts and Modules  

Conventional topological checks are usually executed on  schematic views at the die level. These checks cannot  seamlessly run at package/module levels for several reasons.  The biggest challenge is that there is no standard for  package/module schematic representation.

Package/module designs have more mechanical aspects  than a circuit design, hence they use a different set of  mechanics-focused CAD tools, sometimes not created to  interface with EDA tools used for circuit designs. Unlike a  SPICE netlist of a circuit which typically contains a hierarchy  of subcircuits listing the connections of all its components  and their electrical characteristics, a netlist in a package  design can be just a spreadsheet relating die pads and their  corresponding connections to the package pins or other  System in Package (SiP) components.

Although it is possible to create a schematic view of the  package/module that includes the die to facilitate running  typical topological checks, such creation is often a manual  process that involves manual wiring of die pads to packaging  pins following the spreadsheet netlist. This process is prone  to human error and requires some kind of schematic-vsspreadsheet comparison to validate the correctness of the  schematics.

Components in SiP/module would need special libraries  for proper netlisting. In many cases, such components are  supplied by 3rd party vendors without enough internal circuit  details that describe its ESD characteristics. This forces the  modeling of such components as black boxes, which  obstructs the ESD verification of the whole package/module.

ESD and latch-up checks depend heavily on the  classification of the pins as power, ground, or I/O. At dielevel, this is done by associating die pad names to each of  these classes. At package-level, this association is lost as the  names of the package pins typically inherit product marketing  names which may be different from the die pad names. The  same problem extends to module level where module port  names are different from package pin names. There is no  EDA standard yet for pin classification at the die-level, let  alone package and module levels.

These challenges are addressed in the proposed  improved verification flow for running package-level ESD  checks (Fig.8). It starts with summarizing package-level  information in the spreadsheet and generating netlist from it.  This is followed by the creation of package schematic view  following the spreadsheet netlist. This step requires to create  a symbol of the die out of the die’s Verilog, a symbol of the  package interface, and symbols for other components from  3rd parties. Wires are manually created following the  spreadsheet netlist. Then a full package netlist is created for  the schematic built in the previous step. This required us to  adjust the proper stop view for netlisting of each component  and to stitch the die’s SPICE netlist when a die does not have  a schematic view. A package-level pin definition file is then  created for the package netlist. This requires ensuring correct  pin naming: package pin names and not die pad names must  be used. Also, each package pin needs to be assigned to the  correct pin group and voltage. While there is some  redundancy to a similar assignment at die level, there are  some cases (e.g., Fig. 4) where assignments on package level  do not match the ones on die level. Finally, ESD topological  checks can be run on the package netlist using package-level  pin definition file.

## C.    Physical ESD and  Latch-up Verification Flow in  Packaged Parts and Modules  

Similarly   to   topological   checks,   conventional  verification flows for latch-up, ESD current density and  point-to-point resistance checks are executed on physical  layout views at the die level. Such flows cannot seamlessly  run at package/module levels.

One of the main challenges for extending these checks to  package level is that there is no standardized universal  physical view of the package. Package physical layout is   designed with EDA tools which are different from the ones  used for die layouts. Package designs are not expected to be  in typical GDS or Oasis formats used for die-level checks.  There is no standard conversion between EDA formats.

Dealing with geometrical information of packages and  dies introduces further complication. Technological shrink at  silicon level makes the die dimensions physically different  from drawn dimensions. Package designs typically use  physical dimensions only. Merge attempts between die and  package designs should not only scale the die to measured  dimensions, but also consider corresponding changes in  technology files.

Discrete components in SiP would need special libraries  for proper physical representations. Complexities of such  components can start from a simple resistor or capacitor to  another complete chip manufactured at a different foundry  with its own ESD protection.

Intra-package connections can be made in different  ways. RDL layers and interposer interconnects are closer to  die interconnects so they can be easier to model with standard  EDA tools, but bondwires can bring a third dimension to the  physical view, which is not represented in a typical chip  layout. However, wire length and corresponding impedance  has a big impact on ESD performance and cannot be ignored.  To properly model impedance it must be part of a universal  physical view.

The scale of the above problems is multiplied when ESD  and latch-up checks are considered for module/PCB levels.  There is no standard for a module/PCB view that have all the  physical details of the chips and packages on the board.

In the absence of a universal physical package view, we  tried to establish verification flow for running package-level  physical checks using hybrid package view (Fig. 9). Hybrid  package view takes information from a die physical view and  augments it with information from package schematic view.

We start with extracting the layout netlist from the die  (DE Netlist). This extraction creates DFMDB which contains  all other physical layer information not represented in the  netlist. DFMDB retains the relationship between the devices  and nets in DE Netlist and their spatial information in a GDS.  Then we follow the improved verification flow for running  package-level topological ESD checks to create a full  package netlist but use DE Netlist to represent the die netlist

at the stitching step (DP Netlist). When running LVS on  netlists representing DP Netlist vs. DE Netlist, LVS is correct  at die level and below. However, there is expected mismatch  at all other objects at package level. DP Netlist is processed  to find topologically objects of interest (OoI) which include  devices and nets. Found nets should carry true connectivity  established in the package. DFMDB cross-reference (X-REF)  process is used to find corresponding OoI in die layout from  the matching object in DE Netlist. With relationship between  DE Netlist and DP Netlist established, DFMDB can be  queried for the spatial information of the OoI. This  information is needed to apply geometrical ESD and latch-up  rules and to report found violations.

Following the flow, most checks ran successfully.  However, some results were inaccurate, and some real  violations were missed. For example, latch-up checks for  grounded n-wells did not produce correct results. This has  happened since some OoIs exist exclusively in DP Netlist and  are missing in DE Netlist. For example, VSS in Fig. 10 is the  net that has all the die ground pads (VSS1 and VSS2)  connected at the package level. ## IV.   C ONCLUSIONS   

## R EFERENCES   

In this paper we described new package-level ESD and  latch-up topological and physical verification flow. The  implementation of the flow accounts for different  connectivity changes due to different packaging options. It  overcomes limitations of conventional flows that requires a  package-level physical design database. This flow is  applicable for packages with complex die-to-package  connectivity, multi-die packages and systems in packages.  This flow is aimed to achieve more realistic package and  module-level latch-up and ESD (topological, current density,  point-to-point resistance, and netlist aware DRC) verification  results.

Partnership with EDA vendors is required to streamline  the interface between different EDA tools for die and package  co-design. ESD abstraction might be needed to represent  smaller components in larger contexts for ESD and latch-up  verification. This can benefit 3rd party IPs inside chips,  different dies inside a SiP, package inside a module.

The proposed package-level flow is not without technical  limitation. Chief among them is the absence of the packagelevel physical design database. Crafting a physical layout  view of the whole package including the dies will enable  running complete physical checks at the package level. This  enables the calculation of the package area for accurate  estimates of CDM current, the calculation of die-to-package  connection (bondwire) lengths based on the pin locations, and  consequently the associated impedance. This would result in  more realistic package-level ESD and latch-up verification  results.
