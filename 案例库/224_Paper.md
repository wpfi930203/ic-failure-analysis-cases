## Study of Charge Neutralization Techniques to Prevent  Copper Corrosion On Post-FIB Wet Stained Sample  

Sharon Lee, Li Li Hong, Khoo Ley Hong, Ang Poh Chuan, and Mo Zhi Qiang

GLOBALFOUNDRIES (S) Pte. Ltd.  60 Woodlands Industrial Park D, Street 2, Singapore 738406  Phone: +65 66701541  Fax: +65 63604370  Email:  sharon.lee@globalfoundries.com

Abstract -  This paper presents the study of various charge  neutralization techniques to prevent copper corrosion on post-FIB  wet stained sample. Conventional fast and economic wet staining  on post-FIB copper processed sample can sometimes cause  unwanted artifact such as copper corrosion due to build-up of  charge on non-grounded, long copper interconnect structure  during FIB milling. Some charge neutralization techniques such  as probe-pin grounding, conductive paint application and UV  treatment were investigated. The results revealed that these  techniques can prevent/reduce copper corrosion effect on the  samples and the most effective technique is recommended to  prevent this artifact.

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

Test structures on a wafer are often used for electrical testing  and   cross-section   profile   check   for   inline   recipe  characterization. Micro-cleaving can cause distortion to the  copper profile, hence FIB was chosen as the preferred method.  Staining or decoration would usually be necessary after FIB  milling in order to differentiate the various layers, particularly  the non-metallic thin films, such as nitride and oxide on a  post-FIB cross-sectioned sample. Current common methods of  delineation are chemical etching, plasma and reactive ion  etching and ion beam etching [1]. One conventional fast and  cheap method for staining is dipping into the Buffered Oxide  Etch (BOE). However, the copper profile of a post-FIB or  mechanically polished sample often gets dissolved/suffered  corrosion during wet staining, such as staining in dilute HF [2].

In this paper, an unusual phenomenon/artifact, repeated  alternating copper corrosion effect was observed after BOE  staining on the post-FIB copper metal-comb electrical test (ET)  structure. A SEM image of this copper corrosion effect is  shown in Figure 1. Such abnormal copper profile can cause  misinterpretation of result to the production line .  A hypothesis  about the suspected cause of this artifact was proposed after  analyzing the metal structure layout of the FIB sample under  the optical microscope .  Further investigation was initiated to  study the effect of each method in preventing the artifact.

To prove that the phenomenon of copper corrosion on  post-FIB sample after BOE stain was due to charge build up on  electrically “floating” long metal structure during FIB milling, some charge neutralization techniques were experimented to  prevent this artifact. The techniques tried included conductive

paint application, probe-pin grounding and ultraviolet (UV)  treatment.

## II.   EXPERIMENTS  

The FIB-target structure used in this study was a copper  metal-comb ET structure on a process completed wafer, used  for electrical testing or inline recipe characterization. Figure  2(a) shows the structure layout wired to some pads for electrical  testing. These pads and the structure are electrically “floating”,  which does not have connection to ground/not connected to the  substrate.

The ET structure was micro-cleaved across horizontally, as  shown in Figure 2(b), by a SELA 600 precision cleaving  system. A few samples were prepared this way with the SELA  600 cleaving system prior to FIB milling. Each sample was then  loaded into a Helios600 dual-beam FIB system to mill-in at the  dense copper lines of the structure, as shown in Figure 3(a).  During FIB milling, the accelerating voltage used was 30kV for  good lateral resolution and signal intensity. The ion beam  currents applied were 2.8nA and 0.46nA for rough and fine  milling respectively. A summary of the samples prepared is  shown in Table I.

All samples were sputter coated with a thin layer  of Pt in a BAL-TEC SCD005 sputter coater to prevent charging  during SEM imaging in the FEI Sirion SEM.

## III. R ESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

During FIB milling on each sample, certain repeated  consistently-spaced alternating copper lines were observed to  be especially “charged up”. An image taken during FIB milling  displaying this phenomenon is shown in Figure 4. After BOE  staining, these “charged-up” lines were observed to have been  “over-etched”, i.e. the copper corrosion effect. Analyzing the  ET structure from top-down optical inspection, it was observed  that those specific “charged up” lines were connected to a  common long metal line at the end of the structure as shown in  Figure 5. Those non-charged up lines are independent and  disconnected.  It was suspected that the long common metal

line, acting as a reservoir for charge accumulation, could have  been charged-up during FIB milling. When it is subjected to a  wet solution during staining, an electrochemical reaction has  taken place causing these “charged up” lines to be etched more  significantly. A self-bias is generated between the copper lines  and their environment when the sample is immersed in the  chemical solution, thus resulted a   $\Delta\Phi$  ,

during FIB milling, the copper corrosion did not take effect on  this sample. Electrochemical reaction could not take place as  the sample was not subjected to wet staining. Delineation was  not necessary for this sample, as the thicknesses of the SiN and  oxide layers were not important.

$$\Delta\Phi=\mathrm{I}\Delta\!\mathrm{R}=\mathrm{I}\uprho\;\frac{\Delta l}{A}$$
where I, ρ, l, and A are current enhanced by self-bias, electric  resistivity of copper, circuit length, and cross-sectional area [2].

## 2.    Conventional FIB-Stained Sample  

A common quick and inexpensive method to delineate a  post-FIB sample to distinguish the inter-nonconductive layers  such as SiN, oxide and silicon, is to dip the cross-section  portion into BOE solution. Figure 7 shows copper corrosion  effect on one of the “charged-up” copper lines. It is supposed  that these Cu lines, linked to a common longer metal area at the  end of the structure, were potentially “charged-up” during FIB  milling and these charges were stored in this long common  metal line. When the sample is   exposed to wet chemical,  electrochemical reaction takes place and these “charged-up”  lines are enhanced   with the copper corrosion effect.

## 3.    Conventional Cleaved-Stained Sample  

Cleaving can usually cause artifact, such as tear, dishing and  dielectric crack to a Cu metal profile. To convince that copper  corrosion takes place only when there is charge build-up in the  metal line, this cleaved sample with BOE staining was  experimented. No FIB milling was performed on this sample. A  SEM image was taken as shown in Figure 8. Due to the absence  of FIB milling causing the charge-up of the metal lines, the  corrosion effect was not observed on this sample.

## 4.    Probe-Pin Grounded-Stained Sample  

After cleaving the ET structure into half as shown in Fig.  3(a), the sample was placed on a probe system. A probe-pin

wired to ‘ground’ on the system was set to contact on Pad 4 of  the structure so that this can help to dissipate any charge that  could have been stored in the connected metal lines in the  structure during FIB milling. Subsequently, the sample was  dipped in BOE staining. SEM images were taken as shown in  Figure 9. Due to the effective charge neutralization with a  probe-pin prior to BOE stain, the copper corrosion effect was  not observed in this sample. This probe-pin grounding method  is effective in preventing copper corrosion on post-FIB wet  stained sample.

## 5.    Conductive Paint Applied-Stained Sample  

The other method tested for charge neutralization was done  by applying a brush of conductive paint, continuously linking  from Pad 4 and ending at the holder which the sample was  mounted on. This served as a manually drawn path for the  stored charges from the ET structure to be dissipated to the  grounded SEM stage through the sample holder. The silver  paint approved for SEM usage was applied. After the applied  paint has dried, the sample was subjected to BOE staining  before SEM images were taken as shown in Figure 10. Mild  copper corrosion effect was observed on those “charged-up”  metal lines after BOE stain. This method is capable to  neutralize charge that was build-up on the metal structure but  not completely efficient. Possible reasons could be due to the  conductivity of the Ag paint mixture and cleanliness of the  sample holder.

## 6.    UV Treated-Stained Sample  

For this experimented sample, the cross-sectioned plane of  the post-FIB sample was exposed to UV treatment before it was  immersed in BOE for staining. SEM images for this sample are  revealed in Figure 11. No copper corrosion effect was seen after  UV treatment and BOE stain. Photoionization has helped to  neutralize the stored charges during UV treatment [3].

## IV. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, a few charge neutralization techniques to  prevent copper corrosion on wet-stained post-FIB sample were  presented. From the results, we can draw the following  conclusion that all factors below must be present for copper  corrosion to take place on post-FIB wet stain sample:

1)    The metal structure needs to have build-up charges  (i.e. during FIB milling), especially on long metal  connection or big metal pad structure.     2)    Metal structure for FIB milling is not grounded.    3)    Structure must be subjected to BOE stain after FIB  mill.

If one of these factors is not present, copper corrosion will not  occur. We can conclude that the technique that uses UV  treatment is the most effective, efficient and cleanest (i.e. has  the lowest chance of contaminating the sample).
