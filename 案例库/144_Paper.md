## Irradiation Effects of 1 MeV Electron on Monolayer  MoS 2  Field Effect Transistors  

Yanqing Zhang 1 , Guoliang Ma 1 , Xuesong Zheng 2 , Zhengyong Hua 3 , Jiaming Zhou 4 , Heyi  $\mathrm{Li^{4}}$  , Chaoming Liu 4 , Yidan  Wei 4 , Tianqi Wang 1 , Chunhua   $\mathrm{{Q}i^{1*}}$  , Shangli Dong 1 , Mingxue Huo 2   1   Research Center of Basic Space Science, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, 150001, China.  2   China Aerospace Components Engineering Center, China Academy of Space Technology (CAST), Beijing 100029, China  3   No.58 Research Institute of China Electronics Technology Group Corporation (CETC), Wuxi 214035, China  4   School of Materials Science and Engineering, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, 150001, China.

\*  Email: qchunh@126.com

Abstract-   The effect of irradiation on monolayer MoS 2  FET (field  effect transistor) with 1 MeV electron beams was investigated.  Raman spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)  was measured before and after irradiation. The results show that  electron irradiation produces a strong desulfurization effect. The  electrical characteristics of the device were measured with fluence  condition of   $\mathbf{1.0\!\times\!10^{12}}$   and   $\mathbf{3.0\!\times\!10^{12}c m^{-2}}$  . The channel leakage  increases after irradiation while transfer and output current  decrease. This phenomenon can be attributed to the combination  of the states at the   $\mathbf{SiO}_{2}/\mathbf{MoS}_{2}$   interfaces and Coulomb scattering.  Our study will enhance the understanding of the influence of 1  MeV electron on MoS 2 -based nanoelectronics devices.

Keywords  – MoS 2 , field effect transistor, electron irradiation,  electrical characteristics

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

Due to their potential applications in atomic-film devices,  two-dimensional (2D) transition metal dichalcogenides (TMD)  have attracted a large amount of attention [1, 2]. Graphene, one  of the most popular 2D material recently, has been widely  studied but was difficult to use as a semiconductor because it  has no band gap [3]. Unlike graphene, TMD materials such as   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  ,   $\mathrm{WSe}_{2}$  , and  $\mathrm{MoSe}_{2}$   have a band gap and can be used as a  semiconductor material [4].   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  , as a representative material  among those TMDs, has attracted tremendous attention in  recent years due to its promising applications in electronic  transistors, optoelectronic devices and sensors [5-7].

Defects in  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  , such as vacancies, adatoms, etc., affect their  electrical, optical, and magnetic properties [8, 9]. For example,  ferromagnetism was observed in defective MoS 2  due to the  presence of single vacancies and vacancy clusters [8]. For  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$    FET devices, many experimental results indicate that defects in   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   can seriously affect their electrical performance.  Therefore, it is very important to find a means to regulate  defects. A lot of methods have been developed to generate  defects in   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  , such as thermal annealing, plasma treatment,  chemical functionalization, and charged particles irradiation.  Among these methods, charged particles irradiation can offer a  wide range of controllability over the defect type and density,  by controlling the irradiation parameters such as the type,  energy, and fluence of the incident ions.

In this work, the effect of electron irradiation on  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  FET  devices prepared with a monolayer   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   flake was investigated.  The device was irradiated with 1 MeV electron beams, which  can completely penetrate the FET device and produce uniform  defects. We demonstrated that the defects could be successfully  formed in   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   layer by 1 MeV electron irradiation, as  confirmed by Raman spectroscopies. As the defect density  increased, the channel leakage current increase while the  operating current decrease. The knowledge gained in this work  can be applied to other TMD FET devices, and therefore is  highly beneficial for the rational design of new functional  materials.

## II.   EXPERIMENTS  

The monolayer  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   was prepared by CVD (chemical vapor  deposition) method on the Si (600 μm)/SiO 2  (300 nm) substrate  produced by Sixcarbon Tech. Shenzhen. After the locations and  coordinates of the   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   flake were determined with an  electron-beam lithography system, source and drain electrodes  were made by depositing Au (70 nm)/Ti (5 nm) with a thermal  evaporation. The 1 MeV electron irradiation experiment was  performed in atmospheric environment in Technical Physics  Institute of Heilongjiang Academy of Science, China. The  fluence were   $1.0{\times}10^{12}$   and   $3.0{\times}10^{12}\mathrm{cm}^{2}$  , respectively. In order  to ensure the stability of the beam ， the flux was chosen to be   $1.5{\times}10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm}^{2}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$  . Fig. 1 shows the schematic illustration of  electron beam irradiation on a   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   FET device. In order to  determine the range and energy loss of the electrons, we  performed simulation calculations using casino software.  Energy loss distribution of 1 MeV electrons injected into  monolayer  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   was shown in Fig. 2. It could be seen that the  electrons completely penetrate the sample and no electron  deposition occurred in the sample. Raman spectroscopy system  (inVia-Reflex, Renishaw) and XPS (ESCALAB 250Xi) were  used to analyse the defect and structural properties of the   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  flakes before and after irradiation. The electrical characteristics  were measured by using a Keithley 4200 semiconductor  characterization system. The measurement of the   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   FET  under the dark condition was finished within one minute after  irradiation   to   reduce   self-annealing   effect. ## III. R ESULTS AND DISCUSSION   

The Using Raman spectroscopy, we demonstrate that the  defects could be introduced into the monolayer   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   model  system by electron irradiation [10-12]. Raman spectra before  and after irradiation were measured at room temperature in air  using a confocal microscope system with a green (532 nm) laser  [13, 14]. The normalized Raman spectra of the CVD   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$    flakes are shown in Fig. 3. Two Raman-active modes can be  observed near   $400~\mathrm{~cm^{-1}}$   corresponding to   $\mathrm{E}_{2\mathrm{g}}^{1}$    and   $\mathrm{A_{1g}}$   ,  respectively [15]. The two strong  $\mathrm{E}_{2\mathrm{g}}^{1}$    and  $\mathrm{A_{lg}}$   signal peaks are  due to in-plane and out-of-plane vibrations of  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   atoms. The  positions of the  $\mathrm{E}_{2\mathrm{g}}^{1}$    and   $\mathrm{A_{1g}}$   modes before irradiation are   ${\sim}387$     $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$   and  $404~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$  , respectively. After electron irradiation,   $\mathrm{E}_{2\mathrm{g}}^{1}$     shift toward a lower wavenumber (red shift) while  $\mathrm{A_{lg}}$   shift  toward a higher wavenumber (blue shift) [14]. Energetic  electron irradiation introduces some point defects in  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  , such  as Mo vacancies and S vacancies. Since the relative atomic  mass of S is much smaller than Mo, it is more likely to generate  S vacancies in   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  . In other words, electron irradiation  induces   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   desulfurization. Due to the absence of sulfur  atoms, compressive stresses are generated in the crystal lattice  [16]. This compressive stress causes the compressive strain in  MoS 2 , which could be responsible to the blue shift of   $\mathrm{A_{lg}}$   as  reported previously [16-18]. Electron irradiation can also cause  the dissociation of the Mo-S bond, which leads to fewer Mo-S  bond involved in the in-plane vibrations and a weaker restoring  force constant of the mode. This cause the redshift of the   $\mathrm{E}_{2\mathrm{g}}^{1}$     mode. However, due to the dissociation of Mo-S bond, more  Mo atoms are involved in the out-of-plane vibration,  strengthening the restoring force constant from the Mo-S bond  vibration, thereby enhanced the blue shift of the  $\mathrm{A_{1g}}$   mode [14,  19].

Fig. 4 shows the XPS spectra of the Mo 3d, S 2p and S 2s  core-level emissions for the monolayer   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   before (Fig. 4 a  and b) and after (Fig. 4 c and d) electron irradiation. Fitting of  the XPS spectra was done by a chi-square iteration program  using a convolution of Lorentzian-Gaussian functions with a  Shirley background. The peaks at   $161.3\;\mathrm{~eV~}$  and   $162.5\,\mathrm{\eV}$    observed in the pristine spectrum of S are identified as   $\mathrm{~S~}2\mathsf{p}_{3/2}$    and  $\mathrm{S}\,2\mathsf{p}_{1/2}$  . The peaks at   $228.4\,\mathrm{eV}$   and   $231.6\,\mathrm{eV}$   observed in the  pristine spectrum of Mo are identified as Mo   $3\mathrm{d}_{5/2}$  and   $3\mathrm{d}_{3/2}$  ,  while the small shoulder at   ${\sim}225.6~\mathrm{eV}$   in Fig. 4 b is the S 2s  peak. It can be seen from the fitting results that the peak shape  before and after irradiation did not change significantly, and no  new peaks were generated. This indicates that the irradiation did  not introduce new bonds. Then we extracted the S/Mo atomic  ratios of the samples before and after irradiation by fitting their  peaks in XPS spectra. The extraction result showed that the  S/Mo ratio before irradiation was 1.85, and it was reduced to  1.5 after irradiation. The XPS results indicated that the defects  in   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   after electron irradiation were mainly S vacancies, and  electrons irradiation have strong desulfurization, which is  consistent with the Raman results in the previous article.  Obviously, electron irradiation is a good means of  quantitatively modifying the S/Mo ratio in   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  .

Effect of electron irradiation on the electrical characteristics  of  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   FET device was measured in shading environment, the  results are shown in Fig. 5. The  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   FET device exhibited nchannel behaviour, with the current increasing as a more  positive gate voltage was applied. Fig. 5 a shows the channel  leakage current   $I_{D S^{-}}V_{D S}$   at   $V_{G}{=}0\mathrm{V}$  . As the irradiation fluence  increases, the channel leakage current increases. This indicates  that the electron irradiation induces a decrease in channel  quality and affects the switching characteristics of the FET. Fig.  5 b shows the output characteristics curve at   $V_{G}{=}20\mathrm{V}$   measured  for FET device before and after irradiation with the fluence of   $1{\times}10^{13}~\mathrm{e}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}$   and  $3{\times}10^{13}~\mathrm{e}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}$  . We can clearly observe that  the current of the device decreased with the irradiation fluence  increasing. This effect can also be seen clearly in the transfer  characteristic curve in Fig. 4 c; that is, the current decreased  significantly   following   electron   irradiation.   And   the  subthreshold slope of the device that was irradiated with  electron became worse after the irradiation. The degradation of  the subthreshold slope after irradiation is attributed to the  interface traps induced by electron.

We now discuss the electrical results of irradiation  experiments on   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   FET device. The electrical characteristic  of   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   FET can be affected by electron irradiation via a few  effects. First, the injected electrons increase the carrier density  of material. Second, the positive oxide-trapped charges and  interface traps. Third, the defects induced by electron irradiation  in   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  . The total thickness of the   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   FET device used  herein as mentioned above is approximately   $600~\upmu\mathrm{m}$  , which  includes highly doped silicon back gate electrodes. The total  range of 1 MeV electron is approximately 1.8 mm, as shown the  calculation result by casino in Fig. 2. This indicated that 1 MeV  electrons can completely penetrate the FET. Therefore, it can be  determined that electron irradiation does not increase the carrier  concentration in the material in the form of charge injection.  When high-energy charged particles pass through the FET  devices, they can ionize atoms and generate electron-hole pairs  in the  $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$   layer, which is most sensitive to ionizing irradiation.  This process is shown in Fig. 6. Since the mobility of electrons  is much higher than that of holes, the generated electrons are

swept out of the  $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$   layer in a few picoseconds. The remaining  holes are absorbed by the defects in   $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$  , forming a positive  electric field in the oxide layer. This positive electric field  attracts electrons in  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  , causing the concentration of carriers  (electrons) in   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   to rise, thereby increasing the channel  leakage current. In addition, the irradiation-induced holes can  transport to the   $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}/\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   interface, leading to the formation  of interface traps. These interface traps introduce a series of  defect levels in the band gap of MoS 2 , as shown in Fig. 7. These  defect levels capture electrons in the n-type   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  , thereby  reducing the carrier concentration of the FET in the active state.

As shown in the XPS results above, electron irradiation  induces many S vacancies. The irradiated   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   can adsorb  more negative charged ion, such as OH - , due to the existence of  S vacancies. Since the valence of S is -2, one S vacancy can  adsorb two OH - . The presence of these charged defects is  equivalent to the introduction of many Coulomb scattering  centers in  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  . The total carrier mobility is related to Coulomb  scattering and phonon scattering. Since the probability value of  phonon scattering is the only function of temperature, if the  temperature is constant, the phonon scattering probability is  constant. So, the total mobility is only related to Coulomb  scattering.   As the concentration of the scattering centre  increases, the carrier mobility decreases. Since the current  density is positively correlated with the carrier concentration  and mobility, the output and transfer characteristics of the FET  are degraded.

## IV. C ONCLUSION 

We successfully generated defects in monolayer   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   by  electron irradiation, and systematically investigated the effects  of defects on the properties of   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  by Raman and XPS  spectroscopies. Lattice defect of material induced the   $\mathrm{E}_{2\mathrm{g}}^{1}$    and   $\mathrm{A_{lg}}$   modes of Raman spectroscopy after electron irradiation are  red-shifted and blue-shifted, respectively. XPS is a good proof  of the desulfurization effect of electron beam on   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  . The

increase in channel leakage current after irradiation is attributed  to the oxide trapped positive charge. The   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   FET device  irradiated by electron exhibited decreases in the current. This  phenomenon can be attributed to combine of the interface traps  at the   $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}/\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   interfaces and Coulomb scattering. This study  improves our understanding of the influence of electron beams  on   $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$  -based nanoelectronics devices and may provide a  method to control the electrical properties of  $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$   FET devices  through regulating interface traps and S vacancies (Coulomb  scattering center) by electron beams.
