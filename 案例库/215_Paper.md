## Planar Staining Technique for Photoresist Related  Implantation Issue  

Li Hong LI, Sharon LEE, Jin YANG, Poh Chuan ANG, Zhi Qiang MO

Physical Failure Analysis QRA Department   GLOBALFOUNDRIES Singapore Pte. Ltd.  60 Woodlands Industrial Park D Street 2, Singapore 738406  Phone: (65) 66701526.   Fax: (65) 63604592.   Email:  lihong.li@globalfoundries.com Category: Sample Preparation, Metrology and Defect Characterization

Abstract- Ion   implantation process is a critical step in the  microfabrication of thin film transistors. Defects due to the  implantation process not only seriously degrade the performance  of the devices, but also are hardly detected via traditional electron  microscopic techniques alone, including scanning electron  microscopy (SEM), Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)  and focused ion beam (FIB), etc. Wet chemical stain technique,  such as junction stain, is an effective method to identify the defect  area because of the different etching rates caused by ion  implantation concentration. In this paper, a photoresist related  implantation issue was reported, and a planar staining technique  was developed to directly identify the defects from the suspected  failure area by combining the junction stain and SEM techniques.  More importantly, the planar staining technique for the  photoresist related implantation issues is convenient, time-saving,  and effective, as there is no need to prepare cross-section samples  for SEM observation.

Keywords  –  Junction stain, SEM, Planar Staining technique,  Thin film transistors .

## I. I NTRODUCTION 

Failure analysis (FA) study is of paramount importance to  reveal the root cause of the failure for thin film transistors, and  enhance the yielding rate of complementary metal-oxide  semiconductor (CMOS) devices in foundry industry. Electron  microscopy techniques including SEM, TEM, FIB, are primary  approaches in FA studies. However, their sample preparation  process is quite time consuming and complicated, especially for  cross-section observation. Ion implantation process is a critical  step in the microfabrication of thin film transistors. The defects  generated by the implantation process are difficult to be  recognized by the electron microscopy technologies alone.  Moreover, it is hard to determine where the cross-section plane  is located in primary dies.

Junction stain, as an effective wet chemical treatment in FA,  can etch the ion implantation area with different etching rates  due to the difference ion implantation concentration [1]-[2]. In  this study, we combined the benefits of Junction stain and SEM,  and develop a planar staining technique to directly identify the  defects under optical inspection without preparing samples for  cross-section observation. The research work in the study can  serve as a typical reference in foundry industry for FA study.

An issue of NPN bipolar transistor failure was reported  during the Electrical Testing (ET) due to much lower NPN Gain  value. Specifically, Gummel plot (Fig. 1a) illustrate the failed  sites still have typical transistor behaviors. However, IC-VC  curve of the collector for the bad sites display that the gain  value is as low as 4 (Fig. 1b-1), and almost 18 times lower than  those of good sites (typical value is larger than 70, Fig. 1b-2).  ET benching data revealed the gain value and distribution of the  bad sites which primarily are located at the center of the wafer  (Fig. 1c). With the reverse bias, Fig.1d also shows bad sites  have higher E (Emitter) B (Base) junction leakage and lower  breakdown voltage.

Second, the abnormal Boron base implant was suspected to  occur.  SIMS (secondary ion mass spectrometry) analysis is at   $80\mathrm{x}300\mathrm{um}$   large area which is the extrinsic base area. But the  result showed no profile difference between good and bad  sample for Boron base implant, and the dosage detected was  within   $5\%$  SIMS variation. As at this large extrinsic open  region, the result only can prove normal implantation process  for the extrinsic base, instead of abnormal intrinsic ion  implantation.

Finally, cross-section junction stain was performed at  affected ET structure, which showed the extrinsic base shorted  into the intrinsic base region. This hypothesis was the  photoresist lifting at the intrinsic region during the extrinsic  base implant process. Further investigation in the prime die was  done at random power NPN location by same FA method,  however there was no defect observed.

For this case, due to the urgency and it is very time  consuming for Electrical Failure Analysis (EFA), additionally  EFA may not easily determine where the failure spot was in  prime die with some power NPN design, and therefore there is  no the information to guide the sample preparation for  cross-section observation.

In order to check the severity of the affected area and hence  screen out the bad region, we developed a novel planar stain  technique to accurately and effectively identify the failed area,  and provide direct evidence to guide the microfabrication  process.

## II.   EXPERIMENTS  

To verify the hypothesis, cross-section junction stain was  performed for the ET structure, and samples were observed  under SEM. First, the dies were dipped into a HF solution for  10-15 minutes, and then soaked in a mixture acid solution to  stain the junction for 3 minutes. Afterwards, optical inspection  and SEM were conducted for the cross-section morphology.  Meanwhile, the same failure analysis procedure was done for  the prime dies to check the failure issue.

As random cross-section junction staining did not work for  the primary die at power NPN location, we developed a new top  down planar junction staining method to directly etch the dies.  Briefly, after the delayer step in HF solution, the planar surface  staining was carried out for primary die chip in a mixture of   ${\mathrm{HN}}{\mathrm{O}}_{3}$  ,  $\mathrm{CH}_{3}\mathrm{COOH}$   and HF solution[3], followed by the optical  inspection and SEM observation.

First, based on ET benching data analysis, higher EB junction  leakage and low gain value indicated the thin film transistor  failure. The root cause was suspected to come from Emitter and  Base interface. Hence, TEM cut was carried out to check the  thickness of the interfacial oxide between poly silicon and  Epitaxial (EPI) silicon. However, the interfacial oxide (1.6nm)  between Poly and EPI was measured and EDX/EELS Line scan  done, there was no physical difference between bad and good  dies.

After the planar junction staining for good and bad dies, good  dies show the regular and alternative intrinsic and extrinsic  structures (Fig. 6a, zoom in view). As lower ion implantation  does in the intrinsic area resulted in lower etching rate, the  extrinsic area forms a deeper trench (Fig. 6b, tilt SEM). On the  contrary, as the photoresist was delaminated from the intrinsic  area during implantation process, the intrinsic area has similar  ion implantation does to the extrinsic area on bad dies.  Accordingly, in some areas intrinsic and extrinsic bases were  merged together after the planar junction staining (Fig. 7a & b).  The cross-section view obviously shows that some intrinsic  structures (the rive-bank-like structure) were etched away (Fig.  7c, cross-section view). Hence, the planar junction stain  provided direct evidence for the root cause of the thin film  transistor failure.

## IV. C ONCLUSION 

Cross-section junction stain of primary dies did not work  properly for the case of random photoresist lifting during the  ion implantation process, while the planar junction staining  method proposed by us is convenient, time-saving, and  cost-effective to provide direct evidence for the root cause of  the device failure. The combination of traditional electronic  microscopy methodology and the novel wet planar junction  staining approach in FA study enables us to prove the  hypothesis and effectively solve the issue occurred in the  microfabrication process, and improve the productivity.
