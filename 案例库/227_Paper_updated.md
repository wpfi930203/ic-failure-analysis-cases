## Optical Power Degradation Related to Turn-On in  Commercial 265 nm UV-C LEDs  

Francesco Piva 1 , Matteo Buffolo 1 , Marco Pilati 1 , Nicola Roccato 1 , Marco Garato 1 , Carlo De Santi 1 , Nicola Trivellin 1,2 , Gaudenzio  Meneghesso 1 , Enrico Zanoni 1 , Matteo Meneghini 1,3  1 Department of Information Engineering, University of Padova, via Gradenigo 6B, 35131, Padova, Italy  2 Department of Industrial Engineering, University of Padova, via Gradenigo 6A, 35131, Padova, Italy  3 Department of Physics and Astronomy, University of Padova, via Marzolo 8, 35131, Padova, Italy  Phone : (+39)0498277928, Email:  francesco.piva@unipd.it

Abstract —In this work, we investigated the optical power  drops present after the turn-on of high-power commercial UVC LEDs. We demonstrated that degradation occurs in  correspondence with the turn-on of the device. By slowing the  turn-on transient,   ${\pmb{5.8}~\mathrm{mA/s}}$   rate,  we demonstrated that even a  gradual increase in the current could not prevent the  phenomenon, indicating that it is not correlated to the sudden  change in temperature and electric field. From thermal  characterization and voltage transients, we extrapolated a high  thermal coefficient for this UV LED with respect to the visible  counterpart. Moreover, from a visual inspection, we observed a  degradation of the encapsulation, which could be caused by both  the high temperatures reached and by the energy of the emitted  photons.

Keywords—Commercial UV LEDs, Optical Power Drops,  Voltage Transients, Disinfection, Sars-CoV-2

## I.    I NTRODUCTION 

After the Sars-CoV-2 pandemic in 2020, ultraviolet (UV)  light-emitting diodes (LEDs) were widely used in the design  and development of disinfection systems [1, 2], since it was  demonstrated that the UV light in the wavelength range of  255 – 295 nm could inactivate several viruses [3]. With  respect to the visible counterpart, UV LEDs suffer from  several problems, such as an external quantum efficiency  lower than   $10\%$   [4], a bad Schottky p-contact since no metal  has a good alignment with p-(Al)GaN [5], self-heating [4]  and low spectral purity [6].

The goal of our work is to study the optical characteristics  of commercial UV-C LEDs, with a nominal emission  wavelength of   $265~\mathrm{~nm}$   and a nominal optical power of   $100\:\mathrm{mW}$  , as commonly used in disinfection systems. With  our accelerating lifetime tests (ALTs), we identified sudden  drops in the optical power during the turn-on phase of the  device, which we ascribed to possible degradation of the LED  encapsulation. We also demonstrated that a slow LED turnon cannot reduce these optical power drops, indicating their  possible correlation with the high-energy emitted optical  power, besides the high junction temperature. Moreover, by  combining thermal measurements and voltage transient  measurements, we estimated a temperature coefficient for  UV LEDs ten times higher than visible LEDs.

## II.    R ESULTS   

We started investigating the electrical and optical  characteristics of the device after an ALT at the absolute  maximum current of   $350~\mathrm{mA}$  , obtaining an optical power  (OP) decrease of   $20\%$   after 20000 min (about 330 h). We

decided to double the stress current to accelerate the observed  degradation mechanism, and the results showed a final OP  decrease of about  $50\%$  . Both stresses were performed at room  baseplate temperature   $\mathrm{\DeltaT=25~{}^{\circ}C}$  ), which was maintained  through a temperature controller and a Peltier cell. As  reported in Figure 1, some fast drops of the OP were present  in both stress tests, and they were more evident at higher  stress currents. During an ageing procedure, decreases in  drive voltage and optical power over the first seconds, or  tenths of, are typically correlated to the self-heating of the  device. Drive voltage variations are associated with the  decrease in the bandgap for Varshni’s Law, whereas the  changes in optical power are correlated with an increase in  carrier escape and overflow from QWs region. In this case  study, OP drops could not be correlated with these  mechanisms, since the optical power trend between  subsequent stress steps was found to be strongly  discontinuous. This suggested the presence of an additional  degradation process, that will be investigated in this work.

Regarding the electrical characteristics shown in Figure  2(a), we could observe an increase in the subthreshold  leakage current, mainly associated with an increase in the  shunt resistance component. This increase could be ascribed  to the increase in percolation path in the structure [7], [8], or  to the formation of surface hillocks, as reported in [9]. Figures  2(b) and (c) present the variation of the optical power during  the ageing procedure. It is worth noticing that the decrease in  OP at high current levels was higher for L2, and this  difference could be ascribed to both the higher stress current

and the higher temperature reached during operation at higher  bias, and therefore self-heating levels. Moreover, the sudden  optical power drops occurring at the turn-on are much more  noticeable, which implies a higher impact on the total  degradation. Figure 2(c) also reports a higher decrease in OP  at low current levels, which can be typically ascribed to the  increase in non-radiative recombination mechanisms,  associated with the increase in defectiveness of the active  region [10, 11].

In this work, we focused our investigation on the origin  of OP drops. We planned all following ALTs at   $700~\mathrm{mA}$  ,  which, among  the tested currents, showed the highest  number of sudden drops, without inducing extrinsic  degradation processes. In the first test, we wanted to study the  effect of the turn-ons after the characterizations on the LEDs,  therefore we performed two stresses where one sample was  stopped at logarithmic time steps, and the other was kept on  continuously. In particular, sample L3 was stressed in the  same conditions as L2, stopping the ageing to mimic the  procedure of a complete characterization; instead sample L4  was stressed continuously, without interrupting the test.  Characterizations were carried out only before and after the  ageing for both LEDs; the optical power trends during the  ageing procedure are shown in Figure 3.

From these tests, we confirmed that these drops appeared  only during the turn-on of the LEDs, and we can recognize  them from a higher amplitude of the OP transient in the first  seconds of stress. As above mentioned, these drops are  associated with the self-heating phase of the UV LEDs,  therefore we planned a test to correlate the drops and the selfheating, to understand if a slower increase in temperature  could prevent the drops. We developed a program for our  instrumentation that slowly turns-on and turns-off the LED,  to reduce the self-heating time constant. After preliminary  tests, we decided to set the turn-on and turn-off time at 120 s,  higher than the usual self-heating transient (Figure 5), and we  planned two more ALTs. In the first test, we turned-on the  LED instantaneously at the stress current (Fast Start), and in  the second we turned it on slowly (Slow Start/Stop), with a  ramp of  $\cdot\pm5.8\,\mathrm{mA/s}$  . To focus our analysis only on the sudden  drops, rather than on the optical power degradation, we  increased the number of turn-ons and -offs and we fixed the  stress time for each step at 20 minutes. We obtained the  results in Figure 4, where we still detected in both tests the  optical power drops. This excluded self-heating as the only  cause of the drops, and it prompted us to carry out further  analysis to understand the phenomena.

To better understand the role of self-heating on the optical  power drops, we analyzed the temperature transient during  both the fast and the slow turn-on. To do this, we placed the

device on a temperature-controlled baseplate fixed at   $25~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  ,  and we replicated the same setup of the stress tests. We  mounted above it a thermal camera FLIR SC300, and we  performed the same type of transients used in the ALTs of L5  and L6. The results of the Fast Start Transient and the Slow  Start Transient are reported in Figure 5. In both cases, the  devices reached the same temperature of   $70\ \mathrm{~}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$   without  showing any temperature overshoot. Moreover, we can  appreciate that the self-heating transient of the Fast Start ran  out in about 50 seconds, instead the self-heating transient of  the Slow Start perfectly followed the increasing current.

Since the optical power drops seem to not be correlated  with chip failures, we proceeded with a visual inspection of  the devices. As reported in Figure 6, we found important  changes in the encapsulation of devices with different levels  of ageing. After an identical and meticulous cleaning  procedure, unaged devices showed a clean encapsulation  with a very low number of scratches. Instead, the aged  devices presented a higher amount of scratches and spots on  and inside the encapsulation, possibly correlated with OP  drops during the ALTs. Recent reviews on the encapsulation  materials used for UV LEDs [12], [13] reported two possible  explanations: (i) cracks in silicon resins due to undesired  polymerization, and (ii) chemical dissociations in fluorine  resins (p-BVE). For these reasons, we hypothesize that these  damages in the encapsulation could be caused by a chemical  reaction induced by the high-energy high-density deep UV  light, and supported by the high  ߂ܶ  induced at each LED  turn-on.

To better show the effect of self-heating on the  performance UV-C LEDs, we measured the voltage transient  during the fast turn-on at the absolute maximum current and  at its double. With our self-developed instrumentation [14],  we were able to measure the transient from about   $4\ \upmu\mathrm{s},$  ,  showing that after this short time the LED junction is already  heating up. In fact, as depicted in Figure 7, the drive voltage  of the LED was already decreasing after the first  measurement point, whereas it reached a thermal steady state  condition after about 50 s, as already shown in Figure 5. This  demonstrates the importance of performing short-pulsed

measurements on UV LEDs, to reduce as much as possible  the self-heating of the device that modifies its electro-optical  characteristics. Moreover, by combining this voltage  transient with the thermal camera measurement, we could  extract the temperature coefficient of this LED, which is  about  $42\,\mathrm{mV/K}$  . This value is ten times higher than the visible  LED [15], indicating a higher voltage drop for the same  junction temperature increase.

## III.    C ONCLUSIONS   

In conclusion, we carried out an important investigation  on the sudden and non-recoverable optical power drops  observed at the LED turn-on. We demonstrated that also a  slow LED turn-on cannot reduce this phenomenon, and we  correlated its origin to a degradation of the encapsulating  material. In particular, it could be due both to the high  temperature reached during the ageing and to the higher flux

of high-energy photons emitted. Finally, we calculated the  thermal coefficient of the LED, showing that it is ten times  the one of the visible LED.    [11]

Further analyses may focus on: (i) ALTs with a visual  inspection to trace the encapsulation degradation, (ii) ALTs  to induce the degradation of the encapsulation with an  external UV light source, and (iii) studying the correlation  between the electrical and the optical kinetics during the self- [12]  heating transient at the turn-on.
