## Effect of Primary Knocked-on Atoms on  Conductivity Compensation in N-type 4H-SiC  Irradiated by 1 MeV Electrons, 25 MeV C Ions and  40 MeV Si Ions  

Guoliang Ma 1 , Yanqing Zhang 1 , Heyi Li 2 , Chaoming Liu 2 , Chunhua Qi 1 , Yidan Wei 2 , Tianqi Wang 1\* , Shangli Dong 2 ,  Mingxue Huo 1   1   Research Center of Basic Space Science, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, 150001, China.   2   School of Materials Science and Engineering, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, 150001, China.  \*  Email: wangtq@hit.edu.cn

Abstract-   The conductivity recombination mechanism of different  ions irradiation was investigated in n-type 4H-SiC Schottky diode.  The incident ions were selected as 1 MeV electrons, 25 MeV C and  40 MeV Si ions, respectively. The primary knocked-on atoms  (PKAs) distribution in the irradiated 4H-SiC is calculated by  SRIM code. After irradiation, the carrier concentration changed  significantly. Compared with virous particles irradiation, it is  indicated that the carrier removal rates under these three  irradiation conditions are greatly different. Based on the  theoretical analysis, different conductivity compensation brought  by different defect status is the major reason for the significant  difference in carrier removal rate.

Keywords  – 4H-SiC, ,conductivity compensation, irradiation  defects, PKA

## I. I NTRODUCTION   

As a wide bandgap semiconductor, silicon carbide (SiC)  could be effectively used in the high frequency, high power, and  high temperature devices [1-3], due to its excellent properties,  such as high thermal conductivity, high electron saturation drift  velocity, high electron mobility, etc. [4, 5]. Moreover, SiC is  also a radiation resistant semiconductor material, allowing the  SiC devices to be used in a harsh radiation environment [6, 7].  For the sake of studying the irradiation effects of the SiC, many  types of particles were chosen as radiation source, such as  electrons [8, 9], protons [10], and alpha particles [11].  Irradiation defects (such as  $Z_{1/2}$  , EH 6/7 ) will impact the electrical  properties of SiC, especially carrier concentration. In the n-type  SiC, radiation induced-defects (RDs) mainly cause conductivity  compensation. As mentioned in references [12, 13], the  researchers found that the RDs (  $\mathrm{Z}_{1/2}$   and   $\mathrm{EH}_{6/7}$  ) caused by low  energy   electron   irradiation   can   lead   to   conductivity  recombination. And these RDs are associated with Frenkel-Pair  (FP) in the carbon sublattice of the n-type SiC. Therefore,  carbon vacancies are considered to be the main “killer” of  carrier concentration in n-type SiC. Carrier removal rate  induced by heavy ions irradiation is much greater than that of  electron and protons. The current theory of this phenomenon is  not consummate. The general explanation is that heavy ions  produce more displacement damage in SiC due to greater  momentum, leading to more conductivity recombination.

Theoretical calculation is a good way to explain this  phenomenon, but the calculation of conductivity compensation  is mainly related to proton and electron irradiation [14, 15].  Until now, there is almost no clear conclusion about the effect  of the heavy ions irradiation on conductivity compensation.

In this paper, the irradiation effect on the conductivity  compensation in the 4H-SiC Schottky diodes caused by various  particles are investigated. This result could provide guidance for  the potential applications of SiC devices in space.

## II.   EXPERIMENTS  

## A.   Device Under Test  

The nitrogen doped epilayer 4H-SiC samples with a doping  density of  $\bar{5^{\times}10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}}$   were used in this study. The back side of  the sample had a high doping density of   $1{\times}10^{19}~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$   for the  fabrication of ohmic contacts. The wafer was cut into the size  of  $2\:\mathrm{mm}\times2\:\mathrm{mm}$  . Aluminum was chosen as the anode metal with  a thickness of   $4\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$   and a size of   $1.7\:\mathrm{mm}{\times}1.7\:\mathrm{mm}$  . The cathode  metal was  $4\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$  thick aluminum, and a layer of silver was plated  on the outside of the aluminum electrode. Fig.1 shows the  schematic cross-section of the DUT.

## B.   Irradiation Experiment  

The   $40\ \mathrm{~MeV}$  Si and 25 MeV C irradiation tests were  performed using the EN Tandem Accelerator in the State Key  Laboratory of Nuclear Physics and Technology, Peking  University, China. For 40 MeV Si, the irradiation fluences were  selected as   $1.0{\times}10^{9}\mathrm{cm}^{2}$  ,   $8.9{\times}10^{9}\mathrm{cm}^{2}$   and    $1.5{\times}10^{10}\mathrm{cm}^{2},$  ,  respectively. For 25 MeV C, the irradiation fluences were

selected as   $6.4{\times}10^{10}\mathrm{cm}^{2}$  ,   $1.06{\times}10^{11}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$   and   $1.7{\times}10^{11}\mathrm{cm}^{2}$  ,  respectively. For both ions, the flux is   $1.5{\times}10^{7}\,\mathrm{cm}^{2}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$  .

The electron irradiation experiment was performed in  vacuum using an accelerator in Technical Physics Institute of  Heilongjiang Academy of Science, China. The fluence were   $1.0{\times}10^{14}\mathrm{cm}^{2}$  ,   $1.0{\times}10^{15}\mathrm{cm}^{2}$   and  $2.0{\times}10^{16}\mathrm{cm}^{2}$  , respectively. The  flux was  $5{\times}10^{11}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$  .

## C.   Electrical Characteristics Test  

The electrical characteristics were measured in situ during  irradiation by using a Keithley 4200-SCS semiconductor  characterization system. The measurement of the Schottky  diode under the dark condition was finished within ten second  after irradiation to reduce self-annealing effect. The irradiation  system and the test system were connected and grounded. For  the sake of preventing electromagnetic interference, aluminium  foil was affixed to the outside of the irradiation chamber for  electromagnetic shielding. The  C-V  test used a scan voltage of  -2 to 0 V with a scanning step of 0.02 V and a frequency of 1  MHz.

III. R ESULTS AND DISCUSSION

## A.   Electrical characteristics 

Fig.2(a-c) shows the semi-logarithmic plot of   I-V   characteristics measured at room temperature, for the 4H-SiC  Schottky diodes before and after the irradiation of 40 MeV Si  ions, 25 MeV C ions and 1 MeV electron, respectively. In  general, the effect of irradiation on SiC Schottky diode can be  qualified by the ideality factor ( n ) and Schottky barrier height   $(\varPhi_{b})$  . In order to obtain these two parameters, the deformation  formula of the thermal electron emission model is used for

calculation, and the specific forms are shown in equation (1)  and equation (2)[16]:

$$\begin{array}{r}{n=\frac{q}{k_{B}T}\times\frac{d V}{d\ln I}}\end{array}$$
(1)

$$\begin{array}{r}{\phi_{b}=\frac{k_{B}T}{q}\times\ln\Big(\frac{A A^{*}T^{2}}{I_{s}}\Big)}\end{array}$$
(2)

Where  $q$   is the electron charge;  $k_{B}$   is the Boltzmann constant;   $T$   is the absolute temperature;   $V$   is the voltage;   $I$   is the current;   $A$   is the area of the Schottky contact;   $A^{*}$   is the Richardson  constant, in theory for 4H-SiC is   $146\mathrm{A}\;\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$  ;  $I_{s}$   is the saturation  current.

From above equations, ideality factor and Schottky barrier  height can be obtained, as shown in Table I. It can be seen that  the ideality factor of all three samples increase with the  increasing of the fluence. According to the thermal electron  emission model, the increase of ideality factor indicated that the  thermal electron emission in the low-voltage region is not the  only dominant conduction mechanism. Other conductive  mechanisms, such as the generation-recombination mechanism,  can also play an important role. This can be attribute to the point  defects introduced by the incident ions, which acts as  recombination centres during the conduction process, leading to  the increasement of the recombination current and the  increasement of the ideality factor. For Schottky diodes, the  Schottky barrier height is related to the density of interface  state. The irradiation can produce a higher density of interface  state, and then lower the Schottky barrier height. This is  consistent with the results obtained in our experiments.

## B.   Theoretical analysis 

In order to study the effect of different energy particles on  conductivity compensation, the FP generation rate  η FP  need to  be calculated. Owing to the fact that carbon vacancies are  considered to be the main “killer” of carrier concentration in ntype SiC. We only need to calculate the  η FP  for carbon atoms.  According to the equation as follow [19]:

$$\eta_{F P}=N_{c}\sigma_{d}\upsilon$$
(4)

where   $N_{C}$   is concentration of carbon atoms consider to be   $4.8{\times}10^{22}\mathrm{cm}^{2}$  ,   $\sigma_{d}$   is the cross section and   $\upnu$   is the multiplication  factors. It’s obviously that we need to get the values of   $\sigma_{d}$   and  ν .

For 1 MeV electron, the cross section can be estimated by the  equation [19]:

$$\begin{array}{r}{\sigma_{d}=\frac{8\pi a_{0}^{2}Z_{2}^{2}E_{R}^{2}}{M_{2}c^{2}E_{d}}}\end{array}$$
(5)

where    $a_{0} $   is the Bohr radius of hydrogen (    $a_{0}=$   $\hbar^{2}/m e^{2}=5.29\times10^{-9}\:c m)$  ),   $E_{R}$   is the Rydberg energy (13.60  eV),  $Z_{2}$   is the atomic number of target atom,   $M_{2}$   is the mass of  atomic atom,   $c$   is the speed of light and   $E_{d}$  is the threshold  energy of atomic displacement. For SiC, the values of 18eV was  taken for carbon sublattice. At this   $E_{d}$   the cross-section of RD  formation, given by equation (5), is about 24 barns for C  sublattice.

The generation of FP include two processes. First, target  atoms leave their position as a result of a direct interaction with  an incident particle, named primary knocked-on atoms (PKAs),  and the PKAs gain kinetic energy in this process. Then an  energetic PKA can itself knock out a lattice atom: the  multiplication factor ν represent the average number of  displaced atoms per PKA grows, and a cascade of  displacements is formed. In order to get the value of  multiplication factor ν, the average energy   ${<}\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{PKL}}\mathrm{>}$   need to be  estimated. The average energy received by PKA in those  Rutherford collisions which displace atoms,   ${<}\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{PMA}}{>}$  , is an  important quantity and is easily calculated as [19]:

$$\begin{array}{r}{\langle E_{P K A}\rangle=\frac{\int_{T=E_{d}}^{T=T_{m}}T d\sigma}{\int_{T=E_{d}}^{T=T_{m}}d\sigma}=\binom{E_{d}T_{m}}{T_{m}-E_{d}}\ln\binom{T_{m}}{E_{d}}}\end{array}$$
(6)

where  $T_{m}$   is the maximum energy transferred to a semiconductor  atom by a relativistic electron with mass m and energy   $E$   [19]:

$$\begin{array}{r}{T_{m}={\frac{2\left(E+2m c^{2}\right)}{M_{2}c^{2}}}E}\end{array}$$
From the equation (6) and equation (7),   ${<}\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{PMA}}{>}$   and   $T_{m}$   are   $356\mathrm{eV}$   and 56eV, respectively. Then the multiplication factor   $\nu$  can be estimated by the Kinchin-Pease formula modified by  Ziegler [20]:

$$\begin{array}{r}{\upsilon=\frac{\left\langle E_{P K A}\right\rangle}{2.5E_{d}}}\end{array}$$
(8)

Based on the above discussion, the multiplication factor  $\nu$   and  the FP generation rate   $\eta_{F P}$   can be calculated by equation (4), (6),  (7), (8). The calculation results of the relevant parameters are  shown in Table II.

Calculation of scattering cross section is more complicated  for   $25\ensuremath{\mathrm{~\MeV~C~}}$   and 40 MeV Si. In Rutherford collisions, the  cross section of charged particles can be estimated [19]:

$$\begin{array}{r}{\sigma_{d}=16\pi{a_{0}}^{2}Z_{1}^{2}Z_{2}^{2}\frac{M_{1}^{2}}{(M_{1}+M_{2})^{2}}\frac{E_{R}^{2}}{T_{m}^{2}}({T_{m}\mathord{\left/{\vphantom{T_{m}{E_{d}}}}\right.\kern-\nulldelimiterspace}E_{d}}-1)}\end{array}$$
(9)

The cross sections of 25 MeV C and 40 MeV Si are  $1.83\!\times\!10^{5}$  and   $1.49\!\times\!10^{6}$  , respectively. It can be seen that the scattering  cross section of 40 MeV Si is one order of magnitude larger than  25 MeV C, which indicates that 40 MeV Si is more likely to  scatter with the carbon sublattice.

The maximum energy transferred to a semiconductor atom by  a charged particle is quite different from a relativistic electron.  From energy and momentum conservation it is easily seen that  [19]:

$$T_{m}=[4M_{1}M_{2}/(M_{1}+M_{2})^{2}]E$$
Based on the equation (4), (6), (8), (10), the multiplication  factor ν and the FP generation rate η FP  can be calculated. The  calculation results of the relevant parameters are shown in Table  II.

However, it can be clearly seen that the carrier removal rate  is significantly less than the FP generation rate. This indicated  that not all generated FPs are steady-state, and some FPs  recombine after they are generated. In order to match the  experimental results with the theoretical results, the quality of  RDs need to be considered, specifically, the average distance  between the components of the FPs being formed. The fraction   $f_{F P}$   is introduced to characterize dissociated FPs percentage of  total FPs:

$$f_{F P}=\eta/\eta_{F P}$$
By the equation (11), the for  $1\;\mathrm{MeV}$   electron, 25 MeV C and  40 MeV Si are   $13\%$  ,  $30\%$   and  $60\%$  , respectively. Assuming that  hot PKAs (energetic PKAs, in the right hand of PKA spectrum)  produce dissociated FPs, according to the inverse-square law  we can calculate the boundary energy   $\mathrm{E_{ch}}$   that produces  dissociated FPs:

$$f_{F P}=(1/E_{c h}-1/T_{m})/(1/E_{d}-1/T_{m})$$
Base on the equation (12), V.V. Kozlovski proved that  $122\mathrm{eV}$  is the characteristic energy [15] (when carbon atoms receive  energy of   $122\ \ \mathrm{eV}$  , they will leave the spontaneousrecombination area to form stable RDs leading to the  conductivity compensation in n-SiC.) of C atoms in SiC. The  recombination radius is   ${\sim}8\,\mathrm{~\AA~}$   corresponding to four a 0  (the  distance between the nearest carbon atoms) [15].

According to the equation (12), the  $f_{F P}$   of 1 MeV electron, 25  MeV C and 40 MeV Si are   $11\%$  ,   $14\%$   and   $15\%$   respectively.  The result of 1MeV is very close to the experimental value  $13\%$  and the results of  $25\ \mathrm{MeV}\ \mathrm{C}$   and 40 MeV Si are quite different  from the experimental values   $30\%$   and   $60\%$  . Since equation  (10) is based on the statistical rule for the estimation of single  vacancies, high-energy heavy ions may produce multiple

vacancy defects. In this case, the PKA energy spectrum needs  to be considered.

Fig. 4 and Fig. 5 show the histogram of energies received by  PKA atoms for 25 MeV C and 40 MeV Si calculated by SRIM.  Here we divide PKAs into soft PKAs and hot PKAs with a  border of   $122\mathrm{eV}$  . For both   $25~\mathrm{MeV}$   and 40 MeV Si,  ${\sim}80\%$   of  total PKAs are soft PKAs with the average energy of   ${\sim}52~\mathrm{eV}$  .  These soft PKAs can’t generate dissociated FPs, so that they  won’t contribute to conductivity compensation. The average  energies of hot PKAs for  $25\;\mathrm{MeV}$   and 40 MeV Si are  ${\sim}661~\mathrm{eV}$    and   ${\sim}1136~\mathrm{eV}$  , respectively. These hot PKAs are only   ${\sim}20\%$    percent of total PKAs, but a C atom with   ${\sim}661~\mathrm{eV}$   and   $\mathord{\sim}1136$  eV create in a cascade approximately 14 and 25 vacancies,  respectively. Under these conditions, a cascade yields  microscopic regions with wide of 20–50 Å 15], with up to ten  displacements occurring in these microscopic regions.  However, the high concentration of vacancies in these regions  lead to formation of divacancies, multi-vacancies and  complexes of these vacancies with impurity atoms rather than  single vacancy. This is consistent with the DLTS results in  chapter B. Therefore, the main reason for conductivity  recombination for energetic heavy ions irradiation is  divacancies, multi-vacancies, and more complex defect  complexes.

## IV. C ONCLUSION 

The conductivity recombination mechanism of 1 MeV  electron,   $25\mathrm{~MeV~C~}$   ions and 40 MeV Si ions irradiation was  investigated in n-type 4H-SiC. The carrier removal rates of 1

MeV, 25 MeV C and 40 MeV Si were   $0.19\;\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$  ,   $1.5\!\times\!10^{4}$   cm - 1  and  $2.4\!\times\!10^{5}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$  , respectively. FP generation rate calculated  from PKAs were   $1.4~\mathrm{{cm}^{-1}}$  ,   $5\times10^{4}~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$   and   $4\times10^{5}~\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$  .  Through the inverse-square law, we found that single vacancy  is the main reason for conductivity compensation in 1 MeV  electron irradiation. From the PKA spectrum of 25 MeV C and  40 MeV Si irradiation, we found that minority of hot PKAs  (about   $20\%$  ) can yield a large amount of vacancies in these  microscopic regions. These divacancies, multi-vacancies, and  more complex defect complexes were main reason for  conductivity compensation in 25 MeV C and 40 MeV Si  irradiation. Based on the above discussion, the huge carrier  removal rate was due to different conductivity compensation  mechanism brought by different defect status.
